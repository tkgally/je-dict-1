Decision models for je-dict-1 判定モデル

Eighteen “decision models” appeared on OpenRouter in the past few weeks. They return a typed yes/no or multiple-choice answer with a probability instead of generated text, and they cost almost nothing per call. Could they take over any of the dictionary’s checking work? I tested all sixteen that can be called on 1,357 items drawn from seven real dictionary tasks, and compared them with four LLMs.

SummaryThe short answer

  1. All sixteen have enough Japanese to be usable. Every one matched Japanese sentences to their translations and picked the right meaning of common words perfectly in the screening test. They differ on finer points: readings, homophones, conjugation.
  2. Two tasks can move to a decision model now: checking that an example’s English translation matches the Japanese, and checking which sense of a headword an example illustrates. On these the best decision models score within one or two points of the LLMs (translation 98.4% vs. 99.5%; sense 96.2% vs. 96.9%) at a tenth to a hundredth of the cost. A full pass over all 122,570 example translations would cost about $0.60.
  3. For readings, links and conjugations, decision models are not good enough on their own. The best one, GPT-6 Luna Decisions, is close to the cheap LLMs but behind them, and it costs about the same per item as batched Claude Haiku 5.5. A new cheap LLM is the better buy for these.
  4. Nothing, decision model or LLM, can predict how Claude adjudicates reviewer flags from the flag text alone (best 60%, where chance is 50%). The largest time cost in the Routine, Claude reading and deciding about 200 flags a day, cannot be cut this way.
  5. Time savings are small; cost savings are real but only matter because of the $15 daily cap. A decision model answers one item in 0.4–2.8 seconds, but a batched LLM checks 20 items in 4–13 seconds, so bulk throughput is similar. The cap is already spent every day, mostly on audio, so text checks often run on a few cents. Cheaper checks would let more of them run.

BackgroundWhat a decision model is

A decision model reads a state (text or JSON) and answers typed questions about it: noul (yes/no, returning the probability of yes), choice (one option from a list, with a probability for each), or score (a position on an ordered scale). OpenRouter serves them through one endpoint, POST /api/alpha/decisions. TypeSafe’s Jev was the first, in mid-September; the rest arrived in the following weeks, most in the past few days. They charge only for input tokens: $0.02–0.24 per million, against $0.10–2.00 for the LLMs below. They give no explanation, so a person or an LLM still has to say why something is wrong.

Of the six models named in the request, five are decision models. StepFun Step 5 Preview is an ordinary text LLM (600B-parameter mixture of experts) and was not tested here. OpenRouter lists 19 decision-model IDs in all. Two are aliases (inception/mercury-decide:free and ~typesafe/jev-latest), leaving the sixteen tested below. Respan’s two Span models accept only yes/no questions and a plain-string state, so they skipped the two multiple-choice tasks; Span-01 and Span-01 Lite gave identical answers on every item.

Step 1Screening: do they read Japanese?

A 48-item battery in four parts, with items taken from the dictionary: 16 sentence–translation pairs (half correct, half paired with an unrelated sentence’s English), 8 basic-tier words with four candidate glosses, 16 reading checks on common words (half with a deliberate misreading), and 8 English-only questions as a control. Step 2Seven tasks from the real workflow

Each task asks the question a script or Claude asks today, or one the dictionary would benefit from. Wherever possible the right answers come from the project’s own records rather than from me.

  • Furigana reading (230 items, yes/no). Is this reading right for the marked word in this example? 100 correct readings from examples; 70 plausible misreadings (lost voicing or rendaku, dropped long vowel, small ゃ written large); 16 counter readings (三本 さんぼん, 六匹 ろっぴき, 一日 ついたち…); 14 homographs given the other word’s reading (分別 ふんべつ for ぶんべつ, 方々 ほうぼう for かたがた), plus 30 homographs given their own correct reading. 16 further homograph items were dropped after checking because the “wrong” reading is acceptable (日本 にっぽん, 足跡 そくせき, 紅葉 こうよう).
  • Inline-link sense (216, yes/no). Is the marked kana word really the linked entry’s word? Answers come from reviews/link_decisions.jsonl: 110 keep and 110 unlink/retarget verdicts, at most three per base form.
  • Conjugated form (180, yes/no). Is this the correct て-form, negative, potential…? Correct forms come from the entries’ tables; wrong ones were built by conjugating with the wrong verb class or sound change (帰て, 食べらない, 書って).
  • Translation fidelity (190, yes/no). Does the English say what the Japanese says? 100 real pairs; 50 with a sibling example’s English; 40 with one meaning change made by an LLM (negation, tense, number, opposite adjective). Ten generated changes were dropped as still acceptable, mostly because Japanese leaves the subject unstated.
  • Sense of example (160, choice). Which sense of the headword does this example use? Answers from sense_numbers.
  • Transitivity (181, choice of transitive / intransitive / both). Answers from tags.transitivity. Nine items were dropped because all three strong LLMs disagreed with the tag, and the tag looks wrong: 悲観 (tagged transitive), 閉塞 (tagged intransitive), 裁判 (tagged intransitive), ぱたぱたする (tagged intransitive), 荷造り (tagged intransitive), 取引 (tagged intransitive), 参拝する (tagged intransitive), 推理 (tagged both), 復活 (tagged both).
  • Flag triage (200, yes/no). Given a flag from review_accuracy.py (its concern and suggestion), would the editor apply it? Answers come from reviews/decisions.jsonl: 100 applied and 100 rejected gloss, notes and translation flags.

Cost. In absolute terms every check here is cheap; even GPT-6.1 Sol would cover all example translations for under $40. The savings matter because the $15 cap binds. Moving translation and sense checks to decision models, and the reading, link and conjugation checks to Claude Haiku 5.5, would make whole-dictionary passes affordable inside a few days’ leftover budget rather than weeks.

Time. Model calls are a small part of a Routine cycle; Claude’s own reading and adjudicating is the large part. Decision models do not shorten that. They could cut it indirectly in one place: a better link screener means fewer false flags for Claude to clear.

TradeoffsWhat we would give up

  • Accuracy on fine detail. Decision models miss errors that turn on kana spelling: voicing, long vowels, counter sound changes, wrong verb class. On meaning-level questions they match LLMs.
  • No explanation. A decision model says no with a probability, not why. Every flag still needs an LLM or Claude to diagnose and fix it, so they suit sweeps that find rare problems, not fixing.
  • Miscalibration. Several models rank items well but put their 0.5 cut-off in the wrong place (Perplexity Decider ranks true and false translations almost perfectly, yet at 0.5 it lets a quarter of the subtly altered ones through). Cut-offs need tuning on labelled samples like the ones built here, per model and per version.
  • Immaturity. The endpoint is labelled alpha, most models are days old, and behaviour changes between versions; pin dated model IDs. In this test GPT-6 Luna Decisions refused four flag items outright (HTTP 502), Solar Decide Flash answered “both” for every transitivity question, and Solar Decide’s endpoint was slow (several seconds per call under load).
  • Calls are one item each. No batching, so a full pass means hundreds of thousands of HTTP requests; scripts need concurrency, retries and a budget ledger entry, like the existing review scripts.

RecommendationsIf you want to act on this

  1. Add a translation-fidelity sweep (build/review_translations.py) using Microsoft-Decision-1 or Jev 1.13, writing flags to reviews/accuracy_flags.jsonl under the translation dimension for Claude to adjudicate. Cost of a full pass: about $0.60.
  2. Add a sense-number check for multi-sense entries using Perplexity Decider 1.1 (about $0.11 for all 39,480 examples).
  3. Change the default model in review_links.py from Gemini 2.5 Flash to GPT-6 Luna Decisions (cheaper, catches far more wrong links) or Claude Haiku 5.5 (fewer false alarms). Re-tune on the 220 labelled items first.
  4. Separately from decision models: re-test check_conjugations.py and the furigana screen with Claude Haiku 5.5, which came within two points of GPT-6.1 Sol on both tasks here at an eighth to a tenth of the cost.
  5. Keep flag adjudication with Claude.
  6. Review the nine transitivity tags the LLMs disputed (listed in Step 2).

MethodHow the tests were run, and their limits

  • Decision models were called through /api/alpha/decisions, one item per call, with the item as a JSON state and one question whose instructions and criteria were the same for every model. LLMs got the same question and criteria in one prompt per 20 items, at temperature 0 and default reasoning settings, and were asked for a yes/no (or option) plus a probability.
  • All models saw the same items once. With 160–230 items per task, differences of about 5 points or less are within noise.
  • Some answers come from the project’s records (links, flags, transitivity, sense), so they reflect Claude’s past judgments. The others were built deliberately (misreadings, wrong conjugations, altered translations) and checked by hand where models disagreed; 16 reading items, 10 translation items and 9 transitivity items were dropped as doubtful.
  • Flag triage gave the models only the flag and the headword, not the whole entry, because applied flags have already changed the entry. Claude had the full entry when deciding, so this test is harder than the real job, but even GPT-6.1 Sol stayed near chance.
  • Prices are OpenRouter’s on 10 October 2026. The whole evaluation cost about $1.50 of OpenRouter credit.
