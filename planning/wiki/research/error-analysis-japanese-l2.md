# Error Analysis and Learner Corpora for Japanese L2

**Last updated**: 2026-04-25

## Overview

Error analysis — the systematic study of learner errors and their causes — provides direct evidence for what a learner's dictionary should warn about, contrast, and illustrate. While [Vocabulary Acquisition](vocabulary-acquisition.md) research tells us *how* words are learned and [Collocations](collocations.md) research tells us which combinations matter, error analysis tells us *where learners actually stumble*. For a bilingual learner's dictionary, this is actionable: persistent error patterns reveal exactly which usage notes, contrastive information, and example sentences add the most value.

## Major Japanese learner corpora

Research on L2 Japanese errors draws on several corpora, each with different strengths.

### I-JAS (International Corpus of Japanese as a Second Language)

The most comprehensive learner corpus for Japanese, developed by NINJAL (National Institute for Japanese Linguistics and International Studies) under the direction of Sakoda Kumiko. I-JAS contains spoken and written data from over 1,000 Japanese learners across 12 L1 backgrounds (Chinese, Korean, English, French, German, Hungarian, Indonesian, Spanish, Thai, Turkish, Vietnamese, Russian) plus native Japanese controls, collected at 20+ institutions worldwide.

Key design features:
- **Multiple task types**: picture description, story retelling, role-play, opinion essay, email writing — enabling cross-task comparison of the same learner's production
- **Proficiency-stratified**: learners at multiple CEFR/JLPT levels, allowing developmental analysis
- **Publicly available** via the NINJAL website with a concordance search interface
- **Morphologically tagged**: enabling automated searches for specific grammatical constructions

I-JAS has become the standard corpus for large-scale error studies in Japanese SLA since its public release. Its breadth across L1 backgrounds makes it uniquely valuable for identifying which errors are universal (interlingual or developmental) and which are L1-specific transfer effects.

### KY Corpus

Transcripts from Oral Proficiency Interview (OPI) assessments, consisting of approximately 90 conversations with learners at various proficiency levels. The KY Corpus was one of the earliest structured learner corpora for Japanese and remains widely cited for studies of spoken L2 Japanese. Its strength is naturalistic conversational data; its limitation is relatively small size and the interview setting, which skews toward formal register.

### LARP at SCU (Learner Corpus for Academic Japanese)

A corpus focused on academic writing by learners at Japanese universities. Useful for studying errors in formal/written registers, which differ substantially from spoken errors. Contains essays and reports from Chinese, Korean, and other L1 speakers studying in Japan.

### C-JAS (Corpus of Japanese as a Second Language)

A longitudinal spoken corpus from NINJAL tracking the development of 6 individual learners (3 Chinese L1, 3 Korean L1) over a 3-year period. Each learner underwent 8 research sessions of approximately 60 minutes each. The corpus includes error tags for syntax, grammar, and pronunciation. Its longitudinal design makes it uniquely valuable for studying developmental trajectories rather than cross-sectional snapshots.

### Other corpora

- **B-JAS (Beijing Corpus of Japanese as a Second Language)**: A longitudinal corpus tracking Chinese JSL learners over 4 years, also hosted at NINJAL. Examines both morphological and functional aspects of development.
- **Teramura Database**: An error-tagged corpus created by Teramura Hideo and colleagues, one of the foundational resources for Japanese error analysis. Contains systematically classified errors from compositions by learners with various L1 backgrounds.
- **Natane Corpus**: Written compositions by exchange students at Japanese universities, error-tagged and annotated.
- **NAIST Goyo Corpus**: An error-tagged corpus of learner writing developed at the Nara Institute of Science and Technology, used in NLP research on automatic error classification.
- **Lang-8 / TEC-JL**: User-generated correction data from the Lang-8 platform (one of the largest L2 corpora available) and TEC-JL (Koyama et al. 2020), a cleaner evaluation corpus for grammatical error correction of JSL learner writing.

## Common error types at the intermediate level

Research consistently identifies several categories of errors that persist through intermediate and advanced stages of Japanese learning. The order below roughly reflects frequency and pedagogical significance.

### 1. Particle errors

Particles are the most studied and arguably the most persistent error category in L2 Japanese. Studies using I-JAS and the KY Corpus consistently find that particle errors account for the largest share of grammatical errors, even at advanced levels.

**は vs. が**: The topic/subject distinction is the most thoroughly researched particle problem in Japanese SLA. Errors include:
- Using は where が is required (exhaustive listing, new information in subordinate clauses)
- Using が where は is required (contrastive topics, generic statements)
- Omitting particles entirely (common in spoken production, influenced by conversational Japanese norms)

Ichikawa Yasuko's error analysis work identifies は/が confusion as the error that persists longest in L2 development — even advanced learners make contextually inappropriate choices. The problem is compounded because native speakers themselves often have intuitive but not articulable knowledge of the distinction.

**に vs. で (location)**: Learners confuse the existential-location に (existence at a point) with the action-location で (activity at a location). Chinese learners in particular tend to overuse に because Chinese 在 covers both functions.

**を with intransitive verbs**: English-speaking learners sometimes produce errors like *公園を走る when they mean 公園で走る, extending the transitive-object pattern to intransitive contexts. (Note: 公園を走る is actually grammatical — the を marks a path traversed — but learners often produce such constructions without understanding the path-traversal meaning.)

**Directional particles (に/へ/まで)**: Partial overlap among these three creates confusion, especially since textbooks often present に and へ as interchangeable for direction, obscuring the cases where they are not.

### 2. Verb form and conjugation errors

**て-form production**: The て-form is one of the earliest complex conjugation patterns learners encounter, and its irregular phonological rules (五段 verbs: 書いて, 飲んで, 待って, etc.) generate errors well into the intermediate stage. Studies by Banno and others found that even when learners can produce て-forms in isolation drills, they make errors in spontaneous speech, suggesting that procedural automatization lags behind declarative knowledge.

**Conditional form selection**: Japanese has four conditional constructions (～たら, ～ば, ～と, ～なら) with partially overlapping but contextually constrained distributions. Learners tend to:
- Overuse ～たら (the most general and therefore "safest" form)
- Avoid ～ば and ～と due to distributional constraints they cannot articulate
- Produce ～なら errors with non-hypothetical antecedents

**Aspect: ～ている misuse**: The ている construction is polysemous (progressive, resultative, habitual, experiential), and learners — especially those whose L1 has a simpler aspect system — struggle with the resultative reading. Shirai & Kurono (1998) confirmed the Aspect Hypothesis prediction that learners initially associate ている with activity verbs (progressive reading) and only later extend it to achievement/accomplishment verbs (resultative reading). English speakers tend to interpret ている as always progressive (*is V-ing*), producing errors like *ドアが開いている interpreted as "the door is opening" rather than "the door is open." Gabriele & McClure (2011) found that Chinese learners similarly cannot fully extend beyond their L1 aspectual properties when acquiring ている, because Mandarin aspect markers (了, 着, 过) do not map cleanly onto ている's four functions.

### 3. Register and politeness errors

**Casual/formal mixing**: Learners frequently mix です/ます forms with plain forms within a single utterance or text. This is partly a processing issue (maintaining consistent register requires attentional resources) and partly a knowledge issue (learners may not know the plain-form equivalent of a structure they learned in polite form, or vice versa).

**Keigo avoidance and hypercorrection**: Intermediate learners tend either to avoid keigo entirely (producing socially inappropriate plain speech in formal contexts) or to hypercorrect (applying honorific prefixes inconsistently, using 尊敬語 where 謙譲語 is required, or producing double keigo like おっしゃられる).

**Written vs. spoken register**: Learners often apply spoken casual norms to written contexts (using sentence-final particles like よ and ね in essays) or, conversely, produce unnaturally formal spoken language by relying on textbook models.

### 4. Lexical and collocational errors

**False friends from Chinese (同形異義語)**: Chinese-background learners face a distinctive challenge because many kanji compounds appear in both Chinese and Japanese but with different or shifted meanings. Examples:
- 手紙 (Japanese: letter; Chinese: toilet paper)
- 勉強 (Japanese: study; Chinese: reluctantly/under compulsion)
- 大丈夫 (Japanese: okay/fine; Chinese: a real man)
- 検討 (Japanese: consider carefully; Chinese: similar but less formal)

Studies by Xu and others using I-JAS data show that Chinese learners produce more lexical transfer errors than other L1 groups but fewer particle errors, because Chinese word order partially aligns with Japanese.

**Near-synonym confusion**: Learners confuse semantically similar words that differ in register, collocational range, or connotation:
- 見る/観る/眺める/覗く (different modes of looking)
- 作る/造る/創る (different types of making/creating)
- 聞く/聴く/伺う (different modes and registers of listening/asking)

These errors are especially persistent because learners' L1 often uses a single word where Japanese distinguishes multiple options.

**Collocational violations**: Learners produce verb-noun pairings that violate Japanese collocational norms, often by translating from their L1:
- *薬を食べる (eat medicine) instead of 薬を飲む (drink/take medicine)
- *約束を壊す (break a promise) instead of 約束を破る
- *試験に落ちる is correct, but learners may produce *試験を失敗する by analogy

### 5. Sentence structure errors

**Relative clause difficulties**: English speakers in particular struggle with Japanese relative clauses (prenominal modification), which reverse the English head-final/head-initial order:
- English: "the book **that I bought yesterday**"
- Japanese: "**昨日買った**本"

Errors include inserting a relativizer (*本**の**昨日買った), maintaining English word order in the relative clause, or avoiding relative clauses entirely in favor of simpler constructions.

**Excessive use of の**: Learners overextend の as a nominalizer and connector, producing unnatural noun-modification chains or using の where other structures are more appropriate.

## L1 transfer effects

Error patterns vary systematically by learner L1, and this variation is one of the strongest findings in Japanese SLA research.

### Chinese-speaking learners

**Advantages**: Kanji knowledge provides a reading advantage and access to a large receptive vocabulary. Word order similarities (SOV vs. SVO with heavy pre-modification in both languages) reduce some structural errors.

**Distinctive errors**:
- 同形異義語 (same-form-different-meaning) kanji compounds
- Aspect transfer: Mandarin aspect markers (了, 着, 过) do not map cleanly onto Japanese ている/ていた/てある, producing systematic aspect errors
- Overuse of に for location (Chinese 在 conflates Japanese に and で)
- Fewer particle errors overall than other L1 groups but more lexical transfer errors

### Korean-speaking learners

**Advantages**: Extremely similar word order (SOV), agglutinative morphology, and a substantial shared Sino-Korean vocabulary make Korean speakers the fastest learners of Japanese grammar at early stages.

**Distinctive errors**:
- Subtle particle differences despite structural similarity (Korean 에서 covers both Japanese で and から in some contexts)
- Overextension of Korean-Japanese cognate patterns where the languages diverge
- Register system differences (Korean has a simpler honorific structure than Japanese keigo)
- Fewer structural errors but persistent particle-level errors due to assumed equivalence

### English-speaking learners

**Advantages**: Strong motivation for explicit grammar study (English-speaking learners tend to rely more on metalinguistic knowledge).

**Distinctive errors**:
- Relative clause formation (the most dramatic L1-specific error pattern)
- Topic/comment structure misanalysis (forcing English-style subject-predicate patterns onto Japanese topic-comment structures)
- Overuse of pronouns (translating English pronoun usage directly, where Japanese prefers ellipsis)
- Progressive aspect overgeneralization (interpreting all ている as progressive *-ing*)
- Word order errors in complex sentences

## Error analysis methodology

### Contrastive analysis vs. error analysis

The field has evolved from **contrastive analysis** (Lado 1957 — predicting errors from L1-L2 structural differences) through **error analysis** (Corder 1967 — classifying and explaining errors from learner production data) to **interlanguage analysis** (Selinker 1972 — treating learner language as a system in its own right). Modern corpus-based studies combine all three: they use L1-L2 comparisons to generate hypotheses, corpus data to verify which errors actually occur, and interlanguage theory to explain developmental patterns.

### Error classification schemes

Most Japanese error studies use some variant of the classification scheme developed by Ichikawa Yasuko (市川保子), which distinguishes:
- **Surface-level errors**: form (conjugation, spelling, particle choice)
- **Structural errors**: word order, clause structure, modification patterns
- **Lexical errors**: word choice, collocation, register mismatch
- **Pragmatic errors**: politeness level, contextual appropriateness

The Teramura Database uses a more granular scheme with over 30 error categories organized hierarchically.

## Implications for je-dict-1

Error analysis research provides specific, actionable guidance for dictionary design. The most important implications:

### 1. Prioritize particle information in verb entries

Particle errors are the most frequent and persistent error type. Every verb entry should make its particle frame explicit — not just mentioning particles in notes but showing them in example sentences and collocations. The v2 quality standard's "particle predicate lists" (HIGH PRIORITY) is strongly validated by error research. For verbs where learners commonly use the wrong particle, a specific warning is warranted.

### 2. Address ている polysemy explicitly

The aspect section (ている behavior) in verb entries directly targets one of the most persistent intermediate errors. When a verb's ている form has a resultative rather than progressive reading (開いている = "is open", not "is opening"), this must be stated explicitly because learners' default interpretation is progressive. The `polish_aspect_notes.md` task addresses this, but error research suggests it should be among the highest polishing priorities.

### 3. Include contrastive notes for near-synonyms

Near-synonym confusion is a persistent lexical error type. The "SIMILAR WORDS" section in entries should go beyond listing alternatives to explaining *when to use which one and why*. Error analysis data suggests focusing on:
- The collocational range (which nouns each verb takes)
- Register differences
- Connotation differences
- Contexts where the wrong choice sounds unnatural vs. outright wrong

### 4. Flag false friends for Chinese-background learners

The 同形異義語 problem is well-documented and specific enough to address systematically. Entries for kanji compounds that have different meanings in Chinese could include a brief note: "Chinese speakers: note that this word's meaning differs from the Chinese cognate." This serves the dictionary's largest potential learner demographic without cluttering entries for other learners.

### 5. Show register consistency in examples

Register-mixing errors suggest that example sentences within an entry should demonstrate consistent register use. When an entry shows both casual and formal examples, they should be clearly labeled — not interleaved without explanation. The `register` field in entries and the register notes in the notes section address this, but the error research suggests that *examples* are where learners actually internalize register patterns.

### 6. Design examples that preempt collocational errors

Rather than choosing examples that merely illustrate meaning, entries for verbs with error-prone collocations should include examples demonstrating the *correct* collocation — especially where the learner's L1 would suggest a different verb. For 飲む, an example with 薬を飲む (take medicine) preempts the common *薬を食べる error. This connects to [Example Sentence Design](example-sentences.md) and the "encoding-optimized examples" concept from [Grammar Information](grammar-in-dictionaries.md).

### 7. Use error frequency to prioritize polishing

The polishing priority system (`make priorities`) currently uses note quality scores and field-presence checks. Error analysis data could inform an additional priority dimension: entries for words that are frequent sites of learner errors should be polished first, since their notes and examples have the highest pedagogical impact.
