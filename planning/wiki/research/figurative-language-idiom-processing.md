# Figurative Language and Idiom Processing in L2

**Last updated**: 2026-05-28

## Overview

Figurative language — idioms, metaphors, metonymy, and conventionalized expressions — pervades natural discourse. Estimates suggest that speakers produce roughly six novel metaphors and four idioms per minute of discourse (Pollio et al. 1977). For L2 learners, figurative language represents a persistent comprehension and production bottleneck: even advanced learners who control grammar and high-frequency vocabulary stumble on expressions whose meaning cannot be computed from their parts. This page surveys how L1 and L2 speakers process figurative language, what makes idioms easy or hard to learn, and how dictionaries can support figurative competence.

## Processing models for figurative language

### L1 processing: three competing accounts

Early psycholinguistic work assumed figurative language required special processing — the **standard pragmatic model** (Searle 1979) held that listeners first compute the literal meaning, find it anomalous, and then search for the figurative interpretation. This sequential view was challenged by three major alternatives:

1. **Direct access model** (Gibbs 1994). For familiar idioms, the figurative meaning is retrieved directly from the mental lexicon without computing the literal meaning first. Gibbs's experiments showed that familiar idioms were understood as quickly as (or faster than) literal paraphrases, suggesting that figurative meaning is stored as a lexical unit.

2. **Graded salience hypothesis** (Giora 1997, 2003). The most *salient* meaning — the one most frequent, familiar, conventional, or prototypical — is always processed first, regardless of whether it is literal or figurative. For conventional idioms like "break the ice," the figurative meaning is more salient than the literal one, so it is accessed first. For novel metaphors, the literal meaning is more salient. Salience, not literality, is the organising principle.

3. **Idiom decomposition hypothesis** (Nunberg, Sag & Wasow 1994; Gibbs, Nayak & Cutting 1989). Idioms are not uniformly "frozen" but vary along a continuum of semantic *decomposability* — the degree to which individual words contribute identifiable parts of the figurative meaning. "Pop the question" is decomposable (pop → suddenly produce; question → marriage proposal), while "kick the bucket" is non-decomposable. Decomposable idioms allow more syntactic flexibility and are processed partly compositionally.

### L2 processing: the literal salience shift

L2 learners process figurative language differently from native speakers in systematic ways:

**Cieślicka's literal salience model** (2006). In a cross-modal lexical priming study with advanced Polish learners of English, Cieślicka found that L2 learners showed a strong literal-meaning bias: literal meanings of idiom constituent words were activated faster than figurative meanings, even for well-known idioms in figurative contexts. This contrasts with L1 speakers, for whom the figurative meaning of familiar idioms is typically more salient. Cieślicka proposed that for L2 users, literal meanings are *always* most salient because literal words and phrases are more frequently encountered and used by language learners, even at advanced proficiency levels.

**Abel's dual idiom representation model** (2003). Based on decomposability judgments from German learners of English, Abel proposed that L2 speakers maintain two types of mental representations for idioms: *idiom entries* (holistic units retrieved as chunks) for non-decomposable idioms, and *constituent entries* (compositionally processed) for decomposable idioms. Because L2 speakers encounter idioms far less frequently than L1 speakers, they have fewer holistic idiom entries and rely more on compositional processing — which is slower and more error-prone for opaque idioms.

**The parasitic model** (Liontas 2002). L2 idiom acquisition proceeds "parasitically" on L1 knowledge: learners map L2 idioms onto existing L1 conceptual structures. When L1 and L2 share an idiom (e.g., English "break the ice" and Spanish *romper el hielo*), positive transfer facilitates comprehension. When the L2 idiom has no L1 counterpart, learners must build a new form-meaning mapping from scratch — a much slower and more error-prone process.

### Cross-linguistic transfer effects

Irujo's (1986) foundational study with Spanish-speaking learners of English categorised idioms into three types based on L1-L2 similarity:

| Type | Example | Comprehension | Production |
|------|---------|---------------|------------|
| **Identical** (same form and meaning in L1/L2) | "break the ice" / *romper el hielo* | Highest accuracy | Positive transfer |
| **Similar** (related form, same meaning) | "give a hand" / *echar una mano* ("throw a hand") | Good, with occasional L1 form intrusion | L1 form substitution errors |
| **Different** (no L1 counterpart) | "spill the beans" | Lowest accuracy | Avoidance or circumlocution |

The avoidance pattern is particularly important: learners systematically underuse idioms that lack L1 equivalents, even when they know them receptively. This production gap echoes findings from formulaic language research more broadly (see [Formulaic Language](formulaic-language-phraseological-competence.md)).

## Metaphorical competence and conceptual fluency

### Danesi's conceptual fluency framework

Danesi (1992, 1995) argued that true L2 proficiency requires not just grammatical and communicative competence but also **metaphorical competence** — the ability to comprehend and produce metaphors as used in natural discourse. He introduced the construct of **conceptual fluency**: the ability to align formal linguistic structures with the underlying conceptual structures of the target language. Advanced L2 speakers who lack conceptual fluency produce grammatically correct but conceptually L1-influenced speech — they "think in the L1 while speaking in the L2."

Danesi observed that even advanced learners tend to map L1 conceptual metaphors onto L2 structures. An English speaker learning Japanese might extend the English metaphor ARGUMENT IS WAR (attack a position, defend a claim) to contexts where Japanese conventionally uses different source domains (e.g., 議論を{戦|たたか}わす exists, but many English "war" metaphors for argumentation have no Japanese equivalent).

### Boers's metaphor awareness approach

Boers (2000) demonstrated experimentally that raising learners' awareness of the conceptual metaphors underlying figurative expressions significantly improves vocabulary retention. In three EFL experiments, learners who studied figurative expressions grouped by source domain (e.g., BODY metaphors: "the heart of the matter," "shoulder responsibility," "the backbone of the economy") outperformed learners who studied the same expressions in alphabetical order on both immediate and delayed post-tests.

Boers (2004) further specified that the optimal proficiency level for metaphor-awareness instruction is **intermediate**: beginners lack the lexical base to engage in metaphorical analysis, while advanced learners may already have established (possibly incorrect) form-meaning mappings that resist restructuring.

### Littlemore's four-component model

Littlemore (2001) operationalised metaphorical competence as four measurable components:

1. **Originality of metaphor production** — creative generation of novel figurative expressions
2. **Ability to find meaning in metaphor** — accuracy of figurative interpretation
3. **Speed of figurative interpretation** — processing fluency for figurative meanings
4. **Fluency of metaphor interpretation** — range and variety of figurative readings generated

Littlemore found that these components were largely independent of each other, suggesting that metaphorical competence is not a unitary ability. Speed of interpretation correlated with a holistic cognitive style, while accuracy depended more on vocabulary depth and contextual reasoning.

## Factors affecting idiom difficulty for L2 learners

Research identifies several properties that predict how easy or difficult an idiom will be for L2 learners:

### Transparency and decomposability

The most robust predictor. Transparent idioms whose figurative meaning can be recovered from the meanings of their parts (e.g., "test the waters") are easier than opaque idioms whose figurative meaning bears no relation to constituent meanings (e.g., "kick the bucket"). Nunberg et al. (1994) formalised this as a continuum, not a binary, and experimental work confirms that decomposable idioms are processed faster and learned more easily by L2 speakers (Abel 2003).

### Cross-linguistic equivalence

As Irujo (1986) demonstrated, idioms with direct L1 equivalents are comprehended and retained far more easily than language-specific idioms. This has strong implications for Japanese-English dictionaries, where many Japanese figurative expressions (慣用句, 四字熟語) have no English structural equivalent.

### Frequency and familiarity

Higher-frequency idioms are learned faster because more encounters build stronger memory traces and eventually shift the idiom from compositional processing to direct retrieval (Abel 2003). This follows from frequency-based models of language acquisition (Ellis 2002) — the same mechanism that drives general vocabulary acquisition.

### Context support

Idioms encountered in rich supportive contexts are more easily interpreted than those encountered in isolation or in neutral contexts (Liontas 2002). However, context alone is insufficient for opaque idioms: learners need explicit instruction or dictionary support to establish the form-meaning link.

### Imageability

Idioms based on concrete, imageable source domains (e.g., body-part idioms like "turn a blind eye") tend to be more learnable than abstract ones. Boers (2000) attributes this to the dual-coding effect: imageable idioms activate both verbal and visual memory traces.

## Japanese figurative language: categories and challenges

Japanese has several major categories of figurative/fixed expressions, each presenting distinct challenges for L2 learners:

### 慣用句 (kan'yōku) — conventional idioms

Body-part idioms are among the most common and productive: {目|め}を{回|まわ}す (to be stunned — lit. "eyes spin"), {腹|はら}が{立|た}つ (to be angry — lit. "stomach stands"), {手|て}を{打|う}つ (to take measures — lit. "strike hands"). Many have partial English equivalents using different body parts (Japanese "stomach stands" vs. English "blood boils" for anger), creating a cross-linguistic mapping problem where L1 transfer can produce plausible but incorrect interpretations.

### 四字熟語 (yojijukugo) — four-character compound idioms

Compressed Sino-Japanese expressions like {一石二鳥|いっせきにちょう} ("one stone two birds" = killing two birds with one stone) and {以心伝心|いしんでんしん} ("heart-to-heart transmission" = tacit understanding). Transparency varies widely: some are decomposable from their component kanji ({自業自得|じごうじとく} — "own deed, own result" = you reap what you sow), while others are culturally opaque ({朝三暮四|ちょうさんぼし} — "three in the morning, four in the evening" = inconsistency, from a Chinese fable about monkeys). For Chinese-background learners, many yojijukugo are cognates with their Chinese equivalents, providing a transfer advantage not available to alphabetic-L1 learners.

### ことわざ (kotowaza) — proverbs

Proverbs like {猿|さる}も{木|き}から{落|お}ちる ("even monkeys fall from trees" = even experts make mistakes) are transparent in isolation but require cultural knowledge to use appropriately — the register, the situations where a proverb is apt, and the social dynamics of quoting proverbs in conversation.

### 比喩的用法 (hiyuteki yōhō) — figurative senses of ordinary words

Beyond fixed expressions, many ordinary Japanese words carry conventionalised figurative senses that L2 learners must acquire separately: {甘|あま}い can mean sweet (literal), naive/lenient (figurative); {固|かた}い can mean hard (literal), stiff/formal/reliable (figurative). These polysemous figurative extensions may map differently from L1 equivalents — English "sweet" extends to "sweet person" (kind), while Japanese {甘|あま}い extends to "sweet judgment" (lenient), a different evaluative dimension.

## Dictionary treatment of figurative language

### The challenge for learner dictionaries

Figurative language poses several specific problems for dictionary design:

1. **Discovery problem**: Learners encountering an unfamiliar idiom in context may not recognise it as a fixed expression, instead trying to look up individual words. The more opaque the idiom, the worse this problem is.

2. **Placement problem**: Where should an idiom be found — under which headword? "Turn a blind eye" could logically appear under *turn*, *blind*, or *eye*. Cross-referencing can help but multiplies the editorial burden.

3. **Explanation problem**: A simple L1 translation equivalent may convey the denotation but miss register, connotation, and usage restrictions. "Kick the bucket" = "to die" loses the informal/humorous register.

4. **Production support**: Receptive definitions ("X means Y") are insufficient for learners who want to use figurative expressions. They need information about register, collocational constraints, syntactic restrictions, and situational appropriateness.

### How major dictionaries handle idioms

The Big Four English learner dictionaries (OALD, LDOCE, COBUILD, MED) generally place idioms at the end of the entry for the most semantically salient constituent, with cross-references from other constituents. COBUILD's full-sentence definitions are particularly effective for idioms because they embed pragmatic information naturally ("If you say that someone has *kicked the bucket*, you mean that they have died. This expression is used humorously.").

Japanese monolingual dictionaries (広辞苑, 大辞林) typically list idioms under the first content word, with limited cross-referencing. JMdict tags expressions with "exp" but provides minimal contextual or pragmatic information. Kenkyusha's dictionaries give the richest treatment, with usage notes and example sentences showing register.

### Implications for je-dict-1

je-dict-1's structure offers several affordances for figurative language support:

1. **Dedicated expression entries**: The dictionary's 931 expression entries give idioms their own headword space rather than burying them under a component word. This solves the placement problem but requires robust cross-referencing back to component words.

2. **Notes field for motivation**: The notes field can explain *why* an expression means what it means — the underlying conceptual metaphor or cultural scenario. Research (Boers 2000) shows that etymological/motivational information aids retention. For {腹|はら}が{立|た}つ, explaining the body-part metaphor linking stomach to emotions in Japanese culture helps learners anchor the figurative meaning.

3. **Similar words for figurative near-synonyms**: The SIMILAR WORDS section can discriminate between figurative expressions with overlapping meanings — e.g., {怒|おこ}る vs. {腹|はら}が{立|た}つ vs. {頭|あたま}にくる (three ways of expressing anger with different registers and nuances).

4. **Examples showing figurative vs. literal use**: For polysemous words with both literal and figurative senses ({甘|あま}い, {固|かた}い, {深|ふか}い), examples can be ordered to progress from literal to figurative uses, helping learners recognize the metaphorical extension.

5. **Cross-references as conceptual networks**: Cross-references between body-part idioms ({目|め}, {手|て}, {口|くち}, {腹|はら} expressions), or between semantically related proverbs, mirror the conceptual clustering that Boers (2000) found aids retention. Currently, cross-references are primarily used for synonym/antonym links rather than conceptual-domain links, but the infrastructure supports this.

6. **Transparency information in notes**: For fixed expressions, the notes field could explicitly indicate whether the expression is transparent, semi-transparent, or opaque — information that research shows affects both processing strategy and learnability. This would be particularly valuable for yojijukugo, where Chinese-background learners and alphabetic-L1 learners face very different transparency levels.

### Current gaps and priorities

| Gap | Impact | Difficulty |
|-----|--------|------------|
| Figurative senses of polysemous words often lack explicit "figurative" labeling | Learners may not notice the literal→figurative extension | Low — add sense labels |
| Expression entries rarely explain motivating metaphor/scenario | Misses the retention benefit of etymological elaboration | Medium — requires research per expression |
| No conceptual-domain cross-references (body-part idioms, nature metaphors, etc.) | Missed clustering opportunity for retention | Medium — needs a tagging scheme |
| Limited register/pragmatic marking on expressions | Production errors from using idioms in wrong contexts | Medium — existing register framework can be extended |
| No transparency rating for fixed expressions | Can't guide study strategy (transparent → opaque) | Low conceptually, but needs evaluation criteria |
