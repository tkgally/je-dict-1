# Dictionary Skills and Reference Skills Training

**Last updated**: 2026-06-04

## Overview

Using a dictionary is not a trivial act. It requires a set of identifiable, teachable skills — from recognizing when a lookup is needed to navigating entry structure, selecting the right sense, and integrating retrieved information into comprehension or production. Research consistently shows that many L2 learners lack these skills and that explicit training improves outcomes. Yet dictionary skills instruction remains what Nesi (1999) called "a considerably neglected part of L2 teaching." This page surveys the research on what dictionary skills are, how they are acquired, and what training interventions have been shown to work, with implications for how je-dict-1's design can compensate for skill deficits and support self-directed learning.

## The dictionary lookup as a multi-stage process

### Scholfield's process model

Scholfield (1982, 1999) was among the first to decompose dictionary use into ordered stages, distinguishing between lookup for **comprehension** (receptive) and for **production** (productive). His 1999 model for receptive use identifies five stages:

1. **Locate the unknown** — identify which word or phrase in the text is problematic
2. **Determine the search form** — derive the citation form from the inflected form encountered (e.g., 食べている → 食べる)
3. **Search the dictionary** — find the relevant entry
4. **Select the appropriate sense** — choose among multiple senses listed in the entry
5. **Integrate the meaning** — incorporate the retrieved meaning back into the original context

For productive use, the process runs partly in reverse: the learner starts with a concept and must find the appropriate L2 word, then verify its constraints (collocation, register, syntax) in the entry information.

Each stage is a potential failure point. Scholfield's framework makes visible why even well-designed dictionaries produce poor results when users lack skill at particular stages — a point confirmed by Nesi and Haill's (2002) error taxonomy (see below).

### Nesi's stages of dictionary use

Nesi (1999) proposed a parallel framework with three broad stages:

1. **Before consultation** — deciding whether to consult at all, choosing which dictionary to use, and determining the search term
2. **During consultation** — locating the entry, navigating its structure, reading and interpreting the information
3. **After consultation** — evaluating the retrieved information, deciding whether the lookup was successful, and applying the result to the task

This framework emphasizes the metacognitive dimension: skilled users make strategic decisions at every stage, while unskilled users often bypass evaluation entirely, accepting the first definition found.

### Lew's digital-era adaptation

Lew (2013) revised both earlier frameworks for the digital era in "Online Dictionary Skills," arguing that several traditional print dictionary skills have become less important (alphabetical ordering, guide words, abbreviation decoding) while new skills have emerged:

- **Query formulation** — typing effective search strings rather than browsing alphabetically
- **Result evaluation** — assessing the quality and relevance of results, especially when multiple dictionary sources return results simultaneously
- **Interface navigation** — understanding expandable sections, hyperlinks, audio controls, and pop-up definitions
- **Source selection** — choosing among multiple online dictionaries with different strengths
- **Critical evaluation** — assessing the authority and accuracy of free online resources, which vary enormously in quality

Lew argued that the "search" stage has become trivially easy in digital dictionaries (type and press Enter), shifting the skill burden toward evaluation and integration.

## Components of dictionary reference skills

### Hartmann and James's definition

Hartmann and James (1998) define **reference skills** as "the abilities required on the part of the dictionary user to find the information being sought." This framing emphasizes that dictionary skills are user-side abilities, not dictionary-side features — though good dictionary design can reduce the skill threshold required.

### A skills taxonomy

Synthesizing across Scholfield (1982, 1999), Nesi (1999), Lew (2013), and Lew and Szymańska (2015), the following taxonomy captures the components:

| Skill category | Components | Print era | Digital era |
|---------------|------------|-----------|-------------|
| **Pre-lookup** | Recognizing a need; choosing a dictionary; determining the search form | High importance | Reduced (search handles inflection, but form recognition still matters for Japanese) |
| **Search** | Alphabetical navigation; using guide words and thumb indexes | Central skill | Largely automated |
| **Entry navigation** | Understanding entry structure; reading POS labels, pronunciation guides, grammar codes | High importance | Changed but still important — hyperlinks, collapsible sections |
| **Sense selection** | Identifying the correct sense among polysemous entries | Critical | Critical — unchanged by medium |
| **Information integration** | Applying the meaning to the original context; evaluating collocational fit, register, syntax | Critical | Critical — unchanged |
| **Cross-reference use** | Following see-also links, understanding relationship types | Moderate | Elevated — hyperlinks make cross-referencing frictionless but require evaluation skill |
| **Critical evaluation** | Assessing dictionary quality, recognizing gaps, comparing sources | Low (trusted publisher brands) | High (open web, variable quality) |

### Japanese-specific skill challenges

Dictionary use for Japanese learners involves additional skill requirements not shared by alphabetic languages:

- **Form determination is harder**: a learner who encounters 食べられている must decompose this into a stem (食べる), identify it as potential (or passive), and derive the citation form — a multi-step morphological analysis. Print dictionaries require the user to know the citation form; even digital dictionaries that accept inflected forms need the user to type correctly
- **Script selection for search**: the learner must decide whether to search by hiragana, katakana, kanji, or romaji — each may yield different results in different dictionaries
- **Kanji lookup**: looking up an unknown kanji by radical, stroke count, or handwriting recognition is a distinct skill set from word lookup (see [Kanji Learning and Dictionary Treatment](kanji-learning-dictionaries.md) for the lookup method research)
- **Multiple reading disambiguation**: a single entry may have multiple readings (e.g., 生 with on'yomi セイ/ショウ and kun'yomi い/う/なま/は/き); the user must connect the reading encountered in context to the correct entry

## Evidence on dictionary skills deficits

### Nesi and Haill (2002): the error taxonomy

Nesi and Haill (2002) analyzed 89 international students' dictionary-use errors during natural reading tasks at a British university. They categorized failures into five types:

1. **Wrong entry or subentry selected** — the user found a different word entirely (often a homograph or near-miss)
2. **Correct entry, misinterpreted information** — the user found the right word but misunderstood the definition, grammar code, or usage note
3. **Correct entry, wrong sense selected** — the user chose a sense that didn't fit the context
4. **Correct entry, correct sense rejected** — the user found the right answer but dismissed it, usually because it didn't match their prior expectation
5. **Word or appropriate sense not in the dictionary** — a genuine gap in dictionary coverage

Type 3 (wrong sense selection) was the most common failure mode. Type 4 (correct answer rejected) was particularly insidious — the user did the mechanical work correctly but lacked the metacognitive skill to evaluate their result.

### Tono (2001): Japanese learners

Tono (2001) found that Japanese university students overwhelmingly preferred bilingual dictionaries and that "only a handful" used monolingual dictionaries for L2 learning. His research on lookup strategies revealed that Japanese EFL learners frequently:

- Failed to identify parts of speech, hindering searches in dictionaries organized by POS
- Accepted the first translation equivalent without checking contextual fit
- Rarely used usage examples to verify their chosen translation
- Ignored grammar codes and register labels entirely

These findings are consistent with the international evidence but are amplified by the additional complexity of Japanese orthography, which adds a layer of difficulty to the search stage.

### Wingate (2002): bilingual dictionary dependence

Wingate (2002) identified systemic problems with learners' over-reliance on bilingual dictionaries:

- They reinforce the tendency to translate rather than think in the target language
- They do not support paraphrasing skills needed for academic writing
- They provide little information about sense relations (synonyms, antonyms, hyponyms)
- They give a false impression of one-to-one translation equivalence

This last point connects directly to the problem documented in [Translation Equivalence](translation-equivalence.md) — the bilingual mapping problem. Learners who lack awareness of this mapping problem are poorly equipped to evaluate bilingual dictionary entries critically.

## Can dictionary skills be taught?

### Lew and Galas (2008): the training experiment

Lew and Galas (2008) conducted a controlled training study with 57 Polish primary school learners of English, delivering 12 sessions of explicit dictionary skills instruction. The study addressed whether dictionary skills could be improved through teaching — a question that seems obvious but had received surprisingly little empirical attention.

Key findings:
- Most participants (50 of 57) had **no prior dictionary training** — their schools had simply not taught it
- After the 12-session intervention, trained students performed significantly better on dictionary-use tasks than untrained controls
- The greatest gains were in **sense selection** and **entry navigation** — the very skills that Nesi and Haill (2002) had identified as the most common failure points

The study demonstrated that dictionary skills are indeed teachable and that even brief, structured instruction produces measurable improvements. The implication is that the absence of dictionary skills training — not inherent difficulty — is the primary reason learners use dictionaries poorly.

### Productive skills training

Research on productive dictionary use (using dictionaries for writing and speaking) shows that learners need different skills than for receptive use. For production, the critical skills are:

- **Verifying constraints**: checking that a word found in the dictionary is appropriate for the intended register, collocation, and syntactic context
- **Example sentence mining**: using example sentences as models for production, not just as comprehension aids
- **Distinguishing near-synonyms**: using the dictionary to choose between similar words — which requires skills in reading synonym discrimination notes, collocation information, and register labels

These production-oriented skills are particularly relevant for dictionaries like je-dict-1, which provide rich information about collocations, similar words, and register.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### Compensatory design: reducing the skill threshold

Dictionary design can compensate for user skill deficits at each stage of the lookup process:

| Lookup stage | Common failure | je-dict-1 design feature |
|-------------|----------------|-------------------------|
| Pre-lookup: determining search form | Can't derive citation form from inflected form | **Conjugation-based search**: the search index includes inflected forms, so searching for 食べている finds the entry for 食べる |
| Search: finding the entry | Can't type the correct form; unsure of reading | **Multiple search paths**: romaji, hiragana, kanji, and English gloss all work; `headword_alternatives` index variant forms |
| Entry navigation: understanding structure | Ignores grammar codes and abbreviations | **Natural-language notes** instead of coded abbreviations; structured sections with clear headers (TRANSITIVITY, ASPECT, COLLOCATIONS) |
| Sense selection: choosing the right meaning | Accepts the first sense | **Numbered senses with distinct glosses**; example sentences for each sense showing the word in context |
| Information integration: applying the result | Doesn't verify contextual fit | **Progressive example sentences** (simple → complex) demonstrate the word in varied contexts; collocations show natural pairings |
| Cross-reference use: following links | Doesn't notice see-also links | **Prominent see-also** for must-know connections displayed at the top of the entry; inline word links (`⟦...⟧`) in examples and notes |
| Critical evaluation: assessing quality | Unaware of dictionary gaps or errors | Less relevant for a curated dictionary — but the tier system and coverage metrics help learners understand the dictionary's scope |

### Supporting self-directed learning

Research on dictionary skills consistently emphasizes that the ultimate goal is **learner autonomy** — the ability to use dictionaries independently without a teacher. Several je-dict-1 features support this:

- **Furigana on all kanji** removes the kanji-reading bottleneck that prevents less advanced learners from reading entries independently
- **Cross-references as exploration paths** reward browsing and serendipitous discovery, building the learner's sense of how vocabulary networks work
- **Self-contained tier structure** ensures that basic- and core-tier entries use vocabulary the learner already knows, reducing lookup chains (looking up a word in a definition, then looking up that word, etc.)
- **Structured notes** make information scannable rather than requiring the user to read dense paragraphs — important for the "entry navigation" skill

### The sense selection problem and je-dict-1

Sense selection — the most common failure point in the research — deserves special attention. je-dict-1 mitigates this through:

- **Concise, distinct gloss lines** at the top of each sense, making it easy to scan for the right meaning
- **Example sentences per sense**, not just per entry — the learner can match their encountered context against the examples to verify which sense fits
- **"Similar words" sections** that explicitly contrast near-synonyms, addressing the production-side challenge of choosing between alternatives

### What je-dict-1 cannot do

No dictionary design can fully substitute for reference skills training. Two persistent challenges:

1. **The "first definition" trap**: research shows many users select the first definition they encounter and stop reading. This is a user behavior problem that even well-designed dictionaries cannot solve through structure alone. A potential mitigation is sense ordering: placing the most common/prototypical sense first so that the "first definition" strategy more often succeeds.

2. **The consultation decision**: the decision of when to look up a word and when to guess from context is a metacognitive skill that sits outside the dictionary entirely. Research (see [Vocabulary Learning Strategies](vocabulary-learning-strategies.md)) suggests that learners who over-rely on dictionaries may have worse retention than those who develop a balanced strategy of guessing, then confirming.
