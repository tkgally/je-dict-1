# Semantic Prosody

**Last updated**: 2026-04-15

## Overview

**Semantic prosody** is the tendency for certain words to habitually occur with positive or negative contexts, such that the word itself becomes coloured by that evaluative environment. The term was coined by John Sinclair (1991, 1996) and developed by Bill Louw (1993), Michael Stubbs (1995, 2001), and Alan Partington (1998, 2004).

A classic English illustration: the verb *cause* almost always takes undesirable objects (*cause problems, cause cancer, cause delays*) while its near-synonym *bring about* is more neutral. Learners who treat them as interchangeable produce odd-sounding output like *?cause happiness*. Semantic prosody is different from individual word connotation: it is a statistical pattern of co-occurrence that creates evaluative meaning across a phraseological window.

For learner dictionaries, semantic prosody presents a problem. The information is rarely explicit in monolingual native-speaker dictionaries (because native speakers internalise it unconsciously) and is often invisible in bilingual glosses (because near-synonyms in the target language may have different prosodies). It must be surfaced deliberately, usually in usage notes or through carefully chosen examples.

## Types of evaluative patterning

Partington (2004) and Stubbs (2001) distinguish several overlapping levels at which evaluation attaches to words:

- **Denotational meaning** — what the word literally refers to (*cancer*: a disease).
- **Connotation** — stable emotional or attitudinal associations carried by the word in isolation (*slim* vs. *skinny*).
- **Semantic preference** — the tendency to co-occur with words from a particular semantic field (*utterly* + adjectives of absoluteness or extremity).
- **Semantic prosody** — the evaluative colouring that emerges from habitual collocation with positive, negative, or neutral contexts, often across a larger phraseological unit than a simple collocation.

Semantic prosody is the most diffuse and hardest to pin down with a single dictionary entry, because it lives in the environment of the word rather than in the word itself. Hunston (2007) and Whitsitt (2005) have challenged whether "semantic prosody" is a coherent category distinct from connotation or preference, but the phenomenon — that some words sound wrong with positive things and others wrong with negative things — is robust even if the theoretical boundaries are contested.

## Semantic prosody in Japanese

Japanese displays the same patterns. Some commonly-cited examples:

### Adverbs with fixed evaluative polarity

- **ろくに** — almost always occurs with negatives: *ろくに寝ていない* (haven't slept properly), *ろくに食べない* (barely eats). The polarity constraint is stronger than in English "properly."
- **なかなか** — shows polarity-dependent evaluation: in affirmative contexts it upgrades (*なかなかいい* = "quite good"), but in negative contexts it conveys impatience or frustration (*なかなか来ない* = "[annoyingly] doesn't come").
- **いちいち** — typically negative, carrying annoyance: *いちいち文句を言う* (complain about every little thing).
- **いかにも** — frequently pejorative when applied to people or behaviour (*いかにも学生らしい* can be condescending), though neutral in some uses.
- **わざわざ** — neutral-to-positive when acknowledging someone's effort, but can turn passive-aggressive in complaint contexts.
- **せっかく** — marks effort or opportunity at risk of being wasted; the evaluation is about anticipated loss.
- **まさか** — signals disbelief and typically anticipates a negative outcome or bad news.

### Verbs and auxiliaries

- **〜てしまう / 〜ちゃう** — perfective aspect with a prosody of regret, unintendedness, or irreversibility: *忘れてしまった* (I regretfully forgot). The English gloss "end up (doing)" captures the prosody; "finished (doing)" misses it.
- **〜てやる / 〜てあげる / 〜てくれる / 〜てもらう** — the benefactive auxiliaries carry deontic and affective colouring (imposition, gratitude, condescension) that is not captured by "for" or "do for me."
- **〜がる** — objectifies internal states of third parties, often with a faintly distancing or judgemental prosody (*欲しがる* = "acts like they want it").
- **〜ぶる** — "put on airs of" — strongly negative evaluative prosody (*えらぶる*, *知ったかぶる*).

### Nouns and noun-modifiers

- **とんでもない** — literal "unthinkable" but prosodically carries outrage; the idiomatic *とんでもないです* ("not at all / you're welcome") has drifted toward a politeness formula, illustrating how prosody can be reshaped by conventionalisation.
- **〜くさい** (compound suffix) — adds a pejorative prosody of suspicion or distastefulness (*面倒くさい*, *嘘くさい*, *古くさい*).
- **〜っぽい** — vaguely judgemental approximation, often slightly negative (*子供っぽい*, *忘れっぽい*).

### Formality-bundled prosody

Some Japanese items bundle register with prosody: *やつ* carries a casual-to-rough prosody, while its polite counterpart *方* does not, even though both can refer to people or things. A learner who substitutes them preserves the referent but disrupts the social meaning.

## Acquisition and L2 production

The L2 literature on semantic prosody — including Xiao & McEnery (2006), Wei & Li (2014), and Hoey's (2005) *Lexical Priming* framework — consistently finds that:

- Advanced learners can produce the correct lexical items but misuse them prosodically (e.g., pairing a negative-prosody word with a neutral or positive context).
- Prosody errors are more resistant to teaching than purely grammatical errors because the feedback loop is slow: native speakers rarely correct them explicitly.
- Exposure alone is not sufficient — learners need to notice the evaluative pattern, which requires either direct instruction or attention-directing dictionary information.

Hoey's **lexical priming** hypothesis reframes semantic prosody as a product of repeated exposure: every encounter with a word primes it with its collocational and prosodic environment. Learner dictionaries can accelerate this priming by explicitly stating the pattern rather than leaving it to be inferred from corpus exposure.

## Dictionary treatment strategies

How learner dictionaries surface semantic prosody varies:

1. **Explicit usage notes** — "Usually used in negative contexts" (Kenkyusha's *Luminous* does this occasionally; the *Macmillan English Dictionary* and *Longman* use labels like "usually disapproving").
2. **Prosody-marked glosses** — glosses that include the evaluative flavour: "(regrettably) to end up ~ing" rather than "to finish ~ing."
3. **Selected examples** — choosing examples that exemplify the typical prosodic environment, so the pattern is inferrable.
4. **Negative evidence** — noting what the word is *not* used for ("not used of pleasant experiences").
5. **Contrast panels** — pairing the word with a prosody-neutral near-synonym ("ろくに vs. きちんと").

Most general-purpose J-E dictionaries favour strategy 3 implicitly, but without explicit framing the prosody is often missed. Corpus-aware pedagogical dictionaries (advanced L2 English dictionaries in particular) have moved toward strategies 1 and 2.

## Implications for je-dict-1

The project's current notes structure is well-suited to prosody documentation but does not yet treat it as a named category. Concrete openings:

- **USAGE sections already carry prosody information implicitly**. Entries for ろくに, いちいち, せっかく, 〜てしまう, 〜ぶる, 〜くさい should make the evaluative colouring explicit ("nearly always used in negative sentences," "carries a prosody of regret or unintendedness").
- **SIMILAR WORDS contrasts already function partly as prosody panels** — the contrast between prosody-carrying and prosody-neutral near-synonyms is one of the most useful discriminators a learner can be given. Reviewers should verify that contrastive blocks for evaluative adverbs and suffixes name the prosodic asymmetry.
- **Example sentences reinforce prosody only if chosen deliberately**. Progressive-length examples that all happen to use a negative-prosody word in neutral or positive contexts actively mislead. This is something the multi-model review pass could be asked to flag.
- **Polarity-dependent items** (なかなか, あまり, べつに) need notes that state the polarity behaviour *and* its evaluative consequences, not just the grammatical constraint.
- **LLM-based analysis is a good fit**. As noted in [Beyond Flat Corpora](beyond-flat-corpora.md), flat frequency data cannot say whether an environment is positive or negative; an LLM can. Semantic prosody is a natural candidate for automated flagging in a future review pass.
- **Candidate prioritisation**. Words from the "evaluative adverb" cluster (やたら, ろくに, いちいち, せっかく, いかにも, とんでもない, くだらない, 下手に, 妙に, 変に, etc.) are high-value because they are where prosodic errors concentrate in L2 Japanese production. Some are already entered; the cluster would benefit from a coordinated consistency pass.

The phenomenon is subtle enough that notes about it are probably more valuable in *core* and *general* tier entries than in the *basic* tier, where simpler glosses predominate. It is not a reason to reopen the basic tier, but it is a reason to treat prosody-bearing adverbs and auxiliaries as first-class targets for note expansion.
