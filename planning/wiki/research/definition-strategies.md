# Definition and Gloss Strategies in Bilingual Learner Dictionaries

**Last updated**: 2026-04-07

## Overview

The central task of a bilingual dictionary is explaining what words mean — not through monolingual definitions (as in Merriam-Webster or the OED), but through equivalents, glosses, and explanatory paraphrases in the target language. This page surveys research on the types of meaning explanation available to bilingual lexicographers, practical techniques for writing effective glosses, and special challenges that arise in Japanese-English dictionaries.

This page complements [Translation Equivalence](translation-equivalence.md), which focuses on the theoretical problem of cross-linguistic non-equivalence. Here, the focus is on practical craft: what techniques produce glosses that genuinely help learners understand and use words.

## Types of meaning explanation

Adamska-Sałaciak (2010), building on Zgusta (1971), identified a typology of equivalents that is now widely used in lexicographic research. Each type serves a different purpose:

### Translational equivalent

A target-language word or phrase that can substitute for the source word in translation. The primary tool of traditional bilingual dictionaries.

- **Strength**: Concise, immediately useful for reading and writing
- **Weakness**: Can mislead when the semantic ranges of source and target don't align
- **Example**: 食べる → "to eat" (strong translational equivalent; close semantic match)

### Cognitive equivalent

The first word that springs to mind for a bilingual speaker hearing the source word — a kind of default association. Often identical to the translational equivalent, but not always.

- **Strength**: Captures the most psychologically salient mapping between languages
- **Example**: 先生 → "teacher" (the cognitive equivalent, even though sensei encompasses doctors, lawyers, politicians, etc.)

### Explanatory equivalent

A free target-language combination — essentially a mini-definition — that describes what the source word means without providing a direct translation.

- **Strength**: Handles culture-specific terms and lexical gaps; always available even when no translational equivalent exists
- **Weakness**: Cannot be used as a plug-in translation; longer and less convenient
- **Example**: 木漏れ日 → "sunlight filtering through tree leaves" (no single English word exists)

### Functional equivalent

A target-language expression that serves the same communicative function, even if the literal meaning differs.

- **Strength**: Helps learners understand pragmatic usage and appropriate contexts
- **Example**: お疲れ様です → "good work today" or "thanks for your effort" (functionally equivalent greetings, though the literal meaning is different)

### Mixed strategies

In practice, good bilingual dictionary entries combine these types. The most effective pattern is often:

1. **Translational equivalent** (for quick comprehension) + **explanatory gloss** (for accuracy) + **usage note** (for production)

## Degrees of equivalence

Zgusta (1971) identified three fundamental degrees of equivalence between source and target language items:

| Degree | Description | Lexicographic treatment |
|--------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Full equivalence** | Items match in designation, connotation, and range of application | Simple translational equivalent; rare outside technical terminology |
| **Partial equivalence** | Target covers only part of source's meaning, or vice versa | Multiple sense-specific equivalents with discriminating glosses |
| **Zero equivalence** | No established target-language equivalent exists | Explanatory paraphrase, cultural note, or functional equivalent |

Full equivalence is rare. Partial equivalence is the norm. The lexicographer's main challenge is making partial equivalence transparent to the user — showing which parts match and where the mapping breaks down.

## Techniques for effective gloss writing

### 1. Lead with the clearest equivalent

The first word or phrase in a definition should be the most useful single answer. Learners scanning an entry need to find the core meaning immediately.

- **Good**: "holiday, day off; rest" (for 休日)
- **Weak**: "a day on which one is free from work or school obligations" (buries the answer)

### 2. Use sense-specific discriminators

When a word has multiple senses, each needing different English equivalents, provide brief discriminating glosses that tell the user which sense is which.

- **Good**: "(of weather) clear; (of personality) cheerful; (of color) bright" (for 明るい)
- **Weak**: "clear, cheerful, bright" (no guidance on which English word fits which context)

### 3. Supplement equivalents with explanatory notes

For partial equivalents, a short note explaining the gap prevents misuse. This is especially important for words where the English equivalent has a broader or narrower range than the Japanese.

- **Example**: 先輩 → "senior, elder" — note: "Refers to someone who entered an organization, school, or group before you; implies respect and a mentoring relationship"

### 4. Handle zero equivalence honestly

When no English word captures the concept, avoid forcing a misleading equivalent. Use an explanatory phrase as the primary gloss, optionally followed by the nearest (imperfect) equivalent in quotes with a caveat.

- **Example**: 積ん読 → "buying books and leaving them unread" — sometimes rendered as "tsundoku" in English

### 5. Calibrate gloss length to word complexity

Simple, concrete nouns need short glosses. Abstract concepts, culture-specific terms, and function words need longer treatment. The length of the gloss should correlate with how much work the learner needs to do to understand the word, not with how important the word is.

### 6. Avoid translationese

Glosses should be natural English. Avoid calques or word-for-word translations that produce unnatural English phrases. If the natural English equivalent doesn't mirror the Japanese structure, that's fine — the examples and notes can show how the Japanese works.

### 7. Mark register in the gloss itself

When the English equivalent is register-neutral but the Japanese word is formal or casual, the gloss alone can mislead. Either choose a register-matched English equivalent or add a label.

- **Example**: 召し上がる → "(honorific) to eat, to drink" — not just "to eat"

## Sense ordering in polysemous entries

For words with multiple senses, the order in which senses appear affects usability. Research identifies several competing principles:

| Approach | Rationale | Best for |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| **Frequency-based** | Most common sense first | Receptive use (reading) |
| **Core/prototype first** | Central meaning first, extensions later | Cognitive clarity |
| **Concrete → abstract** | Literal meanings before metaphorical | Tracing semantic development |
| **Historical** | Earliest attested meaning first | Etymology-focused dictionaries |

For learner dictionaries, **frequency-based ordering** is generally preferred (Atkins & Rundell 2008), because learners encounter the most common sense first and need the quickest answer. However, when a word has a clear concrete core meaning that grounds the metaphorical extensions, leading with the prototype can be more pedagogically effective.

In bilingual dictionaries, sense division is also shaped by the target language: senses that require different target-language equivalents must be separated, even if they would be a single sense in a monolingual dictionary. For example, English "play" would need more sense divisions in a Japanese dictionary (遊ぶ, 弾く, 演じる, etc.) than in an English monolingual dictionary.

## Japanese-English specific challenges

### Anisomorphism at scale

Japanese and English are typologically distant, making full equivalence exceptionally rare. Areas of systematic mismatch include:

- **Politeness/register**: Japanese grammatically encodes social relationships that English handles through word choice and tone
- **Verb aspect**: ～ている encodes stative, progressive, and resultative meanings that English splits across different constructions
- **Giving/receiving**: The direction-relative system (あげる/くれる/もらう) has no structural equivalent
- **Counters**: Hundreds of classifiers with no English counterpart
- **Particles**: は, が, も, etc. carry meaning that English distributes across word order, stress, and articles

### Loanword false friends

Japanese contains many English-origin loanwords (和製英語 / wasei-eigo) whose meanings have shifted: マンション (apartment building, not mansion), ナイーブ (sensitive, not naive), スマート (slim/stylish, not intelligent). Glosses for these words must explicitly flag the semantic divergence.

### Formality tiers requiring multiple equivalents

A single English word like "eat" maps to multiple Japanese words across formality levels (食べる, 召し上がる, いただく, 食う). In the reverse direction (JE), entries must indicate which English equivalent is appropriate and why.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### Current practice aligns with research

je-dict-1 already uses several evidence-based strategies effectively:

1. **Concise translational equivalents** as the primary sense glosses — learners get a quick answer
2. **Explanatory notes** for culture-specific and zero-equivalence terms — the notes field handles what glosses alone cannot
3. **Sense-specific examples** — each numbered sense gets its own examples, preventing confusion
4. **Similar-word sections** — contrastive notes address partial equivalence between related Japanese words
5. **Register labels** — formality marking helps learners choose the right word

### Areas for continued attention

1. **Discriminating glosses for polysemous entries**: Some entries list multiple English equivalents without enough context to tell them apart. Adding brief parenthetical discriminators (domain, typical subject/object, register) would help.

2. **Loanword divergence notes**: Entries for wasei-eigo should routinely flag that the English-origin word has a different meaning in Japanese.

3. **Sense ordering consistency**: Ensuring that the most frequent or prototypical sense always comes first, across all entries, would improve lookup efficiency.

4. **Functional equivalents for pragmatic terms**: Greetings, set phrases, and discourse markers benefit from functional equivalents ("used when...") alongside literal translations.
