# Vocabulary Size and Text Coverage

**Last updated**: 2026-04-28

## Overview

How many words does a learner need to know? This deceptively simple question has driven decades of vocabulary acquisition research and directly shapes decisions about dictionary size, tier boundaries, and expansion priorities. The consensus answer — "it depends on the task, the text, and what counts as 'knowing'" — is precise enough to be useful. This page synthesizes the key findings on vocabulary size thresholds, text coverage models, and their implications for a Japanese-English learner dictionary at 25,500+ entries.

## The text coverage model

The foundational idea is straightforward: if a learner knows X% of the words in a text (the **lexical coverage**), how well can they comprehend it? Research has converged on two critical thresholds.

### The 95% and 98% thresholds

**Laufer (1989)** was the first to empirically link lexical coverage to reading comprehension. Studying 100 university students reading English academic texts, she found that comprehension improved markedly when learners knew at least 95% of the words — a finding she termed the **lexical threshold hypothesis**. Below 95%, readers lacked enough context to compensate for unknown words; above it, they could use reading strategies to infer the rest.

**Hu & Nation (2000)** refined this in "Unknown Vocabulary Density and Reading Comprehension," testing readers at 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% coverage levels on a 633-word narrative text. They proposed:

- **95% coverage** — minimally acceptable comprehension. The reader can follow the main ideas but misses nuance and may misinterpret some passages.
- **98% coverage** — adequate for unassisted comprehension. The reader encounters roughly one unknown word per 50 running words, which is manageable through contextual inference.

**Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe (2011)** partially challenged these thresholds, finding a more linear relationship between coverage and comprehension rather than a sharp step function. **Kremmel & Schmitt (2023)** replicated Hu & Nation's study with 104 Sri Lankan adult L2 learners and found the original results did not fully hold across populations — the 98% threshold was not a reliable guarantee of adequate comprehension for all groups, suggesting that other variables (background knowledge, reading skill, text type) also matter substantially. But even with these caveats, the 95–98% range remains the standard reference point in the field.

### What these thresholds mean in practice

At 98% coverage, a learner encounters approximately **1 unknown word per 50 words** — roughly one per short paragraph. This is low enough that context usually disambiguates the unknown word, and the reader can maintain the flow of comprehension.

At 95% coverage, the learner hits **1 unknown word per 20 words** — roughly one per sentence or two. This is survivable for a motivated reader but effortful, and comprehension accuracy drops measurably.

Below 90%, the density of unknowns overwhelms contextual inference, and reading becomes laborious guesswork rather than genuine comprehension.

## How many words for each threshold?

**Nation (2006)**, in the landmark paper "How Large a Vocabulary Is Needed for Reading and Listening?", analyzed the British National Corpus (BNC) to calculate how many **word families** are needed for different text types. (A word family = base form + inflected forms + regular derivations; e.g., *run, runs, running, runner* form one family.)

| Text type | 95% coverage | 98% coverage |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Novels | 4,000 WF | 9,000 WF |
| Newspapers | 4,000 WF + proper nouns | 8,000–9,000 WF + proper nouns |
| Spoken English (unscripted) | 3,000 WF + proper nouns | 6,000–7,000 WF |
| Children's movies | 4,000 WF | 6,000 WF |
| Graded readers | 2,000 WF | 3,000 WF |

Key observations:
- Spoken language requires fewer word families than written text at the same coverage level, because speech uses a narrower vocabulary range.
- **Proper nouns** contribute 1–6% of running text (higher in newspapers) and must be accounted for in coverage calculations — they are "free" coverage if the reader recognizes them.
- **Written text at 98% requires 8,000–9,000 word families** — this is the headline finding and the most-cited number in the field.

### The diminishing returns curve

The relationship between vocabulary size and text coverage follows a sharply diminishing-returns curve:

| Vocabulary size (word families) | Approximate text coverage (written English) |
|-------------------------------|---------------------------------------------|
| 1,000 | ~72–78% |
| 2,000 | ~80–85% |
| 3,000 | ~86–90% |
| 5,000 | ~92–95% |
| 8,000–9,000 | ~98% |
| 14,000+ | ~99%+ |

The first 2,000 word families cover roughly 80–85% of text — a massive return on investment. Each subsequent 1,000 families adds progressively less coverage. Moving from 90% to 98% coverage requires learning roughly **three to four times** as many words as getting to 90% in the first place. Webb & Nation (2017, *How Vocabulary is Learned*) call this the "vocabulary learning burden" — the effort per percentage point of coverage increases steeply at higher levels.

This curve has a direct implication for dictionary design: the words in a dictionary's first 2,000–3,000 entries are disproportionately valuable compared to the words in entries 10,000–15,000, which is why the basic and core tiers are pedagogically important even though they represent only ~11% of the dictionary's total entries.

## Vocabulary size measurement

### The Vocabulary Size Test (VST)

**Nation & Beglar (2007)** developed the Vocabulary Size Test, a 140-item multiple-choice test sampling 10 items from each of 14 frequency bands (1st–14th 1,000-word families of the BNC). A score of, say, 70/140 estimates a vocabulary size of 7,000 word families. **Beglar (2010)** validated the test using Rasch analysis, finding reliability of .96 — high enough for individual-level diagnostic use. The VST is now the most widely used receptive vocabulary size test in applied linguistics.

### The Vocabulary Levels Test (VLT)

An older instrument, the VLT (Nation 1983, revised 1990) tests knowledge at five frequency bands (2,000, 3,000, 5,000, academic, and 10,000 word families). It tells researchers whether learners have mastery of a given level but doesn't produce a continuous size estimate. Schmitt, Schmitt & Clapham (2001) validated a revised version. The VLT remains useful for diagnostic purposes (identifying which frequency band a learner needs to work on).

### Challenges in vocabulary size testing

- **Word family vs. lemma vs. type**: Different counting units yield different size estimates. A 9,000-word-family vocabulary corresponds to roughly 30,000–40,000 individual word forms.
- **Receptive vs. productive size**: Tests typically measure receptive (recognition) vocabulary. Productive vocabulary is smaller — Laufer (1998) found productive size at 50–80% of receptive size.
- **Depth vs. breadth**: Size tests measure breadth (how many words) but not depth (how well each word is known). A learner might "know" 8,000 word families receptively while having deep productive knowledge of only 3,000.

## Japanese-specific considerations

### Word counting challenges

The "word family" concept that underlies the English-language research does not map cleanly onto Japanese. Several factors complicate direct comparison:

1. **Sino-Japanese compounds**: Productive compounding with Sino-Japanese morphemes means that knowing 経済 (economy) and 学 (study/field) gives the learner a reasonable shot at guessing 経済学 (economics). The text coverage gained per "root" morpheme is potentially higher in Japanese than in English because of this combinatorial productivity.

2. **Script barriers**: Even when a learner "knows" a word aurally, they may not recognize it in kanji. Vocabulary size in Japanese must account for reading knowledge (kanji recognition), not just form-meaning connections.

3. **Multiple readings**: A single kanji may have several on'yomi and kun'yomi readings, each corresponding to different words. Counting vocabulary by kanji understates the word-learning burden.

4. **Inflectional complexity**: Japanese verb conjugation produces dozens of forms per lemma. Whether these forms count as separate vocabulary items or parts of one word family depends on the counting convention.

### JLPT as a rough vocabulary benchmark

The JLPT does not publish official vocabulary lists, but community-reconstructed estimates provide approximate benchmarks:

| JLPT level | Approximate vocabulary | Rough equivalent |
|------------|----------------------|------------------|
| N5 | ~800 words | Survival basics |
| N4 | ~1,500 words | Simple daily conversation |
| N3 | ~3,750 words | Everyday reading |
| N2 | ~6,000 words | Newspapers, business |
| N1 | ~10,000–12,000 words | Novels, academic texts |

These numbers are rough (there are no official lists, and different sources give different counts), but they provide a useful anchor. N1-level vocabulary (~10,000–12,000 words) is roughly comparable to the 8,000–9,000 English word families that Nation found necessary for 98% written text coverage — suggesting the cross-linguistic size requirements are in the same ballpark despite the different counting methods.

### Japanese text coverage studies

Direct text coverage studies for Japanese are less numerous than for English. The BCCWJ (Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese, ~104 million words) provides frequency data, but the field lacks a widely used Japanese equivalent of Nation's BNC-based word-family lists with coverage analysis. This gap means that precise "X word families for Y% coverage" numbers for Japanese text are less established than for English.

Notable work in this area:

- **Tono, Yamazaki & Maekawa (2013)**, *A Frequency Dictionary of Japanese* (Routledge), provides frequency-ranked vocabulary data based on a 100-million-word corpus spanning spoken, fiction, non-fiction, and news texts — the closest Japanese equivalent to Nation's BNC word-family lists.
- **Matsushita (2012)** examined optimal vocabulary learning orders for Japanese learners in a corpus-based PhD thesis at Victoria University of Wellington, developing frequency lists and a vocabulary size test specifically for Japanese reading.
- **Sato (2014)** found that the proportion of high-frequency words to tokens is lower in Japanese than in English, meaning **more vocabulary may be needed for equivalent text coverage**. Sato also found that vocabulary demands vary more by genre in Japanese than in English — the overlap between high-frequency words in easy vs. difficult text bands was smaller than expected.

The Sino-Japanese compound system and the concentration of high-frequency words in the first few thousand lemmas create a coverage curve that is broadly similar to English — a small core vocabulary yields disproportionate coverage, and the tail is long — but the Japanese curve may be somewhat less steep at the high-frequency end.

## How large should a learner dictionary be?

### Major dictionary sizes

For perspective, the major English learner dictionaries contain:

| Dictionary | Headwords | Other |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| OALD (10th ed.) | ~60,000 | 79,000 phrases, 89,000 meanings |
| LDOCE (6th ed.) | ~80,000 | ~2,000-word defining vocabulary |
| COBUILD (9th ed.) | ~40,000 | Full-sentence definitions |
| Cambridge Advanced | ~140,000 | Words, phrases, meanings |

These dictionaries vastly exceed the 8,000–9,000 word families needed for 98% coverage. Why? Because a dictionary must serve not just coverage needs but also:
- **Multiple senses**: One headword may cover many meanings
- **Specialization**: Users encounter domain-specific vocabulary unpredictably
- **Production support**: Learners need collocations, register guidance, and usage patterns for words they already know receptively
- **Reference completeness**: A dictionary that lacks a word the learner wants to look up loses the learner's trust

The lesson from major learner dictionaries is that **vocabulary size for a dictionary** is a different question from **vocabulary size for comprehension**. A learner might need 8,000 word families for reading, but the dictionary should contain 30,000–60,000+ entries to serve as a reliable reference when any of those 8,000 words — or rarer ones — need looking up.

### The "lookup disappointment" problem

When a learner encounters an unknown word and cannot find it in their dictionary, the cost is high — they learn that the dictionary is unreliable and stop using it. Bogaards (1996) called this the "coverage problem": a dictionary must include not just the words learners need most but also the long-tail words they occasionally encounter. Even if a word is in the 15th frequency band and covers only 0.01% of text, a learner who meets it in a novel needs to find it.

This is why major learner dictionaries aim for 40,000–80,000+ headwords despite the 8,000–9,000 word-family threshold for reading comprehension. The dictionary's role is to be there for every lookup, not just the frequent ones.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### Current size in context

With 25,500+ entries, je-dict-1 is well past the vocabulary size needed for 98% text coverage (roughly 8,000–9,000 English word families, or perhaps 10,000–12,000 Japanese lemmas). The dictionary's basic + core tiers (~2,800 entries) likely correspond to the first 2,000–3,000 word families that cover ~80–90% of text. The general tier extends coverage deep into the long tail.

However, direct comparison is complicated by counting differences: je-dict-1 counts entries (one entry per headword, including compound expressions), while the coverage research counts word families. A single word family can span multiple je-dict-1 entries (e.g., 教える, 教わる, 教育, 教師, 教室 are five entries but might be two or three word families). Conversely, one je-dict-1 entry can cover multiple senses that the VST would count as separate "known words."

### Tier alignment with coverage thresholds

The three-tier system maps loosely onto the coverage research:

| je-dict-1 tier | Size | Coverage analogue |
|---------------|------|-------------------|
| Basic | 801 | First 1,000 WF — survival vocabulary, ~72–78% coverage |
| Basic + Core | 2,783 | First 2,000–3,000 WF — ~80–90% coverage, Laufer's minimal threshold |
| + General (all) | 25,827 | Well beyond 98% written coverage — reference completeness |

This mapping reinforces the tier design: basic + core gives the learner enough vocabulary to read with struggle; the general tier progressively reduces the density of unknowns toward comfortable reading. The 25,000-30,000 entry target in the [growth plan](../ideas/dictionary-growth.md) for "Phase 1: learner completeness" aligns with the major English learner dictionaries' wordlists and should provide comfortable reference coverage.

### Expansion priority implications

The diminishing-returns curve argues for two simultaneous strategies:

1. **Fill gaps in common vocabulary first**: Any missing word in the first 5,000–8,000 frequency band represents a disproportionate coverage loss. The [semantic field audits](../ideas/corpus-prioritization.md) and [scenario gap analysis](../project/content-pipeline.md) serve this goal.

2. **Continue long-tail expansion for reference completeness**: Even words beyond the 10,000th frequency rank serve the "lookup disappointment" use case. Since the dictionary already covers common vocabulary well, ongoing expansion adds value primarily as reference depth, not as coverage.

### Self-containment and the coverage model

The [self-containment principle](../project/vocabulary-tiers.md) — that basic and core entries should use only vocabulary from their own tier or below — is the dictionary's internal expression of the coverage model. A learner who has mastered the basic tier (roughly the first 800 most essential words) can read any basic-tier entry without secondary lookups. This mirrors the CDV tradition (see [Controlled Defining Vocabulary](controlled-defining-vocabulary.md)) and the finding that 2,000–3,000 word families suffice for graded reader comprehension.

### Dictionary size vs. vocabulary size

The coverage research clarifies that je-dict-1's size (25,500+) is not "too large" for intermediate learners — a concern that might arise from the finding that only 8,000–9,000 word families are needed for reading. The dictionary's role is not to represent the vocabulary a learner should memorize but to be a reliable reference for any word they encounter. Major English learner dictionaries contain 5–10× more headwords than the 98% coverage threshold, and je-dict-1 follows the same pattern.
