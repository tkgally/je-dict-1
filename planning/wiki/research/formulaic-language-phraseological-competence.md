# Formulaic Language and Phraseological Competence

**Last updated**: 2026-05-25

## Overview

Formulaic language — prefabricated multi-word sequences stored and retrieved as wholes — constitutes a substantial proportion of everyday language use and plays a central role in both native speaker fluency and second language acquisition. This page surveys the psycholinguistic evidence for holistic storage and processing advantages, the relationship between formulaic competence and L2 fluency, how learners acquire formulaic sequences, Japanese-specific formulaic categories, and implications for dictionary design.

This page complements two related wiki pages: [Collocations in Learner Dictionaries](collocations.md) focuses specifically on habitual word combinations, while [Multiword Expressions](multiword-expressions.md) covers the broader taxonomy and dictionary placement problem. The present page focuses on the psycholinguistic and acquisitional dimension — why formulaic sequences matter for fluency, how they are processed and learned, and what dictionaries can do to support formulaic competence.

## Defining formulaic language

### Wray's definition

The most widely cited definition comes from **Wray (2002)**, *Formulaic Language and the Lexicon* (Cambridge University Press):

> "A sequence, continuous or discontinuous, of words or other elements, which is, or appears to be, prefabricated: that is, stored and retrieved whole from memory at the time of use, rather than being subject to generation or analysis by the language grammar."

This definition is deliberately broad: it encompasses idioms, collocations, fixed phrases, sentence stems, social routines, and any other multi-word unit that shows evidence of holistic storage. The key criterion is not semantic opacity (as with idioms) or statistical co-occurrence (as with collocations) but **prefabrication** — the sequence exists as a unit in the speaker's mental lexicon.

### Related terms

The literature uses many terms for overlapping phenomena:

| Term | Typical scope | Key source |
|------|--------------|------------|
| Formulaic sequence | Broadest umbrella term | Wray (2002) |
| Lexical phrase | Pragmatic function focus | Nattinger & DeCarrico (1992) |
| Prefab | Conventionalized combination | Erman & Warren (2000) |
| Lexicalized sentence stem | Clause-length units | Pawley & Syder (1983) |
| Lexical bundle | Corpus-extracted n-gram | Biber et al. (1999) |
| Collocation | Statistically associated pair | Sinclair (1991); Hoey (2005) |
| Multi-word expression (MWE) | Computational/lexicographic term | Sag et al. (2002) |
| Construction | Form-meaning pairing (any size) | Goldberg (1995, 2006) |

Wray (2002) catalogued over 40 distinct terms in the literature, reflecting the field's fragmented history across phraseology, psycholinguistics, corpus linguistics, and language teaching.

### Nattinger and DeCarrico's classification

**Nattinger & DeCarrico (1992)**, *Lexical Phrases and Language Teaching* (Oxford University Press), proposed a functional classification of "lexical phrases" based on their pragmatic roles:

- **Polywords**: short, fixed phrases functioning as single words (*by the way*, *so to speak*)
- **Institutionalized expressions**: sentence-length fixed forms, often proverbs or social formulas (*How do you do?*, *Once bitten, twice shy*)
- **Phrasal constraints**: phrases with open slots (*a __ ago*, *the __er, the __er*)
- **Sentence builders**: frames providing the skeleton of a sentence (*I think that X*, *not only X but also Y*)

This classification was influential in language pedagogy because it tied formulaic sequences to communicative functions rather than treating them as curiosities at the margins of grammar.

## The prevalence of formulaic language

### Erman and Warren's corpus estimate

**Erman & Warren (2000)**, "The Idiom Principle and the Open Choice Principle," *Text*, 20(1), 29–62, manually analyzed extracts of spoken and written English and estimated that **formulaic sequences ("prefabs") account for 58.6% of spoken discourse and 52.3% of written discourse**. Their definition of a prefab was "a combination of at least two words favored by native speakers in preference to an alternative combination which could have been equivalent had there been no conventionalization."

While this estimate has been debated (it depends heavily on where one draws the formulaic/novel boundary), even conservative estimates place the proportion at 25–30% of running text. The implication is that native speaker language production relies heavily on pre-assembled units rather than word-by-word composition.

### Pawley and Syder's two puzzles

**Pawley & Syder (1983)**, "Two puzzles for linguistic theory: nativelike selection and nativelike fluency," in Richards & Schmidt (eds.), *Language and Communication*, identified two capacities that linguistic theory had not explained:

1. **Nativelike selection**: native speakers routinely choose one phrasing over countless grammatically equivalent alternatives. Why "strong tea" and not "powerful tea"? Why "How do you do?" and not "How do you fare?" The selection puzzle is fundamentally a formulaic language problem — native speakers have stored preferred phrasings as "lexicalized sentence stems."

2. **Nativelike fluency**: speakers produce connected speech at rates that exceed what real-time word-by-word assembly could achieve (4–6 syllables per second in planning windows of about 8 words). Pawley and Syder argued that this is possible because much of speech is assembled from pre-stored multi-word chunks, reducing the computational load on working memory.

This paper has been one of the most cited in the formulaic language literature because it framed formulaic sequences not as a marginal curiosity but as a central mechanism of normal language use.

## Processing advantages

### Self-paced reading evidence

**Conklin & Schmitt (2008)**, "Formulaic sequences: Are they processed more quickly than nonformulaic language by native and nonnative speakers?" *Applied Linguistics*, 29(1), 72–89, used self-paced reading tasks and found that both native and non-native speakers read formulaic sequences significantly faster than matched novel phrases. This was one of the first studies to demonstrate empirically that the processing advantage of formulaic sequences extends to L2 speakers — not just L1 speakers who might be expected to have deeper lexical representations.

### Eye-tracking evidence

**Siyanova-Chanturia, Conklin, & Schmitt (2011)**, "Adding more fuel to the fire: An eye-tracking study of idiom processing by native and nonnative speakers," *Second Language Research*, 27(2), 251–272, used eye-tracking to examine fixation patterns during idiom reading. Native speakers showed shorter fixation times on formulaic sequences compared to novel phrases. Non-native speakers showed a processing advantage for figurative meaning only in supportive contexts, suggesting that L2 formulaic processing exists but is more context-dependent.

**Siyanova-Chanturia, Conklin, & van Heuven (2011)**, "Seeing a phrase 'time and again' matters: The role of phrasal frequency in the processing of multi-word sequences," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory and Cognition*, 37(3), 776–784, demonstrated that phrasal frequency (how often a specific multi-word combination occurs in a corpus) independently predicts processing speed, even after controlling for the frequencies of component words. Higher-frequency formulaic sequences are read faster.

### Frequency effects and chunking

**Ellis (2002)**, "Frequency effects in language processing: A review with implications for theories of implicit and explicit language acquisition," *Studies in Second Language Acquisition*, 24(2), 143–188, reviewed frequency effects across phonology, morphology, syntax, and formulaic language. Ellis argued that language acquisition is fundamentally a process of chunking — binding frequently co-occurring elements into stored units — and that formulaic sequences are the natural product of this process. The implication for L2 acquisition is that repeated exposure to sequences in context is the primary mechanism by which learners build formulaic competence.

**Ellis (2008)**, "Formulaic language in native and second language speakers: Psycholinguistics, corpus linguistics, and TESOL," *TESOL Quarterly*, 42(3), 375–396, further developed the frequency-based account, arguing that L1 and L2 formulaic knowledge is shaped by the same associative learning processes — tallying co-occurrence, building chunk strength through repetition — but that L2 learners have far less exposure and therefore weaker and sparser formulaic representations.

## Formulaic competence and L2 fluency

### Wood's fluency model

**Wood (2010)**, *Formulaic Language and Second Language Speech Fluency: Background, Evidence, and Classroom Applications* (Continuum), proposed that formulaic sequences are the cognitive substrate of oral fluency. Drawing on models of speech production (Levelt 1989), working memory, and automatization, Wood argued that if formulaic sequences are retrieved automatically, the speaker can devote processing resources to planning the next utterance rather than assembling the current one word by word. This dual benefit — faster retrieval of the current segment and freed capacity for forward planning — is what produces the temporal markers of fluency: higher speech rate, fewer pauses, fewer false starts, and longer runs between pauses.

### Boers et al.'s experimental evidence

**Boers, Eyckmans, Kappel, Stengers, & Demecheleer (2006)**, "Formulaic sequences and perceived oral proficiency: Putting a Lexical Approach to the test," *Language Teaching Research*, 10(3), 245–261, conducted a controlled experiment with 32 college students of English. Over 22 hours of instruction, the experimental group (N=17) was trained to notice formulaic sequences in authentic input, while the control group (N=15) received traditional grammar-lexis instruction. Results: the experimental group used more formulaic sequences in speech, and **increased use of formulaic sequences was positively correlated with perceived oral proficiency** as rated by native-speaker judges. This provided experimental support for the hypothesis that formulaic competence directly contributes to how proficient a learner sounds.

## L2 acquisition of formulaic sequences

### Developmental patterns

**Myles, Hooper, & Mitchell (1998)**, "Rote or rule? Exploring the role of formulaic language in classroom foreign language learning," *Language Learning*, 48(3), 323–364, tracked 16 child beginner learners of French over two years, examining how rote-memorized chunks were gradually broken down and reanalyzed. They found that learners initially used formulaic sequences as unanalyzed wholes (e.g., memorized question forms), then progressively extracted patterns and productive rules from them. This supported the hypothesis that formulaic sequences serve as a bridge between memorized language and creative grammar — a "stepping stone" model of acquisition.

### Pragmatic formulas

**Bardovi-Harlig (2009)**, "Conventional expressions as a pragmalinguistic resource: Recognition and production of conventional expressions in L2 pragmatics," *Language Learning*, 59(4), 755–795, investigated 122 ESL learners' recognition and production of conventional expressions (e.g., *Nice to meet you*, *I'm sorry for your loss*). Key findings: learners' recognition of formulas exceeded their production, and proficiency predicted both recognition and production — but production lagged substantially behind recognition at all levels. This recognition-production gap parallels the receptive-productive gap documented for single words (see [Productive Vocabulary and Encoding Support](productive-vocabulary-encoding.md)).

**Bardovi-Harlig (2012)**, "Formulas, routines, and conventional expressions in pragmatics research," *Annual Review of Applied Linguistics*, 32, 206–227, reviewed the field and argued that conventional expressions are a critical component of pragmatic competence, distinct from propositional pragmatic knowledge. Learners who know the grammar to construct a request or apology may still fail pragmatically if they do not command the conventionalized formulas that native speakers use.

### The under-representation problem

A consistent finding across L2 formulaic language research is that learners significantly under-use formulaic sequences compared to native speakers, even at advanced proficiency levels. Foster (2001) found that non-native speakers in oral academic discourse used substantially fewer formulaic sequences than native speakers matched for topic and context. This under-representation has multiple causes: insufficient input frequency (L2 learners simply encounter formulas less often), L1 interference (learners translate L1 formulas that don't match L2 conventions), and a grammar-focused pedagogical tradition that emphasizes rule-based composition over chunk learning.

## Japanese formulaic language categories

Japanese has several particularly well-defined categories of formulaic language, each with its own dictionary treatment challenges:

### 慣用句 (kanyōku) — idiomatic expressions

Verb-object idioms where the whole is not predictable from its parts: {腹|はら}を{割|わ}る (lit. "split one's belly" → speak frankly), {顔|かお}が{広|ひろ}い (lit. "face is wide" → well-connected). These typically have a literal and a figurative reading, and the learner needs to know which reading is primary in which context.

### 四字熟語 (yojijukugo) — four-character idioms

Compounds of exactly four kanji, often of Chinese origin, carrying idiomatic meaning: {一石二鳥|いっせきにちょう} (killing two birds with one stone), {七転八起|しちてんはっき} (perseverance through repeated failure). These are particularly opaque to L2 learners who cannot infer meaning from component kanji, and they are frequent in formal writing and speech.

### 挨拶表現 (aisatsu hyōgen) — greeting and ritual formulas

Fixed expressions tied to social situations: いただきます (before eating), {行|い}ってきます (when leaving home), お{疲|つか}れ{様|さま}です (acknowledging someone's work). These are among the most formulaic sequences in any language — they tolerate essentially no variation and their pragmatic function is entirely conventional. L2 learners must memorize them as wholes; no grammar rule predicts their form or use.

### 文型 (bunkei) — grammar patterns as formulas

Many Japanese grammar points function as formulaic frames with open slots: ～について (concerning), ～によると (according to), ～わけにはいかない (it's not that one can). These blur the line between vocabulary and grammar in ways that challenge both pedagogical grammars and dictionaries.

### Keigo formulas

Honorific language involves extensive formulaic sequences: お{忙|いそが}しいところ{恐|おそ}れ{入|い}りますが (a formulaic preamble to a request), ～させていただきます (a formulaic humble permission form), ご{確認|かくにん}のほどよろしくお{願|ねが}いいたします (formulaic business closure). These are highly conventionalized and any deviation signals non-native or inappropriate usage.

## Dictionary treatment of formulaic language

Dictionaries face several interrelated problems in handling formulaic sequences:

### Selection

Which formulas merit dictionary entries? Frequency alone is insufficient because some low-frequency formulas are essential for specific social situations (funeral condolences, business email closings). Pedagogical utility, cultural salience, and non-inferability (whether a learner could construct the formula from component knowledge) all play a role.

### Placement and findability

The traditional question of where to file a multi-word entry (see [Multiword Expressions](multiword-expressions.md) for the full analysis) is particularly acute for formulaic sequences because learners may not know which component to look up. Digital dictionaries solve this through search, but the entry still needs a primary headword.

### What information to provide

For formulaic sequences, the critical information is often pragmatic rather than semantic: when is this formula used, what social function does it serve, what register is it, and what are the consequences of using it in the wrong context? Standard dictionary microstructure (headword → definition → example) may not be the best format for ritual formulas whose "meaning" is entirely situational.

### Production support

Since the recognition-production gap for formulaic sequences is particularly large (Bardovi-Harlig 2009), dictionaries that aim to support production need to make formulas findable from the communicative situation rather than only from the form. A learner who wants to express condolences needs to reach お{悔|く}やみ{申|もう}し{上|あ}げます without already knowing its form.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### 1. Expression entries as formulaic language coverage

je-dict-1's "expression" POS tag (927 entries) already covers many formulaic sequences. These entries should be recognized as serving a distinct function from single-word entries — they are primarily about pragmatic and situational knowledge rather than compositional meaning. The notes field for expression entries should prioritize **when to use** and **register/formality** over etymological or grammatical analysis.

### 2. Aisatsu and ritual formulas as high-priority entries

Greeting and ritual formulas (いただきます, ごちそうさま, お{疲|つか}れ{様|さま}, よろしくお{願|ねが}いします, and their variants) are among the most important formulaic sequences for learners. These entries should include extensive situational guidance, register information, and examples of the social contexts that trigger each formula. Several recent entries (おかえりなさいませ, お{願|ねが}いいたします, こんばんは, ようこそ) address this need.

### 3. Collocation fields as formulaic knowledge

The collocation sections in je-dict-1's notes field serve exactly the function that formulaic language research recommends — they make conventionalized word combinations explicit and findable. The research on processing advantages (Conklin & Schmitt 2008) and fluency benefits (Wood 2010, Boers et al. 2006) provides empirical justification for the project's emphasis on collocation documentation.

### 4. Cross-references as formula networks

Formulaic sequences often exist in families: the keigo system has sets of related formulas (お～になる, ～ていただく, ～させていただく), greetings come in pairs (行ってきます ↔ 行ってらっしゃい), and many 慣用句 share structural patterns. Cross-references between related formulas help learners build the network knowledge that native speakers have.

### 5. The grammar-pattern boundary

Some of je-dict-1's expression entries are grammar patterns (なければならない, ～わけにはいかない) that function as formulaic frames. The research supports treating these as vocabulary items (with entries, examples, and notes) rather than relegating them to a separate grammar reference — Nattinger & DeCarrico (1992) specifically argued that "sentence builders" should be taught as lexical items, and the processing research confirms they are stored and retrieved like other formulaic sequences.

### 6. Production-oriented formula access

The recognition-production gap documented by Bardovi-Harlig (2009) suggests that learners need ways to find formulas from situations, not just from forms. je-dict-1's semantic tagging and scenario-based coverage analysis (see the [Learner Scenarios](../../build/data/learner_scenarios/) system) provide a framework for organizing formula access by communicative need. Ensuring that ritual and social formulas are tagged with relevant scenarios (greeting, apology, request, farewell, condolence) would improve production-oriented access.
