# Input Processing, Noticing, and Depth of Processing in L2 Vocabulary Acquisition

**Last updated**: 2026-06-05

## Overview

Three interconnected theoretical strands — Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis, the Levels of Processing framework, and models of lexical input processing — converge on a central question for dictionary design: what determines whether a word encountered in input actually gets learned? The answer, broadly, is *depth and quality of processing at the moment of encounter*. This page surveys the theoretical landscape, the empirical evidence, and the implications for how je-dict-1 structures its entries.

## Theoretical foundations

### Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis (1990–2010)

Richard Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis is the most influential account of how attention mediates language learning. In its original form (Schmidt 1990), the hypothesis made a strong claim: *what learners notice in input is what becomes intake for learning*. Intake — the subset of input that is actually processed and stored — requires conscious registration of form.

Schmidt (1990) distinguished two levels of awareness:

1. **Noticing** — conscious registration of the occurrence of a form in input ("I heard/saw that word"). This is awareness at the level of surface form.
2. **Understanding** — awareness at a deeper level, involving recognition of rules, patterns, or principles ("I see how this word relates to others I know").

The strong version held that noticing is *necessary* for acquisition. In 1994, Schmidt softened this: noticing is *facilitative* — it helps enormously, but some learning may occur without full conscious awareness. By 2001, the position stabilized: "People learn about the things they attend to and do not learn much about the things they do not attend to" (Schmidt 2001: 30).

Robinson (1995) extended this framework by connecting it to cognitive psychology's models of attention and memory. He argued that the attentional demands of pedagogical tasks, combined with individual differences in working memory capacity, determine the extent of noticing. His work established that those who exhibited "understanding" (deeper awareness) learned the most; those who noticed instances without generalizing learned next most; and there was minimal learning in the absence of noticing.

Leow (2000, 2015) provided extensive empirical support. His 2000 study of "aware vs. unaware" foreign language learners demonstrated facilitative effects of attention and awareness on subsequent processing, intake, and learning of targeted L2 forms. The overall body of evidence converges on Schmidt's core insight: attention is the gateway to acquisition.

### Tomlin and Villa's fine-grained attention model (1994)

Tomlin and Villa (1994) decomposed "attention" into three separable functions:

| Function | Definition | Example in vocabulary learning |
|----------|-----------|-------------------------------|
| **Alertness** | General readiness to receive input | A learner motivated to read, with interest in the topic |
| **Orientation** | Directing attentional resources toward a specific type of stimulus | A learner focusing on unknown words rather than skimming past them |
| **Detection** | Cognitive registration of a particular stimulus | Encoding the form-meaning pairing of a specific new word |

The key theoretical contribution was the claim that *detection* — not full noticing-with-awareness — is the minimal attentional event sufficient for learning. This creates a productive tension with Schmidt: Tomlin and Villa allow that some form registration can occur below the threshold of conscious awareness, while Schmidt requires at least low-level awareness (noticing) for effective learning.

For vocabulary acquisition, the practical resolution is that both frameworks predict the same instructional recommendation: *increase the likelihood that learners detect and attend to target forms*. The debate over whether detection without awareness constitutes "real" learning matters less than the shared conclusion that attention must be directed.

### Levels of Processing (Craik & Lockhart, 1972)

Craik and Lockhart's (1972) Levels of Processing framework, originally developed for general memory research, has become a cornerstone of vocabulary acquisition theory. The core claim: memory retention is a function of the *depth* of processing at encoding, not the duration of exposure or the number of repetitions.

The processing continuum runs from shallow to deep:

| Level | Processing type | Vocabulary example | Retention |
|-------|----------------|-------------------|-----------|
| Shallow | Orthographic/visual | Seeing a word's shape on the page | Fragile, rapid decay |
| Intermediate | Phonological | Hearing or pronouncing the word | Moderate |
| Deep | Semantic | Relating the word to known concepts, evaluating its meaning in context | Durable, resistant to decay |

The framework predicts that a learner who processes a word's meaning, evaluates it against known vocabulary, and generates associations will retain it far better than one who merely encounters the form — even with more exposures at the shallow level. This has been repeatedly confirmed in L2 vocabulary research (Hulstijn 2001; Laufer & Hulstijn 2001; Webb 2005).

However, the framework has an important nuance that Barcroft (2002) brought into sharp focus: depth of *semantic* processing is not always beneficial for *form* learning.

## Bridging theories: from attention to vocabulary tasks

### The Involvement Load Hypothesis (Laufer & Hulstijn, 2001)

The Involvement Load Hypothesis (ILH) operationalized the Levels of Processing framework specifically for vocabulary acquisition. Laufer and Hulstijn (2001) proposed that vocabulary retention is predicted by the "involvement load" of the learning task, composed of three factors:

| Factor | Type | Definition | Example |
|--------|------|-----------|---------|
| **Need** | Motivational | The drive to learn/use the word | Looking up a word to understand a passage (+) vs. encountering it passively (−) |
| **Search** | Cognitive | The effort to find the word's meaning or form | Consulting a dictionary (+) vs. reading a gloss already provided (−) |
| **Evaluation** | Cognitive | Comparing the word with other words, or deciding whether it fits a context | Choosing between near-synonyms in a composition (+) vs. receiving a single translation (−) |

Each factor contributes to retention: higher total involvement load predicts better learning. In the original 2001 study with EFL learners in two countries, retention was highest in a composition task (high need + high search + high evaluation), lower in reading plus fill-in (moderate), and lowest in reading comprehension alone (low involvement).

The ILH has direct implications for dictionary use: looking up a word in a dictionary scores positive on both **need** (the learner wants to know) and **search** (active effort to find the meaning). If the learner then evaluates the word — comparing it with near-synonyms, checking whether it fits the context, examining collocations — the evaluation component adds further processing depth.

### Barcroft's Lexical Input Processing and TOPRA model (2002–2015)

Joe Barcroft's research introduced an important corrective to the "deeper is always better" reading of Levels of Processing. His Type of Processing–Resource Allocation (TOPRA) model (Barcroft 2002, 2004, 2015) proposes that processing resources are *finite and competitive*: allocating resources to semantic processing comes at a cost to form processing, and vice versa.

In a key 2002 experiment, English-speaking learners of Spanish attempted to learn 24 new words under three conditions:

1. **+Semantic**: make pleasantness ratings about each word's referent
2. **+Structural**: count the letters in each word
3. **Control**: "do your best" (no specific elaboration)

Results were counterintuitive: for *Spanish word recall* (i.e., producing the L2 form), the +structural condition outperformed +semantic. The control condition (no elaboration) actually showed higher overall recall than either elaboration condition. Barcroft's interpretation: semantic elaboration, while it strengthens meaning connections, *inhibits* encoding of the novel word form during initial acquisition.

This finding does not contradict Levels of Processing — it refines it. For *new* vocabulary (where the form itself is unfamiliar), learners need to allocate substantial processing resources to form encoding. Excessive semantic elaboration at the initial stage can crowd out form processing. At later stages, once the form is partially established, semantic elaboration strengthens the meaning connection and aids long-term retention.

The pedagogical implication is a *staged model*: initial encounters should emphasize form-meaning mapping with limited elaboration; subsequent encounters can support deeper semantic processing (evaluation, comparison, contextual use). This staging aligns with Nation's (2001) four-strands model and with the general pattern of incremental vocabulary knowledge development documented in [Depth of Vocabulary Knowledge](depth-of-vocabulary-knowledge.md).

### Input Enhancement (Sharwood Smith, 1991, 1993)

Sharwood Smith coined the term "input enhancement" to replace the earlier "consciousness raising," emphasizing that external techniques cannot *guarantee* internal noticing — they can only increase its likelihood. Input enhancement encompasses any external manipulation of input to make target features more salient:

- **Typographic enhancement**: bolding, underlining, color coding, larger font
- **Input flooding**: increasing the frequency of target forms in the text
- **Glossing**: providing translations or definitions alongside target words
- **Textual modification**: simplifying surrounding text to reduce processing load on non-target elements

Research on textual enhancement shows mixed but generally positive effects. A meta-analysis of enhancement studies (Lee & Huang 2008) found that textual enhancement reliably draws attention to target forms but does not automatically produce acquisition. The gap between *noticing* and *learning* reflects the distinction between detection (Tomlin & Villa) and the deeper processing (evaluation, elaboration) needed for durable retention.

Crucially, the effectiveness of enhancement depends on internal factors: learner readiness, L1 knowledge, L2 proficiency, and prior familiarity with the target form. Enhancement works best when it tips a learner from "almost noticing" to "noticing" — it has diminishing returns for forms that learners would have noticed anyway or forms too far beyond their current level.

### VanPatten's Input Processing Theory (1993–2004)

VanPatten's Input Processing (IP) theory focuses on how learners extract both meaning and form from input under processing constraints. Two core principles are especially relevant to vocabulary:

1. **The Primacy of Meaning Principle**: Learners process input for meaning before they process it for form. Sub-principles include:
   - *Lexical Preference*: Learners rely on lexical items (content words) to get meaning, attending to grammatical markers only when lexical cues are insufficient.
   - *Preference for Non-redundancy*: Learners are more likely to process grammatical forms that carry meaning not available elsewhere in the sentence.

2. **The First Noun Principle**: Learners tend to process the first noun in a sentence as the agent — a parsing heuristic that can lead to miscomprehension in non-SVO structures (highly relevant for Japanese SOV).

For vocabulary acquisition, IP theory predicts that content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives) will be processed more readily than function words (particles, auxiliaries, conjunctions), because content words carry independent meaning. Function words that duplicate information already carried by content words or context (e.g., Japanese topic particle は when the topic is contextually obvious) will receive less processing attention.

## Empirical synthesis: what the evidence shows for vocabulary

The accumulated evidence across these theoretical streams supports several empirically robust conclusions:

### 1. Attention is necessary but not sufficient

Mere exposure — even repeated exposure — without attention produces negligible vocabulary learning (Hulstijn 2001; Laufer 2005). Eye-tracking studies confirm that learners who fixate longer on unknown words show better subsequent recall, but fixation duration alone does not predict learning unless accompanied by form-meaning processing (Godfroid, Boers & Housen 2013).

### 2. Quality of processing matters more than quantity of exposure

A single encounter with high involvement load (need + search + evaluation) can produce more durable learning than multiple shallow encounters (Laufer & Hulstijn 2001). This is the empirical basis for the common finding that dictionary consultation during reading produces better vocabulary retention than reading without a dictionary, despite the interruption cost — the "search" and "evaluation" components of dictionary use create deeper processing than contextual guessing alone (see [Incidental Vocabulary Acquisition Through Reading](incidental-vocabulary-reading.md)).

### 3. Form and meaning processing compete for resources

Barcroft's TOPRA model shows that initial vocabulary learning requires a balance of form processing (recognizing and encoding the word's phonological and orthographic shape) and meaning processing (connecting the form to a concept). Tasks that overload either dimension at the expense of the other produce suboptimal results. The practical implication: initial encounters should present form and meaning together clearly, without excessive elaboration that diverts attention from form encoding.

### 4. Staged processing is optimal

The research converges on a multi-stage model of vocabulary processing:

| Stage | Priority | Effective activities |
|-------|----------|---------------------|
| 1. Initial encounter | Form-meaning mapping | Clear gloss, pronunciation, basic context |
| 2. Consolidation | Deepening semantic connections | Collocations, examples in context, near-synonym comparison |
| 3. Productive knowledge | Evaluation and generation | Composition, translation, contextual use decisions |

This staging maps naturally onto the incremental knowledge dimensions documented by Nation (2001) and the VKS stages described in [Vocabulary Testing and Assessment](vocabulary-testing-assessment.md).

### 5. Enhancement has limits

Typographic enhancement (bolding, glossing, highlighting) reliably increases noticing but does not guarantee retention. The enhancement-to-learning gap is mediated by learner-internal factors (proficiency, motivation, working memory capacity) and by the quality of post-noticing processing. Enhancement is most effective as an *attention-directing* tool that increases the probability of deeper processing, not as a learning mechanism in its own right.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### 1. Entry structure supports staged processing

je-dict-1's entry structure maps remarkably well onto the staged processing model:

- **Headword + gloss**: Stage 1 (form-meaning mapping). The clear headword with furigana, followed by a concise English gloss, provides exactly the initial form-meaning pairing that attention research identifies as the foundation of vocabulary learning.
- **Example sentences** (progressive length): Stage 2 (consolidation). Short examples show the word in minimal context; longer examples embed it in richer contexts that require more processing. This progression naturally increases involvement load as the learner moves through examples.
- **Notes** (collocations, similar words, register): Stage 2–3 (evaluation). The structured notes sections demand evaluation — comparing near-synonyms, assessing register appropriateness, considering collocations — which is the highest-involvement processing.

### 2. Cross-references create distributed encounters

Each cross-reference is a potential re-encounter with a word in a new context. When a learner follows a link from 暑い to 熱い, they process both words at the evaluation level (comparing them). This distributed, evaluative processing is exactly what the ILH predicts will produce the most durable retention. The cross-reference network functions as a built-in spaced encounter system (see [Spaced Repetition and Dictionary Design](spaced-repetition-dictionary-design.md)).

### 3. Furigana serves dual attention functions

Furigana on all kanji serves two attention functions identified in the research:

- **Reduces orientation cost**: Learners don't need to redirect attention from meaning processing to form decoding (looking up an unknown kanji reading). This preserves processing resources for the deeper semantic engagement that produces learning.
- **Supports form-meaning mapping**: For words with kanji the learner hasn't fully acquired, furigana provides the phonological form alongside the orthographic form, creating a richer form encoding. This is especially important given Barcroft's finding that form processing needs dedicated resources — furigana reduces the *cost* of form processing without requiring the learner to divert resources from meaning.

### 4. Progressive examples implement involvement load staging

The requirement for examples to progress from simple to complex is directly supported by the processing literature. Short examples (Stage 1) minimize the processing load on surrounding context, letting the learner focus on the target word's basic form-meaning mapping. Longer examples (Stage 2–3) embed the word in richer contexts requiring evaluation — "does this word mean the same thing in this new context? How does the meaning shift?" This is evaluation-level processing, the highest involvement load factor.

### 5. Similar-words sections are high-involvement processing tools

The "similar words" and "near-synonym" sections in notes create the conditions for evaluation: the learner must compare two related forms and make fine-grained distinctions. This is precisely the kind of processing that both Levels of Processing and the ILH predict will produce the most durable and precise vocabulary knowledge. The research on near-synonym discrimination (see [Near-Synonym Discrimination](near-synonym-discrimination.md)) confirms that this evaluative comparison is one of the most difficult but highest-value processing activities for L2 learners.

### 6. Dictionary as attention-directing tool, not just information repository

The input processing literature suggests that a dictionary's primary pedagogical function is not just to *provide information* but to *direct attention*. Every design decision — what appears first, what is highlighted, how information is layered — affects where the learner allocates processing resources. This has concrete implications:

- **Gloss position and prominence**: The concise gloss appearing immediately after the headword ensures that form and meaning are processed together, supporting Barcroft's recommendation for initial form-meaning pairing without excessive elaboration.
- **Notes as optional depth**: The structure of notes (collapsible in the web interface, separated from the core gloss+examples) allows learners to self-regulate their processing depth. A learner at Stage 1 can stop at the gloss; a learner ready for evaluation can dig into similar-words comparisons.
- **Inline links as attention cues**: The ⟦...⟧ inline links in examples and notes function as a form of input enhancement — they signal "this word is worth noticing" within running text. This is analogous to typographic enhancement in reading research, directing orientation toward vocabulary items the learner might otherwise skip.

### 7. The particle processing gap

VanPatten's Lexical Preference sub-principle predicts that particles (function words carrying grammatical rather than lexical meaning) will receive less processing attention than content words. This aligns with the well-documented difficulty L2 learners have with Japanese particles (see [Japanese Particles in L2 Acquisition](japanese-particles-l2.md)). je-dict-1's particle entries — with their predicate lists, particle contrasts, and fixed patterns — are designed to counteract this attention deficit by making particle meanings explicit and evaluable, but the research suggests that learners will naturally allocate fewer resources to processing particle entries than content-word entries. Particle entries may need additional attention-directing features (examples that force particle evaluation, contrast tables that create evaluation involvement) to compensate.
