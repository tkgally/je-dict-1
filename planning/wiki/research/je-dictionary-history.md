# History of Japanese-English Dictionaries

**Last updated**: 2026-05-10

## Overview

The history of Japanese-English bilingual lexicography spans over 400 years, from Jesuit missionary word lists to massive collaborative open-source projects. Understanding this tradition illuminates what je-dict-1 inherits, what it diverges from, and where it sits in the larger arc of dictionary-making for Japanese learners.

## Precursors: European missionary dictionaries (1595–1603)

The earliest dictionaries connecting Japanese to a European language were compiled by Jesuit missionaries in Japan during the Nanban trade period.

**Nippo Jisho** (日葡辞書, *Vocabvlario da Lingoa de Iapam*, 1603). Compiled under the direction of João Rodrigues and published in Nagasaki, this Japanese-to-Portuguese dictionary contained 32,293 entries. It was the first dictionary of Japanese in any European language. Its purpose was practical: teaching missionaries spoken Japanese. The compilers carefully marked regional dialect, written versus spoken forms, women's and children's language, elegant and vulgar words, and Buddhist vocabulary — a remarkably sophisticated approach to register and usage marking that would not reappear in JE dictionaries for centuries. Only four copies of the original survive, held at the Bodleian Library (Oxford), the Bibliothèque nationale de France, the Public Library of Évora, and one in Japan.

An earlier trilingual Latin-Portuguese-Japanese dictionary was published in Amakusa in 1595, but the Nippo Jisho superseded it and became the reference standard.

These Portuguese works are significant for JE lexicography because they established the pattern of foreign scholars compiling dictionaries of Japanese for non-native use — a tradition that would continue through Dutch, English, and eventually collaborative international efforts.

## The Dutch interlude (1600s–1850s)

During Japan's period of national seclusion (鎖国, 1639–1853), the Dutch at Dejima were the only Europeans with access to Japan. Dutch-Japanese dictionaries and vocabularies were compiled during this period, primarily for the use of official interpreters (通詞). These works served as indirect bridges: when the first English-Japanese dictionaries appeared, they were often based on Dutch-Japanese sources.

**Doeff-Halma Dictionary** (ドゥーフ・ハルマ). The major Dutch-Japanese work of this period, compiled under the supervision of *opperhoofd* Hendrik Doeff, modeled on François Halma's Dutch-French dictionary (2nd ed., 1729). Doeff began compiling it privately and undertook full-scale work from 1816 at the bakufu's request. When Doeff returned to the Netherlands in 1817 he had completed entries from A through T; Dutch interpreters finished the remainder, completing the dictionary in 1833. This work proved foundational: early English-Japanese dictionaries drew heavily on Dutch-Japanese sources via dictionaries like this one.

**Walter H. Medhurst** — *An English and Japanese, and Japanese and English Vocabulary* (Batavia, 1830). This was the first English-Japanese bilingual wordbook, compiled by a British missionary who never visited Japan — he worked from Japanese books he obtained, leveraging his knowledge of Chinese characters and existing Dutch-Japanese materials. The work was rudimentary by later standards but established that an English-Japanese reference was possible and useful.

**Hori Tatsunosuke** — *Ei-Wa Taiyaku Shūchin Jisho* (英和対訳袖珍辞書, *A Pocket Dictionary of the English and Japanese Language*, 1862). Published by the Bansho Shirabesho (Institute for the Study of Barbarian Books), this was the first English-Japanese dictionary published in Japan. It was created by translating the Dutch entries of H. Picard's English-Dutch pocket dictionary into Japanese — it took an entire year to print just 200 copies. The chain of transmission is striking: Portuguese → Dutch → English, with each step mediated by existing bilingual resources.

## The Hepburn era (1867–1886)

**James Curtis Hepburn** (1815–1911), an American Presbyterian medical missionary, published the work that established modern JE lexicography.

**和英語林集成** (*Waei Gorin Shūsei* / *A Japanese and English Dictionary with an English and Japanese Index*, 1867). Published in Yokohama after nearly eight years of compilation, with the assistance of journalist and scholar Kishida Ginkō. Three editions appeared:

| Edition | Year | Key changes |
|---------|------|-------------|
| First | 1867 | ~20,000 entries; romanization used "szu" for す, etc. |
| Second | 1872 | Expanded entries; revised romanization |
| Third | 1886 | Codified "traditional Hepburn" romanization; most widely used edition |

Hepburn's contributions extend beyond the dictionary itself:

1. **Romanization system**. The Hepburn system (ヘボン式) became the dominant romanization standard for Japanese and remains so today. It emerged directly from dictionary-making: Hepburn needed a consistent way to represent Japanese pronunciation for English-speaking users.

2. **Direct fieldwork**. Unlike Medhurst, Hepburn compiled his dictionary from direct contact with Japanese speakers across social strata, making it a valuable record of Bakumatsu and early Meiji-era vocabulary.

3. **Template for the field**. Hepburn established the basic structure that JE dictionaries would follow: Japanese headword (in both script and romanization), part-of-speech indication, English gloss, and usage examples.

## The Brinkley transition (1896)

**Frank Brinkley** (1841–1912), a British journalist and scholar who lived in Japan for over 40 years, edited *An Unabridged Japanese-English Dictionary* (和英大辞典, Sanseido, 1896) with Nanjō Bunyū and Iwasaki Yukichika. At 1,687 pages, it adapted and substantially expanded Hepburn's work. Brinkley's dictionary replaced Hepburn's as the primary reference for English-speaking students of Japanese and remained in use as a guide to Meiji-era vocabulary well into the 20th century.

The Brinkley dictionary is notable as the first major JE dictionary published by a Japanese publisher (Sanseido), signaling the shift from foreign missionary projects to the Japanese publishing industry.

**Saito Hidesaburo** — *Saito's Idiomological English-Japanese Dictionary* (Obunsha, 1915). This pioneering work focused on collocations and word combinations; Saito wrote in the preface: "Words are nothing in themselves, and everything in combination." His emphasis on idiomatic patterns influenced Harold E. Palmer's vocabulary research and, through Palmer, modern corpus-based learner lexicography.

## The Kenkyusha era (1918–present)

Kenkyūsha (研究社), founded in 1907 by Goichiro Kosakai, became the dominant publisher of JE and EJ dictionaries through the 20th century. Two parallel dictionary lines define their contribution:

### New Japanese-English Dictionary (新和英大辞典)

| Edition | Year | Editor-in-chief | Notes |
|---------|------|-----------------|-------|
| 1st | 1918 | Takenobu Yoshitarō | First comprehensive modern JE dictionary; known as "Takenobu's" |
| 2nd | 1931 | (revised) | George Sansom as major contributor; renamed "New" JE Dictionary |
| 3rd | 1954 | (revised) | Post-war revision |
| 4th | 1974 | Masuda Koh | 80,000 headwords, 100,000 compounds, 50,000 examples |
| 5th | 2003 | (revised) | ~480,000 entries total (130,000 headwords, 100,000 compounds, 250,000 examples); ~3,000 pages; known as the "Green Goddess" (GG) |

The first edition (1918), completed in under five years by leading scholars, was the most authoritative JE dictionary of its time and established Kenkyūsha's reputation. The "Green Goddess" nickname for the fifth edition (from its dark-green cover) reflects the dictionary's status as the standard professional reference for Japanese-to-English translation.

**Wartime significance**: During World War II, institutions including Harvard University's Department of Far Eastern Languages produced pirated editions of Kenkyusha's dictionary for military use — a testament to its irreplaceability.

### New English-Japanese Dictionary (新英和大辞典)

| Edition | Year | Editor-in-chief |
|---------|------|-----------------|
| 1st | 1927 | Okakura Yoshisaburō |
| 5th | 1980 | Koine Yoshio |
| 6th | 2002 | Takebayashi Shigeru |

This EJ counterpart served Japanese learners of English and translators. Together, the two "New" dictionaries formed the backbone of professional JE/EJ translation work for most of the 20th century.

### The OALD's Japanese origins

A. S. Hornby went to Japan in 1924 to teach English. He joined Harold E. Palmer's vocabulary research program and by 1933 became editor of the Institute for Research in English Teaching's *Bulletin*. In 1937, Hornby began work with E. V. Gatenby and H. Wakefield on a new type of dictionary for foreign learners. The result, the **Idiomatic and Syntactic English Dictionary (ISED)**, was published by Kaitakusha in Tokyo in 1942 — during wartime, so Hornby himself did not see the published dictionary until 1948. Oxford University Press reprinted it in 1948 as *A Learner's Dictionary of Current English*, later renamed the **Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary (OALD)**. The fact that the world's most influential English learner's dictionary was born in Japan underscores the deep connection between Japanese English education and modern learner lexicography.

### Other major post-war publishers

**Sanseido** (三省堂, founded 1881) focused on practical, smaller dictionaries in contrast to Kenkyusha's comprehensive approach. Their *Concise English-Japanese Dictionary* was widely used from the late 1800s through mid-1900s. In monolingual lexicography, the *Sanseido Kokugo Jiten* was innovative for its emphasis on contemporary usage, with chief editor Kenbo Hidetoshi collecting word usages from newspapers and broadcasts on cards — anticipating corpus-based methods.

**Shogakukan** published the **Progressive English-Japanese Dictionary** (1980, editors Konishi Tomoshichi, Minoru Yasui, Tetsuya Kunihiro), which continued through multiple editions. The companion **Progressive Japanese-English Dictionary** also became a standard reference.

**Taishukan** published the **Genius English-Japanese Dictionary** (ジーニアス英和辞典, 1988–89, editors Konishi Tomoshichi and Minamide Kosei), which became "the most famous English dictionary for Japanese" and was adopted in most Japanese schools. The *Unabridged Genius* (2001) contains 255,000 headwords.

## Character dictionaries

A distinct tradition developed around kanji reference works:

**Andrew N. Nelson** — *The Modern Reader's Japanese-English Character Dictionary* (Tuttle, 1962). Nelson, a Seventh-Day Adventist missionary who spent over 40 years in Asia, created the standard English-language kanji reference. It won the 1969 Prize for Promotion of International Cultural Relations. The "New Nelson" revision by John H. Haig added over 1,200 characters and 2,000 new readings. Nelson's radical-based lookup system became the default method for English-speaking students to find unfamiliar kanji — a problem that would later drive innovation in electronic dictionaries.

**Jack Halpern** — *The Kodansha Kanji Learner's Dictionary* (1999) and the later *Kanji Dictionary* (2013). Halpern introduced the SKIP (System of Kanji Indexing by Patterns) lookup method and the "core meaning" concept, which assigns a central semantic value to each kanji to help learners predict compound meanings. His work represents the kanji dictionary tradition's pivot toward learner-centered design.

## The digital revolution (1990s–present)

### EDICT, JMdict, and the open-source model

**Jim Breen**, an Australian computer scientist at Monash University, transformed JE lexicography by applying open-source principles to dictionary-making.

| Project | Year | Format | Size |
|---------|------|--------|------|
| EDICT | 1991 | Plain text (EUC-JP) | ~60,000 entries by 1999 |
| JMdict | 1999 | XML (UTF-8) | 214,000+ entries (as of 2023) |
| KANJIDIC | 1991 | Plain text → XML | Kanji character database |
| WWWJDIC | mid-1990s | Web interface | 130,000+ queries/day (2018) |

EDICT began in 1991 as a simple flat-file dictionary. By 1999, with ~60,000 entries accumulated through community contributions, Breen migrated to JMdict — a structured XML format supporting multilingual translations, part-of-speech tags, and usage annotations. Since 2000, the project has been managed by the Electronic Dictionary Research and Development Group (EDRDG) at Monash University.

The JMdict/EDICT project's significance for lexicography:

1. **Crowdsourced compilation**. Unlike all previous JE dictionaries (compiled by individuals or small editorial teams), JMdict grows through community contributions — a model that has produced the largest JE dictionary ever created.
2. **Open licensing**. Released under Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike, JMdict data underlies virtually every free JE dictionary application, including Jisho.org, Aedict, Takoboto, and dozens of others.
3. **Structured data**. The XML format enabled programmatic access, spawning an ecosystem of dictionary applications that could reuse, filter, and present the same data in different ways.
4. **Continuous revision**. Unlike print dictionaries with discrete editions separated by years or decades, JMdict is updated continuously.

### Handheld electronic dictionaries (電子辞書)

From the late 1990s through the 2010s, dedicated handheld electronic dictionaries (denshi jisho) dominated the Japanese language learning market. Casio's Ex-Word line and Sharp's Brain series were the market leaders.

Key features that distinguished electronic dictionaries from print:
- Multiple dictionaries in one device (a typical unit bundled 100+ reference works)
- Handwriting recognition for kanji input
- Jump-search between dictionaries (look up a word in the definition)
- Audio pronunciation

These devices typically bundled commercial dictionary content (Kenkyusha, Sanseido, Kōjien, etc.) that was unavailable online, making them the only portable way to access professional-grade dictionaries. The market has since contracted sharply as smartphones absorbed this function, though devices continue to be used in Japanese schools and by serious students.

### Browser extensions

The browser extension paradigm brought dictionary lookup directly into the reading workflow. **Rikaichan** (Firefox) pioneered popup dictionary lookup by hovering over Japanese text. **Yomichan** succeeded it with enhanced features including customizable dictionary sources, EPWING support, and Anki integration. When Yomichan was abandoned by its developer in 2023, the community forked it as **Yomitan** and **Rikaitan**. These tools draw on JMdict, KANJIDIC, and other dictionary files, representing a new mode of dictionary use: integrated into reading rather than consulted separately.

### Modern online dictionaries

The current landscape reflects the convergence of open-source data, commercial content, and web/mobile platforms:

- **Jisho.org** (Kim Ahlstrom, Miwa Ahlstrom, Andrew Plummer): Clean, modern interface built on JMdict data; the most popular free English-language JE dictionary online
- **Weblio** (beta 2005, launched 2006): Aggregator operated by GRAS Group, Inc., searching across 665+ dictionaries simultaneously including Kenkyusha's New Japanese-English Dictionary; English-Japanese service includes 4.16 million English words
- **Sanseido Wisdom** (ウィズダム, 2003): Modern EJ/JE dictionary available in both print and electronic formats; represents Sanseido's current flagship
- **dictionary.goo.ne.jp / Kotobank**: Japanese portal sites aggregating multiple dictionary sources
- **Smartphone apps**: Imiwa, Japanese (by Renzo), Takoboto, and others — most built on JMdict data

## Themes and patterns across the tradition

### From glossary to learner's dictionary

The earliest JE dictionaries (Medhurst, Hori) were essentially bilingual word lists. Hepburn added usage context. Kenkyusha added extensive examples and compound words. Modern learner-focused works (including je-dict-1) add structured notes, cross-references, collocations, and register information. This trajectory mirrors the English learner lexicography tradition (OALD 1942 → LDOCE 1978 → COBUILD 1987) — and the connection is more than parallel: the OALD itself was born in Japan, and Saito's 1915 collocation-focused approach anticipated Palmer's work that fed directly into modern corpus-based learner lexicography.

### The compiler question

| Era | Compiler type | Examples |
|-----|--------------|----------|
| 1600s–1800s | Foreign missionaries/scholars | Rodrigues, Medhurst, Hepburn |
| 1890s–1940s | Japanese publishers + foreign advisors | Kenkyusha + Sansom, Brinkley |
| 1960s–1990s | Individual scholars | Nelson, Halpern |
| 1990s–present | Community/open-source | Breen/JMdict |
| 2020s–present | LLM-assisted | je-dict-1 |

Each shift in compiler type brought trade-offs: individual scholars brought deep expertise but limited coverage; open-source projects brought scale but inconsistent quality; LLM-assisted compilation brings speed and consistency but requires verification infrastructure.

### The kanji lookup problem

A persistent challenge unique to JE (vs. English-French, English-Spanish, etc.) lexicography is how users find entries when they cannot read the headword. Solutions evolved from radical-based lookup (traditional) → Nelson's Universal Radical Index → SKIP patterns → multi-radical search (WWWJDIC, 24.8% of lookups per Breen 2004) → handwriting recognition → copy-paste from digital text → OCR from camera. Each innovation made dictionaries more accessible to learners who could not yet identify radicals reliably.

### Print vs. digital content asymmetry

A notable feature of the current landscape is that the highest-quality JE dictionary content (Kenkyusha 5th edition, Sanseido Wisdom) remains behind commercial licensing, while the largest and most accessible dictionary (JMdict) was compiled through open-source contribution without professional editorial oversight. No single resource combines Kenkyusha's editorial depth with JMdict's scale and openness. This gap is part of what motivated je-dict-1's approach: building a high-quality, structured, open dictionary with LLM-assisted compilation.

## Implications for je-dict-1

1. **Continuity with tradition**. je-dict-1's basic structure — Japanese headword with reading, English gloss, examples, usage notes — follows the template established by Hepburn in 1867 and refined by Kenkyusha over the 20th century. The innovation is not in the entry format but in the compilation method (LLM-assisted), the structured metadata (typed cross-references, vocabulary tiers, conjugation tables), and the distribution model (open-source static site).

2. **Filling the quality-accessibility gap**. je-dict-1 occupies a space between JMdict (massive, open, but gloss-only) and Kenkyusha (deep, expert-edited, but proprietary). With 27,200+ entries containing structured notes, 108,000+ examples, and 15,600+ typed cross-references, it offers editorial depth that JMdict lacks while remaining freely accessible.

3. **Register marking as unfinished business**. The Nippo Jisho (1603) marked register, dialect, and social variation with notable care. Four centuries later, systematic register marking remains a challenge for JE dictionaries — including je-dict-1, where only ~35% of entries have register-relevant information in their notes. The tradition shows that this has always been recognized as important but never fully solved.

4. **The lookup problem is solved differently**. Historic JE dictionaries struggled with how non-readers find entries. je-dict-1 sidesteps this entirely through full-text search, furigana on all kanji, and cross-reference networks — digital-native solutions that make the traditional radical-lookup problem irrelevant.

5. **Scale trajectory**. Hepburn: ~20,000 entries. Kenkyusha 5th: 130,000 headwords. JMdict: 214,000+ entries. je-dict-1's 27,200+ entries are modest by headword count, but its per-entry depth (averaging 4.0 examples, structured notes, cross-references) places it closer to the Kenkyusha tradition of rich entries than to JMdict's breadth-first approach.
