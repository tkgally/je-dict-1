# Keigo: Honorific Language Acquisition and Dictionary Treatment

**Last updated**: 2026-05-25

## Overview

Keigo (敬語, "respectful language") is the Japanese honorific system — a grammaticalized apparatus for encoding social relationships, deference, and in-group/out-group distinctions into speech. For L2 learners, keigo is one of the most persistent acquisition challenges: learners may know the grammatical forms but still produce socially inappropriate utterances, because keigo competence requires pragmatic judgment that no amount of rule-learning can substitute for. For dictionary makers, keigo poses the question of how to represent a system that is simultaneously grammatical (verb morphology, suppletive forms), lexical (dedicated honorific vocabulary), and pragmatic (context-dependent social calculus). This page surveys the keigo system's structure, what research says about L2 acquisition, how dictionaries have handled honorifics, and what the findings imply for je-dict-1.

## The keigo system

### Traditional three-category model

The standard classification taught in Japanese education since the postwar period divides keigo into three types:

| Category | Japanese | Function | Mechanism | Example |
|----------|---------|----------|-----------|---------|
| **Sonkeigo** (尊敬語) | Respectful language | Elevates the referent's actions | Suppletive verbs, お〜になる pattern, passive-form honorifics | いらっしゃる (for いる/行く/来る), 召し上がる (for 食べる/飲む) |
| **Kenjōgo** (謙譲語) | Humble language | Lowers the speaker's actions relative to the referent | Suppletive verbs, お〜する pattern | 参る (for 行く/来る), 拝見する (for 見る), いたす (for する) |
| **Teineigo** (丁寧語) | Polite language | Marks general politeness toward the addressee | Sentence-final です/ます | 行きます (for 行く), きれいです (for きれいだ) |

This three-way division, while pedagogically convenient, conflates several distinct functional dimensions.

### 2007 five-category reclassification

In February 2007, the Japanese Agency for Cultural Affairs (文化庁) issued *Keigo no Shishin* (敬語の指針, "Guidelines for Keigo"), which reclassified the system into five categories. The key innovation was splitting kenjōgo into two functionally distinct types and extracting bikago from teineigo:

| Category | Japanese | Function | Distinguished from |
|----------|---------|----------|--------------------|
| **Sonkeigo** (尊敬語) | Respectful language | Elevates the referent | (Unchanged) |
| **Kenjōgo I** (謙譲語I) | Humble language (toward recipient) | Lowers the speaker's action *toward a specific beneficiary* | Directionality toward a person |
| **Teijōgo / Kenjōgo II** (丁重語/謙譲語II) | Courteous language | Lowers the speaker's action *as a general speech-level marker*, without a specific beneficiary | No directionality — pure speech-level |
| **Teineigo** (丁寧語) | Polite language | です/ます sentence endings | (Unchanged) |
| **Bikago** (美化語) | Refined language | お/ご prefixes that soften or beautify words without expressing deference | No honorific directionality — aesthetic |

The distinction between Kenjōgo I (参る used toward a superior, e.g., "I will come to your office") and Teijōgo (参る as a general formal speech-level marker, e.g., "I will go" in a public speech) resolves a long-standing pedagogical confusion. The extraction of bikago recognizes that お水 ("water" with beautifying prefix) is not honorific toward anyone — it is a marker of refined speech register.

### Structural mechanisms

Keigo operates through several distinct linguistic mechanisms:

**Suppletive forms**: Certain high-frequency verbs have dedicated honorific and humble replacements that are unrelated morphologically to the base verb. These are the forms that must be listed in a dictionary as separate entries:

| Base verb | Sonkeigo | Kenjōgo |
|-----------|----------|---------|
| いる (to be) | いらっしゃる | おる |
| 行く (to go) | いらっしゃる | 参る |
| 来る (to come) | いらっしゃる | 参る |
| する (to do) | なさる | いたす |
| 食べる (to eat) | 召し上がる | いただく |
| 飲む (to drink) | 召し上がる | いただく |
| 言う (to say) | おっしゃる | 申す / 申し上げる |
| 見る (to see) | ご覧になる | 拝見する |
| 知る (to know) | ご存じ | 存じる / 存じ上げる |
| くれる (to give) | くださる | — |
| あげる (to give) | — | 差し上げる |

**Regular patterns**: Most verbs can be elevated or humbled through productive grammatical patterns:
- Sonkeigo: お〜になる (お読みになる), passive form as honorific (読まれる), お〜くださる
- Kenjōgo: お〜する (お持ちする), お〜いたす (お持ちいたす), お〜申し上げる

**Nominal honorifics**: お/ご prefixes (お名前, ご住所), honorific suffixes (〜様, 〜先生), humble self-reference (わたくし, 小生), and in-group/out-group nouns (家内 vs. 奥様 for "wife").

**Double keigo and excessive politeness**: Native speakers themselves sometimes produce "double keigo" (二重敬語) — forms like お召し上がりになる that stack two honorific mechanisms — which prescriptive authorities consider errors but are common in service-industry Japanese (接客敬語).

## L2 acquisition of keigo

### The pragmatic competence gap

Research consistently shows that keigo is one of the last aspects of Japanese that L2 learners acquire, and that grammatical knowledge of keigo forms substantially outpaces the ability to use them appropriately. This mirrors the broader pragmatic competence gap documented by Bardovi-Harlig (1998, 2013) and Thomas (1983) for other languages: learners develop linguistic competence before pragmatic competence.

Cook (2008, *Socializing Identities through Speech Style*) showed that learners of Japanese as a foreign language (JFL) construct social identities partly through their speech style choices, including keigo. Her longitudinal data demonstrated that learners' uptake of polite forms is not simply a matter of learning grammar but of being socialized into the norms of situated language use — a process that requires extended interaction with native speakers in authentic social contexts.

### Common error patterns

Research on keigo errors by L2 learners identifies several recurring patterns:

**1. Avoidance**: Learners who feel unsure about keigo forms default to です/ます (teineigo) in all situations, avoiding sonkeigo and kenjōgo entirely. This produces speech that is polite but socially flat — appropriate for a tourist but inadequate for workplace or academic contexts.

**2. Sonkeigo/kenjōgo confusion**: The most common structural error is reversing the direction of honorification — using humble forms (kenjōgo) for someone else's actions or respectful forms (sonkeigo) for one's own. For example, using 参る (humble "go") when describing a superior's movement, or いらっしゃる (respectful "be") when describing one's own presence.

**3. Uchi/soto miscalculation**: Learners struggle with the in-group/out-group (内/外) principle, which requires a speaker to humble their own in-group members when speaking to outsiders. The classic example: when a customer calls a company and asks for the president, the receptionist uses humble forms for the president (自分の社長は今、席を外しております) because the president is uchi relative to the outside caller. This deeply counterintuitive pattern — humbling one's own boss — is particularly resistant to instruction.

**4. Hypercorrection and over-formality**: Some learners, having been taught that keigo is important, apply maximum formality uniformly. This produces awkward speech among peers or in casual settings — using 召し上がりますか rather than 食べる？ when offering food to a friend.

**5. Register mixing**: Learners may combine keigo vocabulary with plain-form grammar, or use teineigo endings with casual vocabulary, producing internally inconsistent utterances.

### L1 background effects

**Korean-speaking learners** have an initial advantage because Korean has a grammaticalized honorific system (존댓말/반말) with structural parallels to Japanese keigo — subject-honorific verb marking, humble forms, and addressee-level sentence endings. However, the systems differ in detail: Korean honorifics are more systematically triggered by age-based hierarchy, while Japanese keigo is more sensitive to situational uchi/soto dynamics. Korean learners may transfer their L1 honorific habits, which works well in many cases but can lead to errors in specifically Japanese social configurations.

**English-speaking learners** lack a grammaticalized honorific system entirely. English registers politeness primarily through lexical choice, indirectness, and hedging ("Would you mind..." rather than a dedicated verb form). For these learners, the very concept that a verb's form changes based on social relationships is foreign, and keigo represents a new dimension of grammatical complexity with no L1 scaffold.

**Chinese-speaking learners** fall between these extremes. Classical Chinese had honorific vocabulary (similar to Japanese suppletive forms like 召し上がる), and modern Chinese retains some lexical politeness (您 for "you"), but lacks the systematic grammaticalization of Japanese keigo. Chinese learners can leverage kanji knowledge for recognizing keigo compounds (e.g., 拝見, 拝借) but still struggle with the productive grammatical patterns and social calculus.

### Instructional challenges

Keigo instruction in JSL/JFL programs typically follows a sequence: teineigo (です/ます) is introduced early (JLPT N5), while sonkeigo and kenjōgo are typically delayed until intermediate or advanced levels (N3–N1). The JLPT tests keigo comprehension and production at the N3 level and above.

Takeuchi (2021, "Language ideologies among Japanese foreign language teachers") found that teachers' own ideologies about keigo affect instruction: some teachers hold different standards for L2 speakers than for native speakers, conveying implicit native-speaker bias that can either excuse or overcorrect learner errors.

The fundamental teaching challenge is that keigo competence cannot be reduced to a set of rules. As Ide (1989) argued, Japanese politeness operates through *wakimae* (discernment) — the "almost automatic observation of socially-agreed-upon rules" — rather than through the strategic, volitional politeness described by Brown and Levinson's (1987) universal politeness model. Wakimae-driven politeness is sociopragmatically obligatory: in certain contexts, omitting keigo is not merely impolite but socially unintelligible. This obligatory nature makes keigo acquisition particularly challenging for learners from cultures where politeness is primarily strategic.

## Uchi/soto and the social calculus

The uchi/soto (内/外, in-group/out-group) distinction is the organizational principle underlying keigo use. Understanding this system is essential for using keigo correctly and equally essential for dictionary treatment.

**Basic principle**: A speaker uses sonkeigo for out-group (soto) members' actions and kenjōgo for in-group (uchi) members' actions — including their own boss's actions when speaking to an outsider.

**Dynamic boundaries**: Uchi/soto is not fixed — the same person can shift from uchi to soto depending on the conversational context. A department head is soto when a subordinate speaks to them directly but becomes uchi when that subordinate speaks about them to an external client. This dynamic recalculation is the source of many learner errors.

**Concentric circles model**: The uchi/soto distinction operates in concentric circles: self → family → department → company → industry → Japanese society. Each boundary can trigger different keigo choices depending on who the interlocutor is.

## Dictionary treatment of keigo

### How major dictionaries handle keigo

**JMdict/EDICT**: Uses entity codes for keigo: `hon` (honorific or respectful language), `hum` (humble language), `pol` (polite language). These are applied to individual entries as tags. For example, いらっしゃる is tagged `hon`, 参る is tagged `hum`. This approach is taxonomically correct but provides no guidance on *when* or *with whom* to use the forms — it labels the form without explaining the social calculus.

**Kenkyusha's New Japanese-English Dictionary**: Includes keigo forms under the base verb entries and also as separate headwords. Cross-references connect base forms to their honorific/humble equivalents. Usage notes in some entries explain the social contexts. However, the coverage is inconsistent — major suppletive pairs are well-documented, but the productive patterns (お〜になる, お〜する) receive less attention.

**Wisdom Japanese-English Dictionary (Sanseidō)**: Provides "communication boxes" that group keigo forms by function, showing the full set of options for expressing a given speech act at different politeness levels. This is closer to the production-oriented approach that learner dictionaries ideally provide.

**Monolingual dictionaries (Kōjien, Daijirin, etc.)**: These provide detailed etymological and grammatical information about keigo forms but are not designed for production. They mark words with 敬語 labels but assume native-speaker pragmatic competence.

### Shortcomings of current dictionary treatment

1. **Isolation of forms**: Dictionaries list suppletive keigo forms as separate entries but don't systematically connect them to base forms or to each other. A learner looking up 食べる may not discover 召し上がる or いただく unless those cross-references happen to be present.

2. **Missing uchi/soto guidance**: No major dictionary explains the uchi/soto calculus in individual entries. The information that 参る should be used for one's own boss's movements when speaking to an outsider is found in grammar textbooks, not dictionaries.

3. **Register vs. function conflation**: Dictionary labels like "honorific" and "humble" describe the form's category but not its function in context. The same humble form (e.g., 申す) functions differently in formal writing (teijōgo, speech-level marker with no specific beneficiary) and in face-to-face speech (kenjōgo I, directed toward a specific superior).

4. **Productive patterns under-documented**: The お〜になる and お〜する patterns are mentioned in grammar references but rarely appear in individual verb entries with examples showing their use.

5. **No error-preemption**: Dictionaries don't warn about common keigo errors — for instance, that using sonkeigo with uchi members when speaking to soto is a common and noticeable mistake.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### Current state

je-dict-1 has 90 keigo-typed cross-references connecting base verbs to their honorific and humble equivalents. Approximately 422 entries mention keigo, honorifics, or humble forms in their notes. Around 22 entries are themselves dedicated keigo forms (いらっしゃる, 申し上げる, 差し上げる, etc.). Three pilot expository articles exist, one of which covers keigo.

### Recommendations

**1. Systematic keigo cross-references**: Every base verb with a suppletive keigo equivalent should have bidirectional `keigo`-typed cross-references in `prominent_see_also`. The current 90 keigo cross-references likely miss many pairs — a systematic audit using the suppletive forms table above would identify gaps. Priority: all verbs in the table above, plus productive patterns for high-frequency verbs.

**2. Uchi/soto notes on keigo entries**: Each keigo entry (e.g., 参る, いたす, 召し上がる) should include a brief note explaining the uchi/soto dynamic with a concrete example. For humble forms, note that the form applies to in-group members' actions when speaking to outsiders. For respectful forms, note that the form is used for soto members regardless of absolute social rank.

**3. Register differentiation in keigo entries**: Distinguish between kenjōgo I (directed toward a beneficiary) and teijōgo (speech-level marker) uses in entries for forms like 参る, 申す, and いたす, which serve both functions. This maps to the 2007 five-category system and helps learners understand when these forms are and aren't appropriate.

**4. Error-preempting notes**: High-traffic keigo entries should include "common mistakes" sections warning about typical L2 errors: sonkeigo/kenjōgo reversal, uchi/soto miscalculation, and double keigo. These notes preempt errors that even advanced learners make regularly.

**5. Productive pattern examples**: For verbs that can take the お〜になる (sonkeigo) or お〜する (kenjōgo) patterns, include at least one example sentence showing the pattern in use. This is particularly important for verbs where the productive pattern is more commonly used than any suppletive form.

**6. Keigo browsing path**: The cross-reference system could support a "keigo learning path" — a curated sequence that guides learners through the keigo system by connecting base forms to their honorific/humble equivalents, with the expository article on keigo serving as the entry point.

**7. L1-aware notes**: Where space permits, note that Korean speakers may transfer their L1 honorific intuitions to Japanese — sometimes accurately, sometimes not. For forms where the Japanese and Korean systems diverge (particularly uchi/soto handling), a brief contrastive note would be valuable.
