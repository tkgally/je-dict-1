# Japanese Aspect and the ている Construction

**Last updated**: 2026-05-02

## Overview

Japanese aspect — how a language encodes the internal temporal structure of events — is one of the most challenging areas for L2 learners and one of the most consequential for dictionary design. The ている construction, formed by attaching いる to the て-form of a verb, carries at least four distinct meanings depending on verb type and context: progressive action, resultative state, habitual activity, and experiential record. Because a learner dictionary provides verb entries as isolated units, it must compensate for the missing context by documenting which interpretation applies to each verb. This page surveys the linguistic research on Japanese aspect, its acquisition by L2 learners, and the implications for how a bilingual learner dictionary should present aspectual information.

## The Japanese tense-aspect system

### Aspect vs. tense in Japanese

Japanese has a two-way morphological distinction often described as "non-past" (る) vs. "past" (た). However, this distinction encodes both tense (when) and aspect (how) simultaneously. The non-past form 食べる can mean "will eat" (future tense) or "eats" (habitual aspect). The past form 食べた can mean "ate" (completed past) or, in some contexts, a perfective viewpoint on a just-completed event. The ている form adds a third option that is primarily aspectual: it marks events as ongoing, states as holding, or habits as established.

Unlike English, which marks the progressive obligatorily (the difference between "I eat" and "I am eating" is grammatically enforced), Japanese uses the same plain form (食べる) for both generic and future reference. The ている form is used for explicitly marked imperfective situations, but its meaning shifts dramatically by verb class.

### Lexical aspect and Kindaichi's verb classification

The foundational framework for understanding Japanese aspect is Kindaichi Haruhiko's (1950) verb classification, published in 「日本語動詞のテンスとアスペクト」 and subsequently refined by Fujii (1966), Okuda (1978), and Kudo (1995). Kindaichi divided Japanese verbs into four classes based on their inherent temporal properties (lexical aspect or *Aktionsart*):

| Kindaichi class | Japanese term | ている meaning | Examples |
|-----------------|--------------|---------------|----------|
| **Stative verbs** (状態動詞) | じょうたいどうし | *Cannot take ている* | ある (exist), いる (be present) |
| **Continuative verbs** (継続動詞) | けいぞくどうし | Progressive (ongoing action) | 食べる → 食べている (is eating), 読む → 読んでいる (is reading) |
| **Instantaneous verbs** (瞬間動詞) | しゅんかんどうし | Resultative state | 死ぬ → 死んでいる (is dead), 結婚する → 結婚している (is married) |
| **Type 4 verbs** (第四種の動詞) | だいよんしゅのどうし | Appearance/characteristic | 優れる → 優れている (is excellent), 似る → 似ている (resembles) |

This classification predicts the interpretation of ている almost entirely from the verb itself. Continuative (activity/process) verbs yield progressive readings; instantaneous (achievement/change-of-state) verbs yield resultative readings; and Type 4 verbs (which describe inherent properties) yield attributive readings that function like adjectives.

### Refinements: Kudo's temporal-modal framework

Kudo Mayumi (1995) provided the most comprehensive modern treatment in 『アスペクト・テンス体系とテクスト』. She expanded Kindaichi's system by analyzing how context, modality, and discourse structure interact with lexical aspect. Key contributions:

- **Outer aspect vs. inner aspect**: The morphological ている marking (outer aspect) interacts with the verb's inherent temporal profile (inner aspect) to produce the surface interpretation. The same verb can shift between readings in different discourse contexts.
- **The role of adverbials**: Temporal adverbs can coerce readings — ずっと読んでいる favors progressive, while 何度も読んでいる triggers habitual interpretation.
- **Discourse-level aspect**: In narrative text, aspect marking functions differently than in conversational description. Dictionary aspect notes should prioritize the conversational defaults.

### Mapping to Vendler's classes

Western aspect theory (Vendler 1957) classifies situations into states, activities, accomplishments, and achievements. The mapping to Kindaichi's system is approximate but useful:

| Vendler class | Kindaichi class | ている interpretation | Example |
|---------------|-----------------|----------------------|---------|
| State | 状態動詞 | N/A (ている unavailable) | ある, いる |
| Activity | 継続動詞 | Progressive | 走る → 走っている (is running) |
| Accomplishment | 継続動詞 (telic) | Progressive or resultative | 書く → 書いている (is writing / has written) |
| Achievement | 瞬間動詞 | Resultative | 着く → 着いている (has arrived / is there) |

The key difference from English is that Japanese achievements with ている systematically produce resultative readings ("is in the state resulting from X") rather than progressive readings ("is X-ing"). English learners must override their L1 expectation that "-ing" = ongoing action.

## The four meanings of ている

### 1. Progressive (ongoing action)

The most prototypical and L1-transferable meaning. With activity and accomplishment verbs, ている indicates an action in progress:

- 食べている (is eating)
- 走っている (is running)
- 手紙を書いている (is writing a letter)

This reading is cognitively easy for English speakers because it maps directly onto English progressive "-ing."

### 2. Resultative state

With change-of-state (achievement) verbs, ている indicates the state that results from the event completing, not the event itself:

- 死んでいる = "is dead" (not "is dying")
- 結婚している = "is married" (not "is marrying")
- 窓が開いている = "the window is open" (not "the window is opening")
- 座っている = "is seated" (not "is sitting down")
- 知っている = "knows" (not "is coming to know")

This is the single most important aspectual distinction for learners to master, and the one that causes the most persistent errors (Shirai & Kurono 1998). The problem is compounded because English uses the progressive for some of these meanings ("is sitting," "is standing") but not others ("knows," not "*is knowing").

### 3. Habitual / iterative

ている can indicate a regularly repeated action, especially with verbs of occupation, commuting, or routine:

- 東京に住んでいる = "lives in Tokyo" (ongoing state of habitation)
- 銀行に勤めている = "works at a bank" (ongoing employment)
- 毎日ジョギングをしている = "jogs every day" (habitual)
- この薬を飲んでいる = "takes this medicine" (ongoing regimen)

The habitual reading often overlaps with the resultative reading (住む is arguably a change-of-state verb, making 住んでいる a resultative, but functionally it reads as habitual). Dictionary notes should use whichever label is most intuitively helpful for learners.

### 4. Experiential / record

Some uses of ている indicate that an event has occurred in the past with present relevance, functioning like English present perfect:

- この本は読んでいる = "I have read this book (so I know its contents)"
- 三回行っている = "I have been [there] three times"

This reading is context-dependent and harder to predict from verb class alone. It often co-occurs with quantifying expressions (三回, 何度も) or topic-marking that foregrounds the experience itself.

### Ambiguity and context-dependency

Many verbs are genuinely ambiguous between readings. 読んでいる can mean "is reading" (progressive), "reads regularly" (habitual), or "has read" (experiential), depending entirely on context. This makes the dictionary's job harder: the entry cannot pick one meaning and ignore the others. The best approach is to document the primary (most frequent/default) reading and note any important secondary readings.

## L2 acquisition of Japanese aspect

### The Aspect Hypothesis

Andersen (1991) and Andersen & Shirai (1994) proposed the **Aspect Hypothesis**, which predicts that L2 learners' acquisition of tense-aspect morphology is guided by lexical aspect:

1. Learners first use past/perfective marking with achievement and accomplishment verbs (telic events)
2. Progressive marking first appears with activity verbs
3. Only later do learners extend these markings to less prototypical combinations

Applied to Japanese, this predicts that learners will:
- First acquire ている with activity verbs (prototypical progressive: 食べている)
- Later extend it to achievements (resultative: 死んでいる)
- Struggle longest with non-prototypical combinations (habitual, experiential)

### Shirai's research on ている acquisition

Shirai Yasuhiro's work, including Shirai (2000) and Shirai & Kurono (1998), confirmed the Aspect Hypothesis predictions for Japanese and added important refinements:

- **L1 transfer interacts with lexical aspect**: English speakers' difficulty with the resultative ている is compounded by transfer from English progressive. When learners see ている, their L1 biases them toward a progressive interpretation regardless of verb class.
- **Developmental sequence**: Learners consistently acquire progressive ている before resultative ている. Habitual and experiential readings are acquired latest.
- **Overextension errors**: Beginners commonly produce ている with stative verbs (*あっている instead of ある) by analogy with English "is existing." More advanced learners may still interpret 死んでいる as "is dying" rather than "is dead."
- **The prototypicality effect**: Even at advanced levels, learners process prototypical combinations (activity + ている = progressive) faster than non-prototypical ones (achievement + ている = resultative), suggesting that the resultative reading requires conscious knowledge that can be supported by dictionary notes.

### Sugaya and Shirai (2007)

Sugaya and Shirai (2007) conducted a large-scale study comparing 26 English L1 speakers with 35 German/Slavic L1 speakers on ている acquisition. Key findings:

- **Resultative accuracy is strikingly low**: L2 learners scored around **30% accuracy** on resultative use of ている — worse than chance — even at intermediate and advanced levels. This is the single most compelling data point for why dictionaries need per-verb aspect notes.
- **L1 effects are secondary to universal tendencies**: Both English and German/Slavic L1 groups showed the same progressive bias, suggesting the Aspect Hypothesis's universal cognitive predisposition outweighs L1 transfer.
- **Explicit instruction helps**: Learners who received explicit instruction on the relationship between verb class and ている meaning outperformed those who learned through exposure alone.
- **Verb-by-verb learning**: Learners appeared to learn ている meanings verb by verb rather than acquiring a general rule and applying it. This finding directly supports the dictionary approach of documenting aspect per entry rather than relying on a single grammar explanation.

### Error types

Common ている errors by English-speaking learners include:

| Error type | Example | Explanation |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| Progressive interpretation of resultative | 死んでいる = "*is dying" | L1 transfer from English progressive |
| Missing ている for current states | *今、結婚する (for "is married") | Failure to use resultative ている |
| ている with statives | *ある**ている** | Overextension by analogy |
| Habitual ている missed | *毎日走る (for "runs every day") | Not recognizing habitual function |
| Past for resultative | 窓が開いた (for "is open") | Using perfective where resultative is needed |

## Related aspectual constructions

### てある (resultative with agent implication)

Where ている with intransitive change-of-state verbs marks a resultative state without specifying agency (窓が開いている "the window is open"), てある marks the result of a deliberate act: 窓が開けてある "the window has been opened [by someone, intentionally]." The semantic contrast is between an observed state and a prepared/intentional state.

### ていく / てくる (directional aspect)

These compound forms add a directional or temporal trajectory to the event:
- ていく: moving away from the speaker's reference point, or continuing into the future (寒くなっていく "is getting colder [going forward]")
- てくる: moving toward the speaker's reference point, or emerging up to now (分かってきた "has come to understand")

### てしまう (completive / regrettable)

Marks an event as completed, often with a nuance of regret, irreversibility, or unintended consequence: 食べてしまった "ate it all up / unfortunately ate it." The distinction between completive (value-neutral) and regrettable (emotive) readings is another area where dictionary notes add value.

### ておく (preparatory)

Marks an action done in advance for future benefit: 予約しておく "make a reservation [in advance]." The preparatory aspect is not inherently marked in the verb and must be learned as a construction.

### ているところ (precisely ongoing)

Emphasizes that an action is at the exact moment of occurring: 食べているところだ "am right in the middle of eating." This explicitly disambiguates toward the progressive reading, which is useful precisely because ている alone can be ambiguous.

## How dictionaries handle aspect

### Current practice in JE dictionaries

Major Japanese-English dictionaries treat aspect inconsistently:

- **Kenkyusha's New Japanese-English Dictionary** (5th ed.): Includes ている forms in example sentences but rarely explains the semantic difference between the base form and the ている form. A reader must infer that 死んでいる means "is dead" from translation alone.
- **Wisdom Japanese-English Dictionary**: Occasionally includes usage notes on ている for high-frequency verbs, but coverage is unsystematic.
- **JMdict/EDICT**: Does not include aspect information at all. The ている form is absent from entries; users see only the dictionary form.
- **Makino & Tsutsui's *Dictionary of Basic/Intermediate Japanese Grammar***: Provides excellent aspect explanations but is a grammar dictionary, not a vocabulary dictionary. A learner looking up 死ぬ in a standard dictionary gets no aspect guidance; they must separately consult Makino & Tsutsui.

### The gap

No widely-used JE dictionary systematically documents ている behavior per verb entry. This means that for the single most important aspect distinction in Japanese (resultative vs. progressive ている), learners must either:
1. Discover it through errors and correction
2. Memorize it from a grammar textbook and apply the rules themselves
3. Infer it from example sentences (if the dictionary happens to include a ている example)

Sugaya & Shirai's (2007) finding that learners acquire ている meanings verb by verb — not by applying a general rule — makes this gap particularly costly. A learner who knows the abstract rule "achievement verbs + ている = resultative" still needs per-verb confirmation, because (a) classifying a verb as "achievement" requires native-level intuition, and (b) many verbs have multiple readings that depend on context.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### The ASPECT note system

je-dict-1 addresses this gap directly through structured ASPECT notes in verb entries. The `polish_aspect_notes.md` polishing task adds per-verb documentation of ている behavior when the meaning is non-obvious (i.e., when it is not a straightforward progressive). As of May 2026, 969 of 7,313 verb entries (13.3%) have ASPECT notes, with systematic polishing progressing through the entry space.

The ASPECT note format:
```
ASPECT (ている):
- [verb]ている: [meaning in English] ([aspect type])
```

For example, a 死ぬ entry would include:
```
ASPECT (ている):
- {死|し}んでいる: is dead (resultative state, not progressive)
```

### Which verbs need aspect documentation

Research and practical experience converge on a priority order:

1. **Highest priority — resultative verbs**: Verbs where ている indicates a state rather than an ongoing action. These cause the most learner errors and are the most counterintuitive for English speakers. Examples: 死ぬ, 結婚する, 座る, 立つ, 持つ, 着る, 住む, 知る, 開く, 閉まる, 壊れる, 決まる, 似る, 慣れる, 疲れる.

2. **High priority — verbs with multiple ている readings**: Verbs where context determines whether ている is progressive, habitual, or experiential. Examples: 読む (reading / has read), 勤める (is working at / works at).

3. **Medium priority — てある contrasts**: Transitive verbs whose てある form contrasts meaningfully with the intransitive ている form. Examples: 開ける (開けてある vs. 開いている).

4. **Low priority — obvious progressives**: Verbs where ている straightforwardly means "is doing X." These do not need aspect notes.

### Alignment with research

The ASPECT note approach aligns well with the SLA findings:

- **Verb-by-verb learning** (Sugaya & Shirai 2007): Per-entry notes match how learners actually acquire aspect.
- **Explicit instruction advantage**: Dictionary notes provide the explicit knowledge that research shows helps with non-prototypical ている readings.
- **L1-relative framing**: Notes that say "is dead, not is dying" directly address the English L1 transfer error.
- **Progressive need not be documented**: Because progressive ている is acquired first and causes few errors, excluding it from ASPECT notes is research-supported, not just a time-saving shortcut.

### Coverage targets

Given 7,313 verb entries, not all need aspect notes. A reasonable estimate:
- ~2,000–2,500 verbs have non-obvious ている behavior (instantaneous verbs, Type 4, ambiguous verbs)
- ~4,500–5,000 verbs have straightforward progressive ている and need no documentation
- Current 969 entries with ASPECT notes represent roughly 40–50% of verbs that need them

Reaching full coverage of non-obvious ている behavior is a tractable polishing goal and one of the highest-impact quality improvements the dictionary can make.
