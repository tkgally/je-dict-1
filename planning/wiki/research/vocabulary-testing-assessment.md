# Vocabulary Testing and Assessment

**Last updated**: 2026-06-03

## Overview

Vocabulary assessment is a core concern of applied linguistics and lexicography. How we measure what a learner knows about words shapes how we understand vocabulary acquisition, design teaching materials, and — critically for this project — decide what information a dictionary should contain. The major instruments in the field test different *dimensions* of word knowledge, and the gaps between what tests measure and what learners actually need reveal opportunities for dictionary design.

This page surveys the principal vocabulary tests, the theoretical frameworks they operationalize, the Japanese-specific challenges they face, and what their design tells us about how dictionaries can better serve learners.

## Theoretical frameworks for word knowledge

### Nation's nine-aspect framework (2001)

The most influential model of what it means to "know a word" is Nation's (2001, *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language*) framework, which identifies nine aspects organized under three categories, each with a receptive and productive dimension (18 cells total):

| Category | Aspect | Receptive (R) | Productive (P) |
|----------|--------|---------------|-----------------|
| **Form** | Spoken | Recognize the word when heard | Pronounce the word |
| | Written | Recognize the written form | Spell/write the word |
| | Word parts | Recognize morphemes | Use word parts to express meaning |
| **Meaning** | Form–meaning link | Retrieve meaning from form | Retrieve form from meaning |
| | Concepts & referents | Understand the concept | Use the word to refer to the right things |
| | Associations | Know related words | Produce related words |
| **Use** | Grammatical functions | Recognize grammatical patterns | Use the word in correct patterns |
| | Collocations | Recognize typical collocates | Produce natural collocations |
| | Constraints on use | Know register, frequency, dialect | Use the word appropriately in context |

This framework has two key implications. First, vocabulary knowledge is not binary — a learner progresses through incremental stages from zero knowledge to full mastery across all 18 cells. Second, no single test can measure all dimensions; each instrument captures a slice.

### Breadth vs. depth

The vocabulary assessment literature distinguishes two macro-dimensions:

- **Breadth** (vocabulary size): How many words does the learner know? Typically operationalized as recognition of form–meaning links across frequency bands.
- **Depth** (quality of knowledge): How well does the learner know those words? Encompasses collocational knowledge, polysemy awareness, morphological relations, register sensitivity, and more.

Qian (2002, *Language Learning*) demonstrated empirically that breadth and depth are correlated but distinct constructs, each contributing independently to reading comprehension. This finding — that knowing *more* words and knowing them *better* are separable — is central to dictionary design: a dictionary that only provides definitions addresses breadth, while one that includes collocations, register labels, and similar-word distinctions also supports depth.

### Vocabulary fluency

A third dimension, sometimes called **vocabulary fluency** or **lexical access speed**, refers to how quickly and automatically a learner can retrieve word knowledge. Kremmel and Schmitt (2016, *Language Assessment Quarterly*) argued that fluency deserves equal standing with breadth and depth in assessment frameworks, because a learner may "know" a word in a testing situation but be unable to retrieve it fast enough for real-time communication.

## Major vocabulary tests

### Vocabulary Levels Test (VLT)

The VLT (Nation, 1983; revised by Schmitt, Schmitt & Clapham, 2001; updated by Webb, Sasao & Balance, 2017) is a diagnostic instrument that estimates how well a learner knows vocabulary at specific frequency levels (1000, 2000, 3000, 5000, and 10,000 word families for the original; 1000–5000 for the updated version).

**Format**: Matching — test-takers match three definitions to six target words at each frequency level. This avoids the guessing problem of four-option multiple choice (25% chance) while keeping the task simple.

**What it measures**: Written receptive vocabulary — specifically, the ability to match a written word form to its meaning. It is a *levels* test, not a *size* test: it diagnoses which frequency band needs work rather than estimating total vocabulary.

**Limitations**: The matching format can allow elimination strategies. Items are drawn from English-specific frequency lists (BNC/COCA for the updated version), making the test language-specific. There is no productive dimension.

### Vocabulary Size Test (VST)

The VST (Nation & Beglar, 2007) estimates total written receptive vocabulary size from the first 1,000 to the 14th 1,000 word families.

**Format**: 140 four-option multiple-choice items (10 per 1,000-word level). The target word appears first in isolation, then in a non-defining context sentence. Answer choices may be in English or the learner's L1.

**What it measures**: Written receptive vocabulary size. Rasch reliability is approximately .96, and the test loads on a single factor. Each correct item at a given level is multiplied by 100 to estimate the number of word families known at that level.

**Limitations**: The non-defining context sentence sometimes inadvertently provides clues. The test assumes word families are the appropriate unit, which is problematic for agglutinative or logographic languages. There is no depth or productive component.

### Yes/No Vocabulary Test (Checklist Test)

The Yes/No test (Meara, 1992; Meara & Buxton, 1987) presents a list of real words mixed with pseudowords (plausible-looking nonwords). Test-takers simply indicate whether they know each item.

**Format**: Typically 40 real words and 20 pseudowords. Binary response: "I know this word" or "I don't know this word."

**What it measures**: Self-reported receptive vocabulary recognition. Extremely efficient — can test hundreds of items in minutes. The pseudowords serve as a honesty check: if a test-taker claims to know many pseudowords, their scores are adjusted downward using correction formulas (Δm, cfg).

**Limitations**: The overestimation problem is well-documented. Test-takers may believe they know a word when their knowledge is partial or wrong. Pellicer-Sánchez and Schmitt (2012) explored reaction-time-based scoring as an alternative to pseudoword correction. Zhang, Liu, and Ai (2020) found that pseudoword design significantly affects false alarm rates and thus score reliability.

### Vocabulary Knowledge Scale (VKS)

The VKS (Wesche & Paribakht, 1996) uses a self-report scale combined with performance verification to assess incremental stages of word learning.

**Format**: Five-level scale per word:
1. I have never seen this word before.
2. I have seen this word before, but I don't know what it means.
3. I have seen this word before, and I think it means ___ (synonym or translation).
4. I know this word. It means ___ (synonym or translation).
5. I can use this word in a sentence: ___ (write a sentence).

Levels 3–5 are verified by the researcher. A learner who claims level 5 but writes an incorrect sentence is scored lower.

**What it measures**: Incremental vocabulary acquisition — the progression from complete unfamiliarity to productive use. Uniquely combines self-report with performance, capturing both breadth and a limited depth dimension.

**Limitations**: Scoring is labor-intensive (requires human verification of sentences). The five levels conflate different types of knowledge (receptive recognition at level 2, recall at levels 3–4, production at level 5). Read (2000) argued the scale is not truly incremental because the dimensions it measures are not stages of a single continuum.

### Productive Vocabulary Levels Test (PVLT)

The PVLT (Laufer & Nation, 1999) assesses controlled productive vocabulary — words a learner can produce when prompted.

**Format**: Sentence completion with the first letters of the target word provided. Example: "The garden was full of beautiful fl___." (answer: flowers). Items are sampled across frequency levels (2000, 3000, 5000, UWL, 10,000).

**What it measures**: Controlled productive vocabulary — the ability to retrieve the form of a word given its meaning in context. The provided initial letters prevent random guessing while constraining the response.

**Limitations**: Providing initial letters makes the task partly receptive (recognition of the beginning letters as a cue). Spelling counts in scoring, which disadvantages L2 learners with weak orthographic knowledge. Internal consistency ranges from .83 to .89 across levels — solid but lower than the VST.

### Word Associates Test (WAT)

The WAT (Read, 1993, 1998) measures depth of vocabulary knowledge by testing paradigmatic (synonym) and syntagmatic (collocation) associations.

**Format**: A stimulus word is presented with eight options. Four of the eight are correct associates: some are synonyms of the stimulus word, and some are common collocates. The test-taker selects the four correct options.

**What it measures**: Vocabulary depth — specifically, whether learners know a word's semantic neighbors and its typical collocational partners. Qian (2002) showed WAT scores predicted reading comprehension independently of vocabulary size scores.

**Limitations**: Confirmatory factor analysis (Batty, 2012, *Vocabulary Learning and Instruction*) found that while a bifactor model (general vocabulary factor + separable synonym and collocate subfactors) fits the WAT data best, the synonym and collocate dimensions are not as psychometrically distinct as originally theorized. The WAT tests only two of Nation's nine aspects (associations and collocations).

## Japanese-specific vocabulary assessment challenges

### The word-family problem

English vocabulary tests assume the "word family" as the counting unit — a base word plus its inflected and derived forms (e.g., *create, creates, created, creating, creative, creativity, creator*). For Japanese, the word-family concept is problematic:

- **Kanji compounds** create an enormous productive word-formation system. Is 経済 (economy), 経済的 (economic), 経済学 (economics), 経済学者 (economist) one word family or four? If one, vocabulary size estimates shrink dramatically; if four, they inflate.
- **Verb morphology** is regular and predictable in Japanese. A learner who knows 食べる can reliably produce 食べない, 食べます, 食べた, 食べて, 食べられる, 食べさせる, etc. Counting these as separate "words" would grossly overestimate vocabulary breadth.
- **On'yomi/kun'yomi** readings mean a single kanji participates in multiple word families. 生 appears in 生きる (to live), 生まれる (to be born), 生活 (lifestyle), 生命 (life), 先生 (teacher), 学生 (student), etc.

### Script complexity and test design

Japanese uses four scripts (hiragana, katakana, kanji, and romaji) with different reading processes for each. A vocabulary test must decide whether to present items in kanji (testing kanji reading ability alongside vocabulary), in kana (isolating vocabulary from kanji knowledge), or in both (testing realistic reading). This decision affects what the test measures: a learner who recognizes 経済 in kana may not recognize it in kanji, or vice versa.

### JLPT vocabulary levels

The JLPT (Japanese-Language Proficiency Test) groups vocabulary loosely into five levels (N5–N1), but the vocabulary lists are not public, the grouping is exam-oriented rather than frequency-based, and the correlation between JLPT levels and actual usage frequency is imperfect. The J-CAT (Japanese Computerized Adaptive Test) provides a computer-adaptive alternative with more granular proficiency estimation, but neither instrument provides the detailed frequency-band diagnostics of a VLT.

### Missing instruments

There is no widely validated Japanese equivalent of the VLT, VST, or WAT that uses corpus-derived frequency bands. Most Japanese vocabulary assessment relies on JLPT levels, which conflate vocabulary, grammar, and reading comprehension. This is a significant gap: there is no standard tool for answering "how many Japanese words does this learner know?" with the same rigor available for English.

## What vocabulary tests tell us about dictionary needs

### The testing–dictionary alignment gap

Vocabulary tests and dictionaries serve different purposes but address overlapping knowledge. The alignment between what tests measure and what dictionaries provide reveals instructive gaps:

| Knowledge dimension | Tested by | Dictionary support |
|--------------------|-----------|--------------------|
| Form–meaning link (R) | VLT, VST, Yes/No | Core function: headword + gloss |
| Form–meaning link (P) | PVLT | Partial: examples show usage, but retrieving a word from its meaning is the learner's task |
| Collocations | WAT (partially) | Collocation fields, example sentences |
| Associations / synonyms | WAT | Cross-references, similar-words sections |
| Register / constraints | No standard test | Register labels, formality tags, notes |
| Grammatical function | No standard test | POS tags, particle patterns, conjugation tables |
| Polysemy / sense discrimination | No standard test | Numbered definitions, sense-specific examples |

The right column shows that dictionaries routinely provide information that no standard vocabulary test measures — register, grammar patterns, polysemy handling. This suggests dictionaries are *ahead* of testing in some respects: they contain knowledge dimensions that the field hasn't yet figured out how to test efficiently.

### Implications of the breadth–depth distinction

The breadth–depth distinction maps directly onto two dictionary use cases:

1. **Breadth support** (encountering unknown words): The learner looks up a word they don't know at all. They need a clear form–meaning link — a good gloss, basic definition, one or two examples. This is the decoding / receptive use case.

2. **Depth support** (enriching known words): The learner looks up a word they partially know. They need collocations, similar-word distinctions, register information, example patterns. This is the encoding / productive use case, and also the "depth-building" use case.

Research consistently shows that depth develops more slowly than breadth (Schmitt, 2014) and that depth deficits persist even at advanced proficiency levels (Qian, 2002). A dictionary that provides only glosses addresses breadth; one that includes structured depth information (collocations, similar words, register labels, particle patterns) actively supports the harder, slower process of deepening word knowledge.

### Implications of the VKS stages

The VKS's five stages map onto dictionary design decisions:

| VKS stage | Learner state | Dictionary need |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|
| 1 (never seen) | Zero knowledge | Clear, memorable gloss; pronunciation guide |
| 2 (seen before, no meaning) | Partial formal recognition | Form–meaning link; furigana for kanji reading |
| 3 (thinks they know meaning) | Tentative recognition | Definition confirmation; examples to verify understanding |
| 4 (knows meaning) | Established receptive knowledge | Depth information: collocations, similar words, register |
| 5 (can use in a sentence) | Productive knowledge | Production support: particle patterns, example sentences as models, constraints |

A dictionary entry that serves all five stages needs both a quick-glance layer (gloss, reading) and deeper layers (collocations, notes, cross-references). This argues for progressive information density — essential information first, enriching detail available on demand.

### Implications of the productive gap

The PVLT and related productive tests consistently show that productive vocabulary lags behind receptive vocabulary by 20–50% (Laufer, 1998; Webb, 2008). Learners know many more words than they can use. This gap is precisely where production-oriented dictionary features — collocations, particle patterns, example sentences showing the word in context, similar-word distinctions — provide their highest value. The gap also suggests that dictionary features supporting production (encoding) deserve at least equal priority with those supporting comprehension (decoding).

## Implications for je-dict-1

1. **Entry structure already maps well to Nation's framework.** je-dict-1 entries address form (headword, reading, furigana), meaning (definitions, examples), and use (collocations, register labels, notes). The main under-covered dimension is spoken form — the dictionary has limited audio coverage (1,028 entries).

2. **Depth-oriented features are the dictionary's differentiator.** Simple form–meaning links are available from any bilingual word list or machine translation tool. je-dict-1's value lies in its depth information: collocations, similar-word sections, particle patterns, aspect notes, register labels. These map to the knowledge dimensions that standard vocabulary tests don't measure but that research shows are critical for advanced proficiency.

3. **Progressive information density serves all VKS stages.** The current entry layout — gloss at top, then definitions, then examples, then notes with collocations and similar words — already implements a natural progression from breadth to depth. Maintaining this layered structure ensures the dictionary serves learners at every stage.

4. **The three-tier system aligns with frequency-based assessment thinking.** Just as the VLT tests vocabulary at frequency levels, je-dict-1's basic/core/general tiers roughly correspond to high/mid/lower frequency vocabulary. The closed basic and core tiers can serve as a vocabulary inventory checklist for learners, while the open general tier extends coverage beyond what any fixed-level test would measure.

5. **Cross-references function as an association network.** The WAT measures whether learners know a word's synonyms and collocates — exactly the relationships encoded in je-dict-1's cross-reference system (synonym, antonym, contrast, related, homophone types). Strengthening cross-reference density and symmetry directly supports the kind of associative knowledge that depth tests measure.

6. **The productive vocabulary gap motivates encoding features.** Collocation fields, particle patterns, and production-oriented example sentences address the 20–50% receptive–productive gap that the PVLT reveals. These features should be prioritized alongside (not below) comprehension-oriented content like definitions and glosses.

7. **No standard Japanese vocabulary size test exists.** This is both a gap in the field and an opportunity: je-dict-1's tiered, frequency-informed vocabulary set could potentially serve as the basis for a vocabulary checklist or self-assessment tool, though that is well beyond the dictionary's current scope.
