# Dictionary Use in the Age of Machine Translation

**Last updated**: 2026-06-02

## Overview

Free neural machine translation (NMT) tools — Google Translate, DeepL, ChatGPT — have transformed how L2 learners interact with target-language text. Usage surveys consistently report 85–95% of language students using MT tools regularly (Clifford et al. 2013; Jolley & Maimone 2022), often as their first resource when encountering unknown language. This raises a question that any dictionary project must confront: if learners can paste a sentence into a translator and get a fluent rendering in seconds, what role remains for a bilingual dictionary?

The research suggests that the answer is not "none" — but the role has shifted. Dictionaries and MT serve genuinely complementary functions, and the cases where MT fails are precisely the cases where a well-designed learner dictionary adds the most value: polysemy, register, collocations, productive use, and depth of vocabulary knowledge.

## How learners actually use MT

### Prevalence and purposes

Jolley & Maimone (2022) reviewed thirty years of MT research in language education and identified four recurring research questions: how learners use MT, what they think about it, how it affects learning, and how instructors should respond. Their review shows that use is near-universal among contemporary students and cuts across all skill levels.

Briggs (2018) found that most university students in Korea use Google Translate for various purposes — vocabulary lookup, writing support, reading comprehension, and homework. Clifford et al. (2013) reported that many students "enter individual words into the system," effectively treating MT as a faster dictionary, despite the fact that single-word queries strip the contextual information that NMT models need for accurate translation.

### MT as a de facto dictionary replacement

A critical finding from multiple studies is that learners increasingly reach for MT where they would previously have reached for a dictionary (Groves & Mundt 2015; Valijärvi & Tarsoly 2019). The speed and apparent comprehensiveness of MT make it the path of least resistance. But this substitution carries costs that learners may not recognize:

- **Single-word MT queries sacrifice context.** NMT processes entire sentences; feeding it isolated words removes the signal it needs for disambiguation, producing unreliable results for polysemous words (Groves & Mundt 2015).
- **MT output is opaque.** The learner receives a finished translation but no information about *why* that translation is correct — no sense division, no register label, no collocation pattern, no usage notes.
- **MT creates a "consultation without learning" pattern.** The learner gets the answer but bypasses the cognitive processes that would make the word stick.

## The depth-of-processing problem

### Laufer & Hulstijn's framework applied to MT

The Involvement Load Hypothesis (Laufer & Hulstijn 2001) predicts that vocabulary retention correlates with the depth of processing during the encounter, measured across three components: need (motivation to find the word), search (effort to locate it), and evaluation (deciding whether it fits the context). Dictionary consultation generates moderate involvement: the learner must search through entries, select among senses, and evaluate fit. MT consultation, by contrast, collapses search and evaluation into a single step — the system delivers a finished translation with no learner decision required.

Urlaub & Dessein (2022) argue that current MT implementations "deny learners the opportunity to engage" in the meaning negotiation that characterizes effective acquisition. They note that MT reduces the cognitive friction that, paradoxically, makes learning durable.

### Empirical evidence on MT and retention

Lee (2023) conducted a quasi-experimental study examining whether vocabulary improvement achieved through NMT-assisted writing leads to vocabulary retention when the tool is removed. The study, published in *Computer Assisted Language Learning*, found a significant proficiency-level interaction:

- **Higher-proficiency learners** achieved similar levels of immediate and delayed retention compared to their vocabulary improvement — they could retain what they learned while using NMT.
- **Lower-proficiency learners** showed vocabulary improvement and immediate retention but significantly weaker delayed retention — the NMT-assisted gains faded without the tool.

This finding aligns with Jiang's (2000) three-stage model of L2 lexical development: lower-proficiency learners, still dependent on L1 lemma mediation, need more cognitive engagement to push words beyond the fragile formal-representation stage. MT provides a shortcut that bypasses this necessary processing.

## What dictionaries provide that MT cannot

### 1. Sense discrimination and polysemy handling

MT delivers *one* translation for a given context. A dictionary presents the full sense inventory, letting the learner understand that 甘い means "sweet" in one context, "lenient" in another, and "naive" in a third. This awareness of polysemous range is a component of depth of vocabulary knowledge (Nation 2001; Read 2000) that single-translation MT output cannot develop.

Research on MT and polysemy (Restrepo Ramos 2023) confirms that polysemous words remain one of MT's persistent failure modes. When the sentence context does not strongly disambiguate, MT may select the wrong sense — and the learner, lacking awareness that other senses exist, will not notice the error.

### 2. Register and formality information

Japanese has a particularly rich register system (keigo, uchi/soto, stratal register across wago/kango/gairaigo). MT systems handle register inconsistently — they may translate a casual sentence using formal vocabulary or vice versa, without flagging the mismatch. A dictionary entry that explicitly labels a word as "formal," "casual," "literary," or "vulgar" provides information that MT embeds invisibly (if at all) in its output.

### 3. Collocations and productive patterns

MT helps learners *decode* (understand input) but offers limited support for *encoding* (producing output). When a learner wants to say "take a bath" in Japanese and types it into MT, they get お風呂に入る — correct, but with no information about what other verbs collocate with 風呂, what particles are required, or what the productive pattern looks like. A dictionary entry with collocation fields, particle patterns, and encoding-oriented examples gives the learner the generative knowledge they need for production, not just a one-shot translation.

### 4. Morphological and etymological transparency

For Japanese, where Sino-Japanese compounds are productively analyzable (教育 = 教 "teach" + 育 "raise"), a dictionary that provides morphemic glosses helps learners develop the compound-inferencing skills that are a major source of receptive vocabulary growth (see [Word Formation and Morphology](word-formation.md)). MT treats compound words as unanalyzed units, providing no window into their internal structure.

### 5. Cross-references and vocabulary networks

A dictionary with cross-references (synonyms, antonyms, transitivity pairs, register variants, compounds) exposes the semantic network around a word. This network view supports the interconnected mental lexicon organization that research associates with deep vocabulary knowledge (Henriksen 1999; Qian 2002). MT provides only one word at a time, in isolation.

### 6. Metalinguistic information

Conjugation tables, transitivity labels, aspect behavior (ている readings), counter patterns, honorific form links — all of these are metalinguistic features that dictionaries present explicitly and MT handles implicitly (when it handles them at all). A learner who looks up 開く in a dictionary learns that it can be read あく (intransitive) or ひらく (both transitive and intransitive), with different usage patterns. MT will choose one reading silently.

## The complementary-tools framework

### Ducar & Schocket's pedagogical position

Ducar & Schocket (2018) argued in *Foreign Language Annals* that MT use is an "essential 21st-century skill" and that instructors should move from prohibition to integration. Their framework emphasizes teaching learners *when* to use MT (whole-text comprehension, rough drafts, checking intuitions) and *when* to use dictionaries (vocabulary depth, sense selection, production, register verification).

### Urlaub & Dessein's redesign proposals

Urlaub & Dessein (2022) proposed three innovations for MT tools to make them more learning-friendly:

1. **Dual modes**: a "classic mode" for translation and an "educational mode" that slows down the process and demands learner engagement.
2. **Multiple translation options**: presenting three alternatives that require the learner to justify a selection — reintroducing the evaluation component.
3. **Input quality feedback**: training learners to formulate unambiguous, contextually rich source sentences.

These proposals implicitly describe features that good dictionaries already provide: multiple senses (= multiple options), usage guidance (= evaluation support), and structured entry navigation (= engagement through search).

### A practical division of labor

| Task | Best tool | Why |
|------|-----------|-----|
| Understanding the gist of a paragraph | MT | Speed; sentence-level context helps |
| Looking up a specific word's meaning range | Dictionary | Sense inventory, examples, notes |
| Checking a collocation or particle pattern | Dictionary | Explicit productive information |
| Drafting a rough translation for revision | MT | Generates a starting point quickly |
| Verifying register appropriateness | Dictionary | Explicit register labels |
| Learning a word for long-term retention | Dictionary | Deeper processing, cross-references |
| Post-editing MT output | Both | MT provides draft; dictionary resolves uncertainties |
| Understanding why two near-synonyms differ | Dictionary | Contrastive notes, similar-words sections |

## Japanese-specific considerations

### Script complexity raises the MT advantage for decoding

Japanese's three-script writing system (kanji, hiragana, katakana) creates a high lookup barrier for dictionary consultation. A learner encountering 承認 in text must know or guess the reading (しょうにん) before they can look it up in many dictionaries. MT bypasses this barrier entirely — paste the text and get a translation. This is a genuine advantage of MT for Japanese learners, especially those with limited kanji knowledge.

However, dictionaries with furigana (like je-dict-1) and conjugation-based search indexes mitigate this barrier. And the furigana itself is a learning aid: seeing {承認|しょうにん} teaches the reading in a way that MT's invisible processing does not.

### Aspect, transitivity, and particles are MT blind spots

Three areas where Japanese-English MT frequently produces correct-sounding but misleading output:

1. **ている ambiguity**: 窓が開いている can mean "the window is open" (resultative) or "the window is opening" (progressive). MT will pick one without indicating the ambiguity. A dictionary entry documenting ている behavior for 開く makes both readings visible.

2. **Transitivity pairs**: MT may translate both 落とす and 落ちる correctly in isolation, but it cannot teach the learner that these form a transitive/intransitive pair with distinct particle patterns (〜を落とす vs. 〜が落ちる). A dictionary with explicit transitivity marking and pair links provides this structural knowledge.

3. **Particle selection**: MT handles particles implicitly, and when it gets them right, the learner gains no insight into *why* that particle was correct. Dictionary entries with particle patterns (e.g., "に乗る, not を乗る") make the rule explicit for production.

### Keigo and social context

MT tools handle keigo (honorific language) inconsistently. They may translate "please eat" as 食べてください, 召し上がってください, or お食べください depending on opaque training-data patterns. A dictionary that links the plain form to its honorific and humble equivalents, with register labels and usage notes, gives the learner the social-pragmatic information that MT cannot reliably provide.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### 1. The dictionary's value proposition has shifted

je-dict-1 does not need to compete with MT on speed or coverage of basic decoding. Its value lies in the *depth* of information that MT cannot provide: sense inventories, register labels, collocations, transitivity pairs, aspect documentation, cross-references, and encoding-oriented examples. Every feature that adds depth to an entry widens the gap between what the dictionary offers and what MT can substitute.

### 2. Production support is the strongest differentiator

MT is strongest at receptive tasks (understanding input) and weakest at productive tasks (generating output). je-dict-1's collocation fields, particle patterns, conjugation tables, and encoding-oriented example sentences directly serve the productive use case that MT handles poorly. Expanding and systematizing these features should be a priority.

### 3. Cross-references create value MT cannot replicate

The interconnected network of cross-references — synonyms, antonyms, transitivity pairs, register variants, compounds — exposes vocabulary structure that MT treats as invisible. Each cross-reference is a learning opportunity that MT cannot generate. The current cross-reference density (0.58 per entry) should continue growing.

### 4. Post-editing as a use case

A growing body of research supports MT post-editing as a productive learning activity (Tsai 2019; Urlaub & Dessein 2022). In this workflow, the learner uses MT to generate a draft and then uses a dictionary to verify, correct, and refine it. je-dict-1's rich entry structure is well-suited to support this verification role — the learner can check register, confirm particle patterns, verify transitivity, and explore alternatives through cross-references.

### 5. Furigana is a competitive advantage

je-dict-1's universal furigana coverage serves a function that MT eliminates for decoding but that dictionaries preserve for learning: it teaches readings. Every encounter with {承認|しょうにん} is a micro-lesson in kanji readings that MT bypasses entirely. This is particularly valuable for alphabetic-L1 learners who need systematic reading exposure (see [L1 Transfer in Japanese L2 Vocabulary](l1-transfer-japanese-vocabulary.md)).

### 6. Browsing creates depth that MT cannot

MT is inherently transactional: question in, answer out. A browsable dictionary invites exploration — following cross-references, reading notes, discovering related words. This exploration creates the multiple encounters and varied processing that research associates with durable vocabulary knowledge (Laufer & Hulstijn 2001; Webb 2007). The dictionary's browsing interface is itself a learning tool that MT cannot replace.
