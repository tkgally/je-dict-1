# The Lexical Approach and Vocabulary-Centered Teaching

**Last updated**: 2026-05-26

## Overview

The lexical approach is a family of language teaching methodologies that place vocabulary — particularly multi-word chunks — at the centre of syllabus design and classroom practice. Originating in the early 1990s with Michael Lewis's *The Lexical Approach* (1993) and Dave Willis's *The Lexical Syllabus* (1990), the movement challenged the prevailing grammar-first model by arguing that fluency depends more on rapid retrieval of prefabricated phrases than on productive application of grammar rules. The approach draws theoretical support from John Sinclair's (1991) corpus-based discovery that language production alternates between an "idiom principle" (retrieving semi-preconstructed phrases) and an "open choice principle" (selecting individual words slot by slot), with the idiom principle governing a larger share of natural discourse than previously assumed.

This page surveys the theoretical foundations, the taxonomy of lexical items, empirical evidence, criticisms, and the approach's implications for learner dictionary design — particularly for je-dict-1.

## Theoretical foundations

### Sinclair's two principles (1991)

Sinclair's *Corpus, Concordance, Collocation* (1991) introduced the distinction that underpins the lexical approach:

- **The idiom principle**: "A language user has available to him or her a large number of semi-preconstructed phrases that constitute single choices, even though they might appear to be analysable into segments" (Sinclair 1991: 110). Corpus studies estimate that 50–60% of both spoken and written discourse is formulaic (Erman & Warren 2000).
- **The open choice principle**: The traditional model in which each word is freely selected from the full lexicon to fill a grammatical slot. This principle accounts for novel, creative language use but is invoked less often than intuition suggests.

The lexical approach takes the idiom principle as its starting point: if most natural language is assembled from prefabricated chunks, then teaching should prioritise chunk acquisition over grammar rule memorisation.

### Lewis's core claims (1993, 1997)

Lewis (1993) summarised the approach in a single axiom: **"Language consists of grammaticalized lexis, not lexicalized grammar."** The key claims are:

1. Lexis, not grammar, is the basis of language.
2. Grammatical competence is not a prerequisite for effective communication.
3. A meaning-centred syllabus should be organised around lexical items rather than grammatical structures.
4. Native speakers' fluency relies on a large store of memorised chunks that are retrieved holistically rather than assembled word by word.
5. Grammar is best acquired through observation and hypothesis-testing, not explicit rule instruction — "grammar exploration instead of grammar explanation."

Lewis's 1997 follow-up *Implementing the Lexical Approach* translated these principles into classroom practice, emphasising collocation as the most productive category of lexical chunk for intermediate learners.

### Willis's lexical syllabus (1990)

Working from the COBUILD project's 20-million-word corpus, Willis (1990) designed a graded syllabus in which the most frequent 700 English words and their common patterns formed Level 1, the next 800 words Level 2, and so on up to 2,500 words by Level 3. The insight was that frequency determines inclusion, and each word is taught not in isolation but with its characteristic collocations, grammatical patterns, and discourse functions — exactly the information a good dictionary provides.

## Taxonomy of lexical items

Lewis (1997) classified the language units that the lexical approach teaches into six categories:

| Category | Definition | Examples |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| **Words** | Individual items with independent meaning | *book*, *run*, *beautiful* |
| **Polywords** | Fixed multi-word items functioning as single words | *by the way*, *upside down*, *of course* |
| **Collocations** | Statistically frequent word pairings | *prices fell*, *rancid butter*, *make a decision* |
| **Institutionalised utterances** | Conventional pragmatic phrases | *I'll get it*, *We'll see*, *Would you like a cup of coffee?* |
| **Sentence frames/heads** | Templates with variable slots | *That is not as ... as you think*; *The fact/suggestion/problem was ...* |
| **Text frames** | Discourse-level organisational patterns | *In this paper we explore ...*; *Firstly ... Secondly ... Finally ...* |

The first three categories correspond roughly to the traditional dictionary's coverage (headwords, collocations, and fixed phrases). The last three — institutionalised utterances, sentence frames, and text frames — extend into territory that conventional dictionaries rarely cover systematically but that learners need for productive fluency.

## Related theoretical work

### Nation's four strands (2001, 2007)

Nation proposed that an effective vocabulary programme balances four "strands": meaning-focused input (extensive reading/listening), meaning-focused output (speaking/writing), language-focused learning (deliberate study), and fluency development (speed-building practice). The lexical approach aligns most closely with strands 1 and 3 but underweights strand 4. Nation's framework is more eclectic than Lewis's and has wider empirical support.

### Schmitt's instructed vocabulary learning (2008)

Schmitt's review concluded that a large vocabulary is necessary for functional L2 use (8,000–9,000 word families for reading; 5,000–7,000 for oral discourse) and that effective programmes need both explicit/intentional and exposure-based/incidental components. This echoes the lexical approach's emphasis on noticing but tempers it with evidence for deliberate study techniques (word cards, spaced repetition) that Lewis de-emphasised.

### Johns's data-driven learning (1991)

Tim Johns coined "data-driven learning" (DDL) to describe an enquiry-based approach in which learners use concordancers and corpora to discover patterns inductively — "observation, classification, generalisation." DDL is the natural pedagogical partner of the lexical approach: where Lewis argued that learners should notice chunks, Johns provided the tools. The lexical approach and DDL together imply that dictionaries should present collocation and pattern information in ways that support inductive discovery.

### Biber's lexical bundles (1999)

Biber et al. (1999) introduced the term "lexical bundles" for statistically recurrent multi-word sequences identified purely by corpus frequency (typically 10–40 occurrences per million words, spanning 3–6 words). Unlike Lewis's categories, which are defined by function, lexical bundles are defined by frequency alone. In academic prose, fewer than 5% of bundles are complete structural units — most straddle phrase or clause boundaries (e.g., *on the other hand*, *as a result of the*). The bundle concept has been influential in ESP/EAP materials but its relevance to general learner dictionaries is limited by the domain-specificity of most bundles.

## Empirical evidence

### Supporting findings

- **Chunk processing advantage**: Conklin & Schmitt (2008) showed that both L1 and L2 speakers process formulaic sequences faster than matched non-formulaic controls in self-paced reading tasks. Siyanova-Chanturia, Conklin & Schmitt (2011) confirmed this with eye-tracking data showing shorter fixation times for formulaic sequences.
- **Fluency effects**: Wood (2010) found that formulaic retrieval frees working memory for forward planning, contributing to perceived fluency. Boers et al. (2006, N=32) showed that instruction in noticing formulaic sequences increased their use in speech and correlated with higher perceived oral proficiency.
- **Collocation instruction**: Multiple studies show that explicit collocation teaching improves productive collocation use (e.g., Boers et al. 2006; Webb & Kagimoto 2009). The Involvement Load Hypothesis (Laufer & Hulstijn 2001) predicts that tasks requiring learners to evaluate collocational fit produce deeper processing and better retention.
- **Corpus frequency validation**: Willis's (1990) COBUILD-based syllabus demonstrated that organising instruction around the most frequent words and their patterns is practically feasible and aligns with Nation's (2001, 2006) coverage research showing that 2,000–3,000 high-frequency word families cover 80–85% of running text.

### Criticisms and limitations

- **Lack of a coherent methodology**: Thornbury (1998) criticised the lexical approach for being "a journey without maps" — heavy on theoretical claims but light on specific procedures for text selection, syllabus sequencing, and learner progression. Lewis never published a complete lexical-approach coursebook.
- **Weak empirical base for the approach as a whole**: While individual claims (chunks aid fluency, collocation knowledge matters) are well supported, no controlled study has compared a full lexical-approach programme against alternative syllabi. Sinclair & Renouf (1988) found that "claims made for the lexical syllabus are not supported by evidence."
- **Grammar cannot be marginalised**: Critics argue that the approach underestimates the role of explicit grammar knowledge, particularly for production. Nation (2001) and Schmitt (2008) advocate integrating grammar and vocabulary instruction rather than subordinating one to the other.
- **Chunk boundaries are fuzzy**: There is no principled way to decide what counts as a "chunk" versus a free combination. Lewis's six categories overlap, and different corpus methods yield different chunk inventories. This fuzziness complicates syllabus design and dictionary entry scope decisions.
- **Limited Japanese-specific research**: Most lexical approach research has been conducted with English as the target language. Japanese presents additional challenges: opaque word boundaries (no spaces), multiple orthographies, productive morphology within compounds, and the wago/kango/gairaigo stratal system all complicate chunk identification and teaching.

## Japanese-specific considerations

The lexical approach's principles map onto Japanese with some adjustments:

### Collocation patterns are stratum-sensitive

Japanese collocations often follow stratal lines: wago verbs take wago objects ({手|て}を{打|う}つ), kango nouns take する ({決定|けってい}する), gairaigo clusters together (アップデートする). A learner who knows the word {決定|けってい} but doesn't know it collocates with する rather than やる has incomplete lexical knowledge — exactly the kind of gap the lexical approach foregrounds.

### Particle selection as collocation

In Japanese, particle choice (に vs. で vs. を with a given verb) functions as a collocational relationship. The lexical approach would treat に{乗|の}る, を{降|お}りる, and で{遊|あそ}ぶ as chunks to be learned holistically rather than as applications of particle-selection rules. This aligns with je-dict-1's `particle_patterns` field in verb entries.

### Formulaic expressions span register levels

Japanese has rich inventories of formulaic language at each register level: casual set phrases (マジで, ヤバい), polite formulas (よろしくお{願|ねが}いします), business keigo ({お忙|いそが}しいところ恐れ入りますが), and seasonal greetings ({暑中|しょちゅう}お{見舞|みま}い{申|もう}し{上|あ}げます). These correspond to Lewis's "institutionalised utterances" and are prime candidates for expression entries in a learner dictionary.

### Compound productivity challenges chunk identification

Japanese compound formation is highly productive — nouns combine freely with する, other nouns compound via rendaku, and Sino-Japanese morphemes combine in semi-predictable ways. This means the "chunk" space is much larger and more graded than in English, making exhaustive listing impractical. A dictionary must decide which compounds merit their own entry (non-inferability principle from Atkins & Rundell 2008) versus which can be left to the learner's morphological knowledge.

## Implications for je-dict-1

The lexical approach provides theoretical justification for several features je-dict-1 already has and suggests areas for development:

### 1. Collocation fields as chunk documentation

je-dict-1's `collocations` field in entry JSON directly serves the lexical approach's central concern: teaching words in their characteristic combinations rather than in isolation. The project's current coverage (74.9% of na-adjectives, improving for other POS categories) maps onto Lewis's claim that collocations are the most teachable and productive chunk category for intermediate learners.

**Gap**: Collocation coverage for verbs and nouns is less systematic. The lexical approach would prioritise verb + object collocations ({約束|やくそく}を{守|まも}る, {責任|せきにん}を{取|と}る) and adjective + noun collocations ({深|ふか}い{眠|ねむ}り, {激|はげ}しい{雨|あめ}) as high-value entries.

### 2. Expression entries as formulaic language coverage

The dictionary's ~929 expression entries cover Lewis's "institutionalised utterances" category — greetings, conversational fillers, set phrases. The formulaic language research page documents how these prefabricated sequences are essential for perceived fluency (Wood 2010) and pragmatic competence (Bardovi-Harlig 2009).

**Gap**: Sentence frames ("〜と{言|い}えば", "〜に{限|かぎ}って") and text frames (discourse connectors like "{一方|いっぽう}で", "つまり") are underrepresented compared to single-phrase expressions. These higher-level chunks are what intermediate-to-advanced learners need most for academic and professional production.

### 3. Cross-references as chunk networks

The lexical approach views vocabulary as a web of associations, not a list of isolated items. je-dict-1's cross-reference system (16,130 references, 40.7% symmetry) supports this by connecting synonyms, antonyms, register variants, and collocational partners. The approach would suggest that cross-references be particularly dense around high-frequency chunk constituents — linking {約束|やくそく} not only to its synonym {誓|ちか}い but also to the verbs it characteristically combines with.

### 4. Examples as chunk exposure

The lexical approach emphasises that learners acquire chunks through repeated exposure in context. je-dict-1's 110,697 example sentences function as miniature concordance lines, showing each word in natural collocational context. The progressive-length design (short → long examples) mirrors the lexical approach's pedagogical sequence of noticing → recognising → producing.

### 5. Notes as pattern documentation

The notes field's structured sections (Common Collocations, Particle Patterns, Similar Words, Usage Notes) directly serve the lexical approach's goal of making lexical patterns explicit and noticeable. This is "grammar exploration" in Lewis's sense — presenting patterns for the learner to observe and internalise rather than formulating explicit rules.

### 6. Tier system as frequency grading

Willis's (1990) COBUILD-based frequency grading (700 → 1,500 → 2,500 words) parallels je-dict-1's three-tier system (basic ~800, core ~2,000, general 25,000+). The tiers ensure that the most frequent vocabulary receives the deepest treatment, consistent with both the lexical approach and Nation's (2001) coverage research.

## Potential developments informed by the lexical approach

1. **Collocation density metric**: Track and report the ratio of entries with populated collocation fields, broken down by POS and tier. Prioritise filling gaps in high-frequency verb and noun entries.
2. **Sentence frame entries**: Consider creating entries for productive sentence frames (〜ば〜ほど, 〜かと{思|おも}えば, 〜に{伴|ともな}って) that bridge grammar and vocabulary — Lewis's "sentence frames/heads" category.
3. **Chunk-aware example selection**: When writing or evaluating examples, prefer sentences that showcase the headword in its most characteristic collocations rather than in unusual or creative uses.
4. **Register-graded formulas**: Expand expression entries to cover the same pragmatic function at multiple register levels (e.g., refusing an invitation: casual, polite, business keigo), following the formulaic-language research on register-appropriate chunk retrieval.
