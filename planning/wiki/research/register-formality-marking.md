# Register and Formality Marking in Learner Dictionaries

**Last updated**: 2026-05-03

## Overview

Register and formality marking — the use of labels, notes, and structured codes to indicate when a word is formal, informal, literary, slang, or otherwise contextually restricted — is one of the persistent challenges in learner lexicography. Despite decades of theoretical work, dictionaries still lack consensus on which labels to use, how to assign them consistently, and how to make learners actually attend to them. The problem is compounded for Japanese, where register operates not just at the lexical level but is grammaticalized into verb morphology, the keigo system, and vocabulary-stratum distinctions (wago/kango/gairaigo).

## The diasystematic framework

The most influential taxonomy of usage labels comes from Hausmann (1989), who classified lexical marking along several **diasystematic** dimensions:

| Dimension | What it captures | Example labels |
|-----------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Diachronic** | Temporal restriction | archaic, dated, neologism |
| **Diatopic** | Regional restriction | British, American, Kansai-ben |
| **Diastratic** | Social stratum | slang, vulgar, elevated |
| **Diaphasic** | Situational register | formal, informal, colloquial |
| **Diatechnical** | Subject domain | medical, legal, computing |
| **Diafrequential** | Frequency restriction | rare |
| **Diaconnotative** | Evaluative colouring | derogatory, euphemistic, humorous |
| **Dianormative** | Correctness judgement | non-standard, substandard |
| **Diaintegrative** | Borrowing status | foreign, loanword |

Dictionaries rarely implement the full framework. Most use a simplified set of labels that conflate several dimensions — for instance, LDOCE's "informal" blends diaphasic and diastratic information (Verkuyl, Janssen & Jansen 2003). The distinction that matters most for production-oriented learner dictionaries is the **diaphasic** dimension: when can this word be used?

## Label inventories in major learner dictionaries

The "Big Four" English learner dictionaries vary considerably in their label sets:

| Label type | OALD | LDOCE | COBUILD | MED |
|-----------|------|-------|---------|-----|
| **Register** | formal, informal, slang | formal, informal | formal, informal | formal, informal, very formal, very informal |
| **Temporal** | old-fashioned, old use | old-fashioned | old-fashioned | old-fashioned |
| **Evaluative** | approving, disapproving, offensive, taboo | not polite, taboo, offensive | offensive | approving, disapproving, offensive, impolite |
| **Style** | literary, figurative, ironic, humorous | literary, humorous, spoken, written | literary, spoken, written | literary, humorous, spoken |
| **Total labels** | ~17 | ~12 | ~10 | ~13 |

Sources: Hünig (n.d.), Oxford Learner's Dictionaries labels page, Vrbinc & Vrbinc (2015).

Key observations:

- **"Formal" and "informal" are the only labels used identically across all four dictionaries.** Every other label shows at least some inconsistency in naming or scope.
- **MED uniquely offers "very formal" and "very informal"** — a four-point scale instead of the usual two-point register marking, which provides finer granularity.
- **COBUILD uses the fewest labels**, consistent with its editorial philosophy of encoding usage information through natural-language definitions and examples rather than metalinguistic codes.
- The evaluative labels (approving, disapproving, offensive, taboo) are the most inconsistently applied. The same word may be labelled "taboo" in one dictionary and merely "informal" in another.

## Problems with label-based marking

### The consistency problem

Empirical studies consistently show that different dictionaries assign different labels to the same words. Ojo (2011) found that "coolie" receives five different labels across five dictionaries: old-fashioned taboo (OALD), offensive (MED), old-fashioned (LDOCE), dated (WBD), and now rare or obsolete (NSOED). This inconsistency arises because:

1. **Register is gradient, not categorical.** Words sit on a continuum from very formal to very informal, but labels impose categorical boundaries. Where exactly does "informal" end and "slang" begin?
2. **Register is context-dependent.** A word may be formal in one domain but neutral in another. Dictionary labels can only capture the most typical register, not the full range.
3. **Register shifts over time.** Words drift toward informality (or formality) faster than dictionaries are revised. "Awesome" was once literary and is now thoroughly informal.
4. **Lexicographer judgement varies.** Without explicit corpus-based criteria, different editors make different calls based on their own intuitions and sociolinguistic backgrounds.

### The consultation gap

Research on dictionary use consistently shows that learners largely ignore usage labels:

- Nesi & Haill (2002) found that learners consulting OALD rarely attended to register labels, focusing instead on definitions and examples.
- Tono (2001) observed through eye-tracking that dictionary users spend the majority of their time on definitions and translation equivalents, with minimal attention to metalinguistic codes.
- Vrbinc & Vrbinc (2015) found that "the inclusion of diasystematic information largely depends on the type of dictionary and especially on its intended users," noting that multiple labels on a single entry can overwhelm learners.

This creates a paradox: the learners who most need register guidance (production-oriented intermediate learners) are the least likely to consult labels. Possible responses include embedding register information in definitions (the COBUILD approach), in examples (showing the word in context), or in notes (prose explanations of when to use the word).

### The bilingual gap

The consultation gap is even wider in bilingual dictionaries. Most bilingual dictionaries provide a translation equivalent and move on, treating register marking as optional. But bilingual equivalents frequently have different registers in the two languages — English "eat" is register-neutral, but Japanese has 食う (rough/masculine), 食べる (neutral), and 召し上がる (honorific), which all translate as "eat" but are emphatically not interchangeable.

## Register marking in Japanese dictionaries

### The stratal register system

Japanese has a distinctive register system rooted in its three vocabulary strata:

| Stratum | Script | Typical register | Example (for "after") |
|---------|--------|-----------------|----------------------|
| **Wago** (和語) | Hiragana/kanji | Everyday, spoken, emotionally warm | あと |
| **Kango** (漢語) | Kanji | Formal, written, academic, abstract | 以後 (いご) |
| **Gairaigo** (外来語) | Katakana | Modern, casual-trendy, technical | アフター |

This stratal dimension is largely absent from European-language lexicography and creates a register marking challenge unique to Japanese. Two words with identical denotation can differ sharply in register solely because one is wago and the other kango — and a learner who uses the kango variant in casual conversation will sound stilted, while one who uses the wago in formal writing will sound uneducated.

### The keigo dimension

Japanese grammaticalises politeness through the keigo (敬語) system, which operates independently of the wago/kango formality axis:

- **Sonkeigo** (尊敬語, respectful language) — elevates the subject's actions
- **Kenjōgo** (謙譲語, humble language) — lowers the speaker's actions
- **Teineigo** (丁寧語, polite language) — general politeness via です/ます endings

Ide (1989) characterised Japanese politeness as governed by **wakimae** (わきまえ, "discernment") — the sociopragmatically obligatory selection of linguistic forms based on social position, group membership (uchi/soto), and formality of situation. Unlike the Western volitional politeness model (Brown & Levinson 1987), wakimae is not a strategic choice but a social expectation. This means register information in a Japanese dictionary is not merely helpful but essential — using the wrong register is a social rule violation, not just a stylistic infelicity.

### How existing Japanese dictionaries mark register

**JMdict/EDICT** uses a rich set of entity codes for register:

| Code | Meaning |
|------|---------|
| `hon` | Honorific (sonkeigo) |
| `hum` | Humble (kenjōgo) |
| `pol` | Polite (teineigo) |
| `fam` | Familiar language |
| `col` | Colloquialism |
| `sl` | Slang |
| `vulg` | Vulgar |
| `arch` | Archaism |
| `obs` | Obsolete |
| `fem` / `male` | Gendered speech |
| `derog` | Derogatory |
| `joc` | Humorous |

This is one of the more complete register label sets in any dictionary, covering the keigo axis (hon/hum/pol), formality (fam/col/sl), social marking (vulg/derog), gender (fem/male), and temporal status (arch/obs). JMdict also distinguishes nine regional dialects (Kansai-ben, Kyuushuu-ben, etc.).

**Kenkyusha's New Japanese-English Dictionary** marks formality (formal, informal) and politeness (honorific, humble, polite) and distinguishes written from spoken usage, though the system is less fine-grained than JMdict's.

**Maebo (2012)** surveyed register labelling across Japanese learner's dictionaries, native-speaker dictionaries, and synonym dictionaries, finding that:

- Japanese learner dictionaries and native-speaker dictionaries use different register labels and assign them from different perspectives
- Learner dictionaries focus on formality (formal/informal) while native-speaker dictionaries focus on style (written/spoken/literary)
- Synonym dictionaries are the most useful for register, because they present register differences in context rather than through isolated labels
- The BCCWJ (Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese) can help standardise register assignment through frequency analysis across different text types (books, newspapers, internet text, spoken transcripts)

## Encoding strategies beyond labels

The label-based approach is only one way to convey register information. Research and practice suggest several complementary strategies:

### 1. Natural-language notes

Prose descriptions like "This word is more formal than X" or "Used mainly in written contexts" are more likely to be read and understood than abstract labels. This is the approach COBUILD pioneered for definitions and that je-dict-1 uses extensively for register (9,294 entries — 35.1% — contain register-relevant prose in their notes).

**Advantage**: flexible, can express gradience and context-dependence.
**Disadvantage**: not machine-readable, hard to ensure consistency across entries, takes space.

### 2. Register-contrastive examples

Showing the same meaning expressed at different register levels within or across entries:

> 食う is rough/masculine: 早く食えよ。 (Eat up already.)
> 食べる is neutral: 一緒に食べましょう。 (Let's eat together.)
> 召し上がる is honorific: どうぞ召し上がってください。 (Please help yourself.)

This approach leverages the finding (Maebo 2012) that presenting register information in context is more useful than listing isolated labels.

### 3. Structured fields

Machine-readable register fields enable filtering, search, and automated consistency checking. A multi-dimensional schema can capture the independent axes:

```
formality: formal | neutral | informal | vulgar
politeness: honorific | humble | polite | plain
style: written | spoken | literary | archaic | slang
domain: business | academic | technical | medical | legal | ...
```

**Advantage**: enables computational use, ensures structured coverage.
**Disadvantage**: imposes categorical boundaries on gradient phenomena, requires systematic population.

### 4. Cross-references

Linking register variants (食う ↔ 食べる ↔ 召し上がる) lets learners see the register landscape for a concept without needing labels at all. The link relationship itself carries register information if the entries are well-documented.

## Corpus-based register assignment

The BCCWJ and similar corpora enable empirical register classification. A word that appears overwhelmingly in internet forums and spoken transcripts but rarely in newspapers or academic texts is empirically informal, regardless of lexicographer intuition. Biber's (1988) multi-dimensional model of register variation — which identifies co-occurring linguistic features along dimensions like "Involved vs. Informational Production" — provides a theoretical foundation for computational register analysis.

For a Japanese dictionary, the BCCWJ's genre-tagged subcorpora (books, magazines, newspapers, government white papers, Yahoo! Answers, blogs) could in principle be used to assign register labels empirically: a word whose frequency peaks in Yahoo! Answers but is rare in white papers is likely colloquial/informal.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### Current state

je-dict-1 has register-related information in three forms, in decreasing order of prevalence:

1. **Prose notes** — 9,294 entries (35.1%) contain register-relevant keywords (formal, informal, casual, polite, keigo, honorific, humble) in their notes. This is the primary register information channel.
2. **metadata.tags.usage** — 45 entries have usage tags (`formal`, `literary`, `informal`, `archaic`, `spoken`, etc.)
3. **metadata.tags.register** — 35 entries have register tags (`formal`, `colloquial`, `literary`, `polite`, etc.), some with data integrity issues (single-character values from a string being treated as an iterable)

The structured register fields exist in the schema but are essentially unpopulated (< 0.3% coverage). The wiki's topics/register.md page documents a four-field schema (formality, politeness, style, domain) that has not yet been implemented at scale.

### What the research suggests

1. **Prose notes are the right primary channel.** The consultation gap research shows that learners ignore metalinguistic labels but read definitions and notes. je-dict-1's extensive prose register information (35.1% coverage) is more useful than the empty structured fields would be even if populated.

2. **The multi-axis schema design is sound.** Separating formality (casual → formal), politeness (plain → honorific), style (spoken/written/literary), and domain captures the independent dimensions of Japanese register. This aligns with Hausmann's diasystematic framework and is more complete than the single-axis labels used by most dictionaries.

3. **Register-contrastive cross-references are a high-value investment.** Linking 食う ↔ 食べる ↔ 召し上がる with register annotations in the cross-reference type conveys register information more effectively than labels. The existing cross-reference system (13,619 references) already supports this — the `keigo` reference type (90 instances) explicitly links keigo pairs.

4. **Stratal register awareness should be explicit.** The wago/kango/gairaigo register distinction is lexicographically unusual and highly relevant to Japanese learners. When near-synonyms differ primarily in register due to stratal origin, this should be noted explicitly. The gairaigo research page and topics/register.md both discuss this, but entry-level implementation remains sparse.

5. **Structured fields should be populated incrementally.** Rather than a massive backfill, adding register fields during routine polishing (when the entry is already being reviewed) avoids the consistency problems that arise from automated label assignment. The usage and register tags could be validated against an enumerated list to prevent the single-character data integrity issues seen in current entries.

6. **Example sentences should model register.** Register-appropriate examples are more effective than labels for teaching register (Frankenberg-Garcia 2014, Maebo 2012). A formal word should appear in formal example sentences; an informal word in casual examples. This aligns with the existing v2 quality standard on example progression.

### Priority actions

| Priority | Action | Channel |
|----------|--------|---------|
| High | Continue enriching prose register notes during polishing | Notes |
| High | Expand keigo cross-references (base ↔ honorific ↔ humble) | Cross-refs |
| Medium | Fix data integrity issues in existing register/usage tags | Structured |
| Medium | Add stratal register notes to near-synonym sets | Notes |
| Low | Systematically populate structured register fields | Structured |
| Low | Add register-based filtering to site search | UI |
