# Gairaigo: Loanwords in Japanese

**Last updated**: 2026-04-28

## Overview

Gairaigo (外来語, literally "words from outside") are loanwords in Japanese, predominantly borrowed from English but also from Portuguese, Dutch, German, French, and other languages. They form a distinct vocabulary stratum alongside native Japanese (和語 *wago*) and Sino-Japanese (漢語 *kango*), with unique phonological, orthographic, and semantic properties that create specific challenges for both learners and lexicographers.

As of 2026, gairaigo account for approximately 5% of tokens and 19% of types in the Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese (BCCWJ; NINJAL, 2017), with lower ratios in spoken language (~2% tokens, ~14% types in the Corpus of Everyday Japanese Conversation). In newspaper text, the proportion has risen to roughly 10%, with about 80% of those loanwords originating from English. The growth trend is clear: gairaigo represented about 3.5% of dictionary headwords in 1956 but had roughly doubled by the early 2000s (Loveday, 1996; Irwin, 2011).

## Phonological adaptation

When foreign words enter Japanese, they undergo systematic phonological adaptation to conform to Japanese phonotactics — the language's mora-timed structure requiring (with minor exceptions) open syllables of the form CV or V.

### Key adaptation processes

| Process | Example | Explanation |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| **Vowel epenthesis** | *strike* → ストライク (sutoraiku) | Vowels inserted to break consonant clusters and close final consonants |
| **Consonant substitution** | *think* → シンク (shinku); *video* → ビデオ (bideo) | English phonemes without Japanese equivalents are mapped to nearest Japanese consonants |
| **Vowel mapping** | *bus* → バス (basu); *ball* → ボール (booru) | English vowels mapped to closest Japanese vowels, often with length changes |
| **Mora obstruent insertion** | *bed* → ベッド (beddo); *cup* → カップ (kappu) | Gemination (っ) inserted to approximate final stops |
| **Truncation** | *television* → テレビ (terebi); *convenience store* → コンビニ (konbini) | Long loanwords are frequently shortened |

The default epenthetic vowel is /u/ after most consonants (e.g., *desk* → デスク desuku), but /o/ after /t/ and /d/ (e.g., *bed* → ベッド beddo), following the phonological patterns of the Japanese mora system. Irwin (2011) provides a comprehensive survey of these adaptation rules across loans from all major donor languages.

The default epenthetic vowel /u/ accounts for over 70% of epenthesis tokens (Irwin, 2011), with /o/ used after /t/ and /d/ to prevent these becoming /tsu/ and /dzu/, and /i/ appearing in older loans after final /k/ (explaining *steak* → ステーキ but newer *break* → ブレーク).

These adaptations can render the source word nearly unrecognizable. A three-syllable English word like *Christmas* becomes a five-mora Japanese word (クリスマス kurisumasu), and *McDonald's* becomes seven moras (マクドナルド makudonarudo). This phonological distance creates a significant barrier for learners trying to connect gairaigo to their source words.

### Adaptation inconsistencies

Older loanwords sometimes preserve non-standard adaptations reflecting the phonology of earlier Japanese or a different donor language. For example:
- *iron* → アイロン (airon, with /r/) — from the English pronunciation but adapted in an era when /l/ vs. /r/ was handled differently
- *energy* → エネルギー (enerugii) — from German *Energie*, not English
- *bread* → パン (pan) — from Portuguese *pão*

These historical layers add complexity, since learners cannot always assume an English source.

## Semantic change

Gairaigo frequently undergo semantic change after adoption into Japanese. Stanlaw (2004) classified the major types as restriction (narrowing), shift, extension (broadening), and redefinition. Research consistently finds that **semantic narrowing is the most common** pattern.

### Types of semantic change

**Narrowing** (most common): The gairaigo covers a subset of the source word's meaning.

| Gairaigo | English source | Japanese meaning | English meaning |
|----------|---------------|------------------|-----------------|
| マンション (manshon) | mansion | apartment building (mid-rise+) | large, grand house |
| ライス (raisu) | rice | boiled rice served on a plate, Western-style | rice in general |
| スマート (sumaato) | smart | slim, slender, stylish | intelligent |
| バイキング (baikingu) | Viking | all-you-can-eat buffet | Norse seafarer |
| クレーム (kurimu) | claim | complaint | assertion, demand |
| サービス (saabisu) | service | free extra, discount, freebie | act of serving |

**Broadening**: The gairaigo covers a wider range than the source.

| Gairaigo | English source | Japanese meaning | English meaning |
|----------|---------------|------------------|-----------------|
| カンニング (kanningu) | cunning | cheating on a test | sly, clever |

**Shift**: The meaning moves to a related but distinct concept.

| Gairaigo | English source | Japanese meaning | English meaning |
|----------|---------------|------------------|-----------------|
| ナイーブ (naiibu) | naive | sensitive, delicate (positive) | showing lack of experience (negative) |
| テンション (tenshon) | tension | energy level, excitement | stress, strain |

### Register and stylistic effects

Gairaigo often carry different connotations from their native Japanese or Sino-Japanese equivalents, even when the denotational meaning overlaps:

- **Modern/casual feel**: トラブル (toraburu) vs. 問題 (mondai) — both mean "trouble/problem," but the gairaigo sounds more casual and contemporary
- **Technical/specialized**: コンプライアンス (konpuraiansu) vs. 法令遵守 (hourei junshu) — both mean "compliance," but the gairaigo dominates business jargon
- **Euphemistic**: リストラ (risutora, from "restructuring") for layoffs, ハラスメント (harasumento) for various types of harassment
- **Fashion/consumer**: ショップ (shoppu) vs. 店 (mise) — the gairaigo sounds more upscale

Rebuck (2002) identified three main functions of gairaigo beyond simple denotation: (1) filling a genuine lexical gap for new concepts, (2) achieving a special effect — lending modernity, sophistication, or cosmopolitan flavor, and (3) serving as euphemisms — the foreignness of gairaigo can soften taboo or uncomfortable topics.

This register dimension means that even when a native Japanese word exists, the gairaigo is not a free synonym. Dictionaries must document these register differences explicitly.

### Public comprehension concerns

The proliferation of gairaigo in official and media communications has become a public policy concern. NINJAL established a committee (2002–2006) that identified 176 loanwords frequently used in government and media but poorly understood by the general public, publishing *Gairaigo Iikae Teian* (Proposals for Replacing Loanwords). Japanese government surveys found that 78.5% of respondents (2012) and 83.5% (2018) reported difficulty understanding loanword meanings — a growing problem as gairaigo adoption accelerates. This comprehension gap is relevant for learner dictionaries because it shows that even native speakers struggle with gairaigo, particularly older speakers and those in non-technical fields.

## Wasei-eigo: Japanese-coined "English"

Wasei-eigo (和製英語, "Japanese-made English") are words that look and sound English but were coined in Japan, either from English morphemes in novel combinations or with meanings that do not exist in English. They are a special category of false friends.

### Common wasei-eigo examples

| Wasei-eigo | Apparent English | Actual meaning |
|-----------|-----------------|----------------|
| サラリーマン (sarariiman) | salary man | office worker, white-collar employee |
| OL (oo-eru) | office lady | female office worker |
| ナイター (naitaa) | nighter | night game (baseball) |
| スキンシップ (sukinshippu) | skinship | physical affection, bonding through touch |
| ハンドルキーパー (handorukiipaa) | handle keeper | designated driver |
| コンセント (konsento) | consent(?) | electrical outlet (from *concentric plug*) |
| ノートパソコン (nooto pasokon) | note personal computer | laptop |
| フリーダイヤル (furii daiyaru) | free dial | toll-free number |
| ガソリンスタンド (gasorin sutando) | gasoline stand | gas station |

Wasei-eigo poses a double challenge: L1-English learners assume the English meaning applies, and L1-Japanese speakers assume the gairaigo form works in English. Both assumptions fail.

## L2 acquisition: the cognate advantage and its limits

### The cognate advantage (Daulton)

Frank Daulton's research program, culminating in *Japan's Built-in Lexicon of English-based Loanwords* (2008), demonstrated that Japanese learners of English enjoy a significant **cognate advantage**: English words that have gairaigo counterparts in Japanese are recognized and recalled more easily than non-cognate words. Daulton found that 45.5% of the 3,000 most frequent BNC (British National Corpus) word families have correspondences to common Japanese loanwords, with the proportion even higher (54.8%) for the first 1,000 most frequent words. In total, he identified approximately 734 high-frequency English headword groups that correlate with Japanese loanword cognates, arguing that this "built-in lexicon" gives Japanese learners a head start in English vocabulary acquisition.

The advantage works in both directions. For English-speaking learners of Japanese, gairaigo can provide "free" vocabulary — words whose meaning is transparently derivable from the English source. An English speaker encountering テーブル (teeburu, "table"), コーヒー (koohii, "coffee"), or コンピューター (konpyuutaa, "computer") can guess the meaning immediately.

### Limits of the cognate advantage

The advantage has significant limitations:

1. **Phonological distance**: The adaptation process can make source words unrecognizable. English speakers often cannot identify the English source of ストライキ (sutoraiki, "strike/labor action"), アルバイト (arubaito, from German *Arbeit*, "part-time job"), or エレベーター (erebeetaa, "elevator"). Nakao (2020) found that learners' ability to identify source words drops sharply as phonological distance increases.

2. **Semantic false friends**: As documented above, many gairaigo have shifted in meaning. A learner who assumes マンション means "mansion" or ナイーブ means "naive" will be seriously misled. Simon-Maeda (2002) found that negative transfer from false-friend gairaigo was a persistent source of errors even among advanced learners.

3. **L1 asymmetry**: The cognate advantage is largely limited to speakers of English (and to some extent other European languages). Learners of Japanese whose L1 is Chinese, Korean, or another Asian language cannot leverage gairaigo the way English speakers can — though Chinese-speaking learners have their own cognate advantage through kango (Sino-Japanese compounds).

4. **Production difficulties**: Even when learners can recognize a gairaigo, producing it correctly — with proper vowel epenthesis, mora timing, and pitch accent — is difficult. English speakers tend to pronounce gairaigo with English phonology, which is often not understood by Japanese speakers (Hatanaka & Pannell, 2016).

5. **Overreliance and avoidance**: Some learners overuse gairaigo as a crutch, producing katakana versions of English words that don't exist as established gairaigo, while others avoid gairaigo out of a sense that "real Japanese" should use wago or kango.

## Dictionary treatment approaches

### How existing dictionaries handle gairaigo

| Dictionary | Approach |
|-----------|----------|
| **JMdict/EDICT** | Marks wasei-eigo entries with a `wasei` tag; includes source language annotation for many loanwords |
| **Kenkyusha New J-E** | Provides etymology (source language and word); occasionally notes semantic shift |
| **Wisdom J-E** | Labels source language; includes usage notes on false friends for common cases |
| **OALD/LDOCE** (for English side) | Do not systematically address gairaigo; occasional "in Japanese" notes |

### What dictionaries should document for gairaigo

Based on the research literature, effective dictionary treatment of gairaigo should include:

1. **Source language and original word**: Not all gairaigo come from English. Indicating the source language (English, Portuguese, Dutch, German, French, etc.) and the original form helps learners connect (or correctly disconnect) the gairaigo from its source.

2. **Semantic shift warnings**: When the gairaigo meaning differs significantly from the source word, the dictionary should explicitly flag this. "Unlike English *naive*, ナイーブ in Japanese has a positive connotation of sensitivity and delicacy" is the kind of note that prevents persistent errors.

3. **Register positioning**: Documenting when the gairaigo carries a different register from its native Japanese synonym — more modern, more casual, more technical, more euphemistic — is essential for production.

4. **Wasei-eigo labeling**: Marking wasei-eigo explicitly warns learners that these forms will not be understood in English, preventing a common class of transfer errors.

5. **Pronunciation guidance**: For L1-English learners, noting the relationship between the English pronunciation and the Japanese katakana rendering helps with both recognition and production. For all learners, pitch accent information is valuable since gairaigo pitch patterns are not always predictable.

## Quantitative analysis: gairaigo in je-dict-1

The dictionary currently contains **1,588 pure katakana headwords** (6.1% of entries) and **337 mixed katakana-plus-kanji/hiragana headwords**, for a total of approximately **1,925 entries** (7.5%) involving loanword elements. This is somewhat above the token frequency of gairaigo in running text (~5%) but well below the type frequency (~19%), suggesting room for expansion.

### Productive compounds

The 337 mixed entries illustrate the productive compounding between gairaigo and native/Sino-Japanese elements:
- **Wago + gairaigo**: 赤ワイン (aka-wain, "red wine"), 生ゴミ (nama-gomi, "food waste")
- **Kango + gairaigo**: 監視カメラ (kanshi-kamera, "surveillance camera"), 就労ビザ (shuurou-biza, "work visa")
- **Gairaigo + wago/kango**: テレビ会議 (terebi-kaigi, "video conference"), セーラー服 (seeraa-fuku, "sailor uniform")

These hybrid compounds pose particular lexicographic challenges: they are fully opaque to learners who know neither component, but may be partially transparent to learners who know one.

### Current limitations

The dictionary does not currently include:
- **Source language fields**: No metadata indicating the donor language
- **Etymology fields**: No record of the original word form
- **False-friend warnings**: Semantic shift documentation relies on the free-text notes field rather than a structured mechanism
- **Wasei-eigo tags**: No systematic label distinguishing genuine loanwords from Japanese coinages

## Implications for je-dict-1

### Short-term: better notes for existing gairaigo entries

The most impactful improvement is adding explicit **semantic shift notes** to gairaigo entries where the Japanese meaning differs from the English source. A systematic pass through the ~1,600 katakana entries could flag and annotate the most egregious false friends. This requires no schema changes — it is purely a content improvement using the existing notes field.

### Medium-term: source language metadata

Adding a `source_language` field to the entry schema (or a semantic tag like `gairaigo:english`, `gairaigo:german`, etc.) would enable structured browsing, filtering, and study-list generation. Combined with the existing tag-based search, this would let learners explore "all German-origin loanwords" or "all wasei-eigo" as coherent groups.

### Medium-term: false-friend annotation

A structured `false_friend_warning` field or a standardized note block format (e.g., "**FALSE FRIEND**: Unlike English *X*, Japanese *Y* means...") would make semantic shift warnings consistent and searchable. Given the error-prevention value documented in the research literature, this is higher-value than most other polishing tasks for gairaigo entries.

### Long-term: cognate-aware features

For a dictionary serving learners from multiple L1 backgrounds, cognate information is inherently L1-specific. However, since the primary audience includes English-speaking learners, English-source gairaigo could carry:
- A "recognizable from English" difficulty rating
- Pronunciation comparison notes (English vs. Japanese rendering)
- Links between gairaigo and their English "false friend" counterpart when applicable

### Expansion priorities

The gap between the dictionary's 6.1% gairaigo coverage and the 19% type frequency suggests that many gairaigo remain unrepresented. Expansion should prioritize:
1. **High-frequency gairaigo** not yet in the dictionary (consult BCCWJ frequency data)
2. **False-friend gairaigo** — the words most likely to cause errors deserve entries even if individually low-frequency
3. **Wasei-eigo** — the most opaque category for all learners
