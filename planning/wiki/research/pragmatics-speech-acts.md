# Pragmatics and Speech Acts in Bilingual Learner Dictionaries

**Last updated**: 2026-05-25

## Overview

Pragmatics — how context shapes meaning beyond literal semantics — is one of the most significant gaps in bilingual dictionary coverage. Learners who achieve grammatical accuracy still produce pragmatically inappropriate language, and dictionaries bear part of the blame: they tell users *what* a word means but rarely *when*, *to whom*, or *why* a speaker would choose it. This page surveys pragmatic information in dictionaries, speech act theory as it applies to Japanese, the well-documented gap between grammatical and pragmatic competence in L2 learners, and how bilingual learner dictionaries can bridge that gap.

## Pragmatic information in dictionaries

### What pragmatic information covers

Pragmatic information in a dictionary entry answers questions that definition and grammar cannot:

| Dimension | Question answered | Example |
|-----------|-------------------|---------|
| **Register/formality** | How formal is this expression? | めしあがる is honorific; 食う is vulgar |
| **Discourse function** | What does this expression *do* in conversation? | あのう signals hesitation and social rapport |
| **Illocutionary force** | What speech act does this perform? | ちょっと... conventionally performs a refusal |
| **Contextual restriction** | Who says this to whom, when? | お疲れ様 is said to peers/subordinates at day's end |
| **Connotation/affect** | What attitude does this convey? | わざわざ carries positive or negative prosody depending on context |

### How dictionaries encode pragmatics

Dictionaries use at least four mechanisms:

1. **Usage labels** — formalized tags like *informal*, *formal*, *slang*, *literary*, *spoken*, *written*. These are the coarsest tool: a label like "informal" covers everything from casual conversation to crude slang.

2. **Usage notes** — prose explanations within entries that capture nuances labels cannot. COBUILD pioneered "full sentence definitions" that incorporate pragmatic context by specifying who says something to whom and why (Sinclair 1987).

3. **Example sentences** — well-chosen examples implicitly convey pragmatic information by showing appropriate contexts of use. This is often the richest pragmatic channel in a dictionary, but only if examples are designed with pragmatic goals in mind rather than merely illustrating grammar.

4. **Dedicated panels** — some dictionaries use boxed notes for register contrasts, cultural notes, or communication guidance, separating pragmatic information visually from definitional content.

### Key works on pragmatics in lexicography

**Yong & Peng (2007)**, *Bilingual Lexicography from a Communicative Perspective*, treat the bilingual dictionary as a system of intercultural communication. They argue that providing entries with "an accurate, consistent labeling system for words with register and stylistic restrictions" is essential for integrating pragmatics into bilingual lexicography.

**Zgusta (1988)**, in "Pragmatics, Lexicography, and Dictionaries of English" (*World Englishes*), examined the influence of pragmatics on lexicographic practice, arguing that dictionaries need to go beyond denotative meaning to capture indexicality (deixis), cultural setting, and situational equivalence.

**Atkins & Rundell (2008)**, *The Oxford Guide to Practical Lexicography*, cover how to analyze corpus data for semantic, grammatical, and pragmatic components of entries, including the application of prototype theory and frame semantics to entry design.

## Speech act theory and dictionaries

### The Austin/Searle taxonomy

Speech act theory (Austin 1962, Searle 1979) classifies utterances by what they *do*, not just what they *say*. Searle's five basic illocutionary types are:

| Type | Function | Japanese examples |
|------|----------|-------------------|
| **Assertives** | Committing to truth of something | 〜と思います, 〜そうです |
| **Directives** | Getting hearer to do something | てください, 〜てもらえますか |
| **Commissives** | Committing speaker to future action | 〜ます (volitional promise), 〜てあげる |
| **Expressives** | Expressing psychological state | すみません, おめでとう, お疲れ様 |
| **Declarations** | Bringing about a change in reality | 宣言する, 命名する |

Searle himself observed that "nothing rules out the possibility of there being illocutionary acts that are not named by a verb either in a particular language such as Swahili or Bengali, or indeed in any language at all." This is directly relevant to Japanese-English dictionaries: many Japanese expressions perform speech acts that have no single English performative verb equivalent.

### Japanese speech acts that resist translation

Several common Japanese speech acts present particular challenges for bilingual dictionaries because their pragmatic force has no neat English counterpart:

**Refusals (断り)**: Japanese speakers avoid explicit "no" in daily interaction. Instead they use indirect strategies — expressions of regret (残念ですが), vague reasons (ちょっと用事がありまして...), positive constructions, or trailing-off silence. The speaker anticipates that the hearer will understand the refusal without it being stated. Research shows that Japanese speakers use direct refusals less frequently than American English speakers (Takahashi & Beebe 1987). A dictionary entry for ちょっと that only gives "a little, somewhat" misses the word's most socially important function.

**Requests (依頼)**: Japanese requests employ multiple politeness levels via verb forms and honorifics, with imposition calibrated through linguistic indirectness. The gradient from てください to お〜ください to ていただけませんか represents not just grammar but a pragmatic scale of deference. Dictionary entries for request forms need to explain this gradient, not just parse the grammar.

**Back-channeling (相槌)**: Aizuchi responses are far more prevalent in Japanese than in English. They serve multiple functions beyond simple acknowledgment: continuation signals, understanding markers, empathy expressions. L2 learners tend to transfer their native backchannel strategies, leading to mismatched conversational rhythms. Entries for そうですか, なるほど, and へえ should describe their discourse functions, not just translate them.

**Untranslatable expressives**: よろしくお願いします expresses gratitude, humility, cooperation, and future goodwill simultaneously — it has "no direct translation." お疲れ様 performs an acknowledgment-of-effort function with no English equivalent. すみません functions in at least seven pragmatic roles (apology, gratitude, attention-getting, acknowledging imposition, and more). These expressions need extensive pragmatic notes, not just approximate glosses.

## Japanese pragmatics challenges for L2 learners

### Indirectness

Japanese communication prefers indirectness to preserve harmony. Key indirectness mechanisms that learners struggle with:

- **ちょっと... (trailing off)**: A conventional indirect refusal ("That's a bit..."), not a statement about degree. Learners who know only the literal meaning miss the pragmatic force entirely.
- **〜んですが (explanatory の + contrastive が)**: A conventional preface to requests, complaints, or bad news. The trailing が signals that the speaker is leaving the conclusion unstated out of politeness.
- **Sentence-trailing particles (けど, が)**: Leaving sentences unfinished is a deliberate pragmatic strategy, not incomplete speech. The speaker invites the hearer to complete the implication.

### Sentence-final particles

These particles carry pragmatic rather than propositional meaning:

- **ね**: Hearer-oriented. Seeks agreement or softens statements. Proposes that the emerging proposition should be treated as shared ground (McCready & Davis 2020).
- **よ**: Speaker-oriented. Asserts new or potentially contested information. Functions include: appealing to the listener's recognition, correcting the listener's assumption, and urging acceptance of the speaker's view.
- **な**: Self-reflective or mildly assertive (with masculine associations in some varieties). Can function as a softened assertion or self-directed musing.
- **さ**: Marks casual assertion; conveys an offhand or dismissive tone.

Dictionary entries for these particles need discourse-function descriptions, not grammatical glosses. An entry for ね that says "sentence-final particle indicating agreement" misses most of what a learner needs to know.

### Honorific pragmatics beyond grammar

Keigo is not just a grammatical system but a reflection of social relationships. The pragmatic challenges include:

- **Uchi/soto switching (内/外)**: When speaking to an outsider about your boss, the boss becomes part of your in-group, so you use humble language for their actions — even though internally you use honorific language for the same person. This contextual switching has no English parallel.
- **Over-politeness as social error**: Being too polite can create awkwardness or distance — "like you're treating them as a customer instead of a peer." Dictionary entries for keigo forms should note when *not* to use them.
- **Wakimae (弁え/discernment)**: Ide (1989) and Matsumoto (1989) argued that Japanese politeness operates on fundamentally different principles from Brown & Levinson's (1987) Western face-management model. Rather than volitional strategies to mitigate face-threat, Japanese politeness involves *wakimae* — social norms that almost automatically determine linguistic behavior based on the speaker's reading of the social context.

### Discourse markers and hedging

- **あのう**: Functions as a hesitation marker but also has a social-pragmatic function similar to ね, indexing interpersonal rapport.
- **ええと**: Not simply equivalent to English "um/uh" — carries different pragmatic implications about the speaker's processing.
- **Exemplifying markers as hedges**: たり, とか, and など can be exploited for semantic approximation and pragmatic hedging, creating effects of vagueness and politeness.

## The pragmatic competence gap

### Grammatical competence precedes pragmatic competence

A consistent finding across SLA research is that even learners with sufficient grammatical and lexical knowledge often cannot produce pragmatically appropriate language. This asymmetry has been documented extensively:

- **Bardovi-Harlig (1998)** demonstrated in "Do Language Learners Recognize Pragmatic Violations?" that instructed L2 learners are often aware of grammatical errors but not pragmatic inappropriateness. The pragmatic dimension is invisible to learners who have not been trained to notice it.
- **Bardovi-Harlig (2013)**, in "Developing L2 Pragmatics" (*Language Learning*), surveyed the field, covering pragmatics task design, implicit/explicit knowledge, measuring pragmatic development, the grammar-lexicon-pragmatics interface, and environmental effects on development.
- **Thomas (1983)** distinguished *pragmalinguistic failure* (misusing conventionalized forms — "fairly easy to overcome" through instruction) from *sociopragmatic failure* (stemming from "cross-culturally different perceptions of what constitutes appropriate linguistic behaviour" — much harder to address). This distinction is directly relevant to dictionary design: pragmalinguistic information (which form to use) can be encoded in entries, while sociopragmatic information (when and why) requires richer contextual notes and examples.

### The role of noticing

**Schmidt (1993)** applied his Noticing Hypothesis to pragmatics in "Consciousness, Learning and Interlanguage Pragmatics." His central claim: learners must consciously notice pragmatic features in the input before they can acquire them. For pragmatics, this means noticing register markers, indirectness strategies, and contextual appropriateness — features that are easy to miss because they carry no propositional content.

This has direct dictionary implications. If noticing is necessary for pragmatic acquisition, then dictionaries that make pragmatic information explicit and salient serve as tools for pragmatic development. An entry that merely translates ちょっと as "a little" does not trigger noticing of its refusal function. An entry that explicitly describes the refusal function — and provides examples showing it in action — does.

### Kasper & Rose (2002)

Their monograph *Pragmatic Development in a Second Language* was the first book-length treatment of L2 pragmatic acquisition. Key findings relevant to dictionary design:

- Explicit instruction in speech acts can significantly improve learners' pragmatic performance
- Both explicit and implicit teaching methods can be effective, but learners need *exposure* to pragmatic patterns
- Acquisitional sequences exist — some pragmatic features are acquired before others, suggesting a principled ordering for dictionary coverage

## How major dictionaries handle pragmatics

| Dictionary | Approach | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|-----------|----------|-----------|------------|
| **COBUILD** | Full-sentence definitions encoding who/whom/why; grammar column with usage | Most pragmatically explicit of the English learner dictionaries | Sentence definitions can be wordy |
| **LDOCE** | Register Notes boxes; systematic register labeling; CDV definitions | Good register contrast coverage | Labels too coarse; discourse function underrepresented |
| **Kenkyusha** (J-E) | Formality/register markers; natural-sounding examples in 5th edition | Systematic labeling; rich exemplification | Pragmatic function rarely explained for discourse markers |
| **Wisdom** (E-J) | ~90,000 entries with extensive corpus-based examples | Implicit pragmatic info through rich exemplification | Relies on examples rather than explicit pragmatic notes |

### Common shortcomings across dictionaries

- Labels applied inconsistently across entries
- Register labels too coarse ("informal" covers casual to crude)
- Pragmatic information buried in long entries rather than highlighted
- Discourse function rarely explained — entries for particles and discourse markers get grammatical descriptions rather than pragmatic ones
- Cultural context for speech acts mostly absent
- The same form performing different speech acts in different contexts is poorly represented

## Implications for je-dict-1

### What the dictionary already does well

je-dict-1's entry design already provides several pragmatic channels:

1. **Register fields**: The schema includes formality, politeness, style, and domain tags — a richer register system than most comparable dictionaries.
2. **Usage notes**: The USAGE and SIMILAR WORDS sections in notes naturally carry pragmatic information when well-written.
3. **Example sentences**: With over 104,500 examples, there is substantial implicit pragmatic input — but its pragmatic value depends on whether examples are designed to show contextual appropriateness or merely grammatical patterns.
4. **Cross-references for keigo**: The cross-reference system links plain/honorific/humble forms, enabling learners to navigate the politeness gradient.

### Areas for improvement

**1. Discourse function notes for functional expressions.** Entries for particles (ね, よ, な), discourse markers (あのう, ええと, まあ), and back-channeling expressions (そうですか, なるほど, へえ) should lead with discourse function rather than grammatical category. An entry for ね should describe its pragmatic roles (seeking agreement, softening assertions, building rapport) before or instead of calling it a "sentence-final particle."

**2. Speech act labels and cross-references.** Expressions that perform the same speech act should be cross-referenced as a group:
- Request expressions: てください → ていただけませんか → お〜ください → 〜てもらえる?
- Refusal strategies: ちょっと... → 〜んですが → 残念ですが → 申し訳ありませんが
- Apology gradient: ごめん → ごめんなさい → すみません → 申し訳ございません

This would enable speech-act-based browsing — a learner looking up "how to refuse politely in Japanese" could navigate the full gradient.

**3. Indirect speech act marking.** For expressions whose pragmatic function differs from their literal meaning (ちょっと as refusal, 〜てもいいですか as request, 結構です as refusal), the entry should explicitly flag the indirect speech act. A "pragmatic note" or "speech act" section could accomplish this.

**4. Pragmatic failure warnings.** Common cases of pragmalinguistic transfer should be flagged. For すみません, note that English speakers under-apologize in Japanese contexts. For よろしくお願いします, explain that attempting a literal English translation ("please treat me well") sounds odd — the expression needs pragmatic, not semantic, translation.

**5. Contextual framing in examples.** Following Schmidt's Noticing Hypothesis, examples for pragmatically rich expressions should include contextual framing — who is speaking to whom, in what situation — to make pragmatic features salient. For keigo entries, examples should show the social relationship, not just the grammatical form.

**6. Register label consistency.** The existing register fields (formality, politeness, style, domain) should be applied more systematically. Currently, many entries lack register tags that would help learners gauge appropriateness. A polishing pass focused on register labeling would have high pragmatic value.

### Priority candidates for pragmatic enrichment

Based on the research, these entry types would benefit most from pragmatic annotation:

1. **Sentence-final particles** (ね, よ, な, さ, わ, ぞ) — discourse function is the primary information learners need
2. **Discourse markers** (あのう, ええと, まあ, つまり, やっぱり) — function varies by context
3. **Keigo expressions** — social logic is more important than grammatical form
4. **Indirect refusal/request expressions** — pragmatic force differs from literal meaning
5. **Culturally specific speech acts** (お疲れ様, よろしくお願いします, いただきます) — require cultural-pragmatic notes, not just translations
6. **Back-channeling expressions** — frequency and function differ markedly from English equivalents
