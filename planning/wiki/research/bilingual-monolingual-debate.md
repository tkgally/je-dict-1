# The Bilingual vs. Monolingual Dictionary Debate

**Last updated**: 2026-05-30

## Overview

For decades, applied linguists and language teachers debated whether learners should use monolingual (L2→L2) or bilingual (L1↔L2) dictionaries. The traditional position — that monolingual dictionaries promote deeper L2 processing and should be preferred — was long treated as pedagogical orthodoxy. From the 1990s onward, empirical research systematically challenged this view, demonstrating that bilingual dictionaries are effective, strongly preferred by learners, and in many cases produce better learning outcomes. The emergence of the **bilingualized dictionary** (combining L2 definitions with L1 translations) further complicated the binary framing. Today, the consensus recognizes that dictionary type interacts with proficiency level, task type, and language pair, and that no single type is universally superior.

This page matters for je-dict-1 because the dictionary sits at an unusual point in the design space: it is bilingual in direction (Japanese→English) but includes extensive L2 context (Japanese example sentences, collocations, and patterns) alongside English explanations. Understanding the research on dictionary types clarifies why this hybrid design serves intermediate learners well.

## The traditional case for monolingual dictionaries

### Origins of the orthodoxy

The preference for monolingual learner dictionaries (MLDs) emerged from the communicative language teaching (CLT) movement of the 1970s–1980s, which emphasized maximal L2 exposure and minimizing L1 reliance. The argument had several strands:

1. **Immersion benefit**: Using an L2-only dictionary forces the learner to process meaning through L2, strengthening L2-internal connections in the mental lexicon rather than routing through L1 translation equivalents.

2. **Richer information**: MLDs like the Oxford Advanced Learner's Dictionary (OALD, first edition 1948), Longman Dictionary of Contemporary English (LDOCE, first edition 1978), and Collins COBUILD (1987) provide extensive contextual information — definitions using controlled defining vocabularies, example sentences, grammatical codes, usage notes, and collocational patterns — that most bilingual dictionaries lack.

3. **Avoiding false equivalence**: Bilingual dictionaries can mislead by suggesting that L1 and L2 words are interchangeable when they are not (see [Translation Equivalence](translation-equivalence.md)). Monolingual definitions, by describing meaning rather than translating it, avoid this trap.

4. **Teacher and curriculum endorsement**: Major EFL curricula and examination boards (particularly in the British EFL tradition) explicitly recommended monolingual dictionaries for intermediate and advanced learners. Many teachers prohibited bilingual dictionary use in class.

### The Big Four

The four major English MLDs — OALD, LDOCE, COBUILD, and the Macmillan English Dictionary (MED) — represent perhaps the most sophisticated learner dictionary tradition in any language. Their innovations (controlled defining vocabularies, full-sentence definitions, corpus-driven examples, grammatical coding) set quality standards that bilingual dictionaries rarely matched. This quality differential reinforced the perception that monolingual dictionaries were inherently better tools.

The problem was confounding dictionary *type* with dictionary *quality*. The Big Four were better not because they were monolingual but because they had large editorial teams, corpus data, and decades of iterative improvement. Bilingual dictionaries of comparable investment (e.g., Kenkyusha's New Japanese-English Dictionary) could match or exceed them in coverage and accuracy.

## Empirical evidence on dictionary type effectiveness

### Comprehension and retention studies

**Laufer & Hadar (1997)** conducted one of the most cited studies comparing dictionary types. They tested 123 Israeli EFL learners at three proficiency levels using monolingual (English), bilingual (English-Hebrew), and bilingualized dictionaries on a reading comprehension task. Key findings:

- At **lower intermediate** level: bilingual and bilingualized dictionaries produced significantly better comprehension than monolingual dictionaries.
- At **upper intermediate** level: bilingualized dictionaries produced the best comprehension scores; bilingual were second; monolingual were lowest.
- At **advanced** level: no significant difference between types.
- Across all levels, monolingual dictionaries never outperformed bilingual ones.

The authors concluded that "monolingual dictionaries are not the most effective dictionaries for comprehension of L2 texts" and that "for all learners below the advanced level, bilingual or bilingualised entries are more helpful."

**Bogaards (1996)** investigated the effect of dictionary type on vocabulary retention with 297 French learners of English. Learners looked up target words in monolingual, bilingual, or bilingualized dictionaries and were tested on retention one week later. Bilingual dictionaries produced the highest retention rates for translation-based recall, while bilingualized dictionaries performed best on recognition tasks. Monolingual dictionaries showed no advantage on any measure.

**Lew (2004)** tested Polish learners of English across multiple tasks (receptive and productive) using monolingual and bilingual dictionaries. Bilingual dictionaries produced better performance across all task types, including productive tasks where monolingual dictionaries might be expected to have an advantage.

### Preference and behavior studies

**Atkins & Varantola (1997)** conducted a large-scale EURALEX user study observing how learners actually use dictionaries. They found that learners overwhelmingly preferred bilingual dictionaries for initial lookup, even when monolingual dictionaries were available. Many learners used a two-step strategy: look up the word bilingually first, then consult a monolingual dictionary for additional detail.

**Thumb (2004)** studied Hong Kong university students using bilingualized English-Chinese dictionaries. She found that most learners relied primarily on the L1 translation for quick confirmation of meaning, then selectively consulted the L2 definition for additional information when the translation was ambiguous or unfamiliar. The L1 translation served as an efficient anchor, not a crutch.

**Tono (2001)**, in extensive studies of Japanese EFL learners, found a strong bilingual preference regardless of proficiency level. Even advanced Japanese learners preferred English-Japanese dictionaries to English-English ones. He attributed this partly to the linguistic distance between Japanese and English: when the L2 is structurally very different from the L1, monolingual definitions are harder to parse and bilingual entries are correspondingly more efficient.

### The role of proficiency

Research consistently shows that proficiency mediates the bilingual-monolingual relationship:

| Proficiency | Typical pattern |
|-------------|----------------|
| **Beginner** | Monolingual dictionaries are largely unusable; bilingual is the only viable option |
| **Lower intermediate** | Bilingual dictionaries significantly outperform monolingual |
| **Upper intermediate** | Bilingualized dictionaries often outperform both pure types |
| **Advanced** | Differences diminish; learners can use either effectively |

This pattern has been replicated across multiple language pairs and experimental designs. The critical insight is that the proficiency threshold for effective monolingual dictionary use is higher than most curricula assume.

## The bilingualized dictionary

### What it is

A bilingualized (or semi-bilingual) dictionary provides both an L2 definition and an L1 translation equivalent for each sense. The concept was developed primarily by Ari Kernerman, whose company produced bilingualized versions of major MLDs (the *Password* series, Kernerman Semi-Bilingual Dictionaries). The Oxford Student's Dictionary for Hebrew Speakers (1985) was an early influential example.

The key insight is that L1 translations and L2 definitions serve complementary functions:
- The **L1 translation** provides rapid, confident meaning identification — the learner immediately knows what the word means.
- The **L2 definition** provides nuance, scope, and contextual understanding — the learner sees how the meaning is bounded and used.

### Empirical support

**Laufer & Melamed (1994)** compared bilingualized and monolingual dictionary use in a vocabulary learning task. Bilingualized dictionaries produced significantly better retention scores on both immediate and delayed tests. The combination of L1 anchor and L2 elaboration appeared to create stronger memory traces than L2 definition alone.

**Laufer & Kimmel (1997)** studied *how* learners used bilingualized dictionaries, finding that the format accommodated diverse individual preferences. Some learners relied mainly on L1 translations; others used the L2 definition as their primary source; many used both. No learner behavior pattern corresponded to the "worse learning" that monolingual advocates feared from L1 availability.

**Nakamura (2000)** studied Japanese learners using bilingualized English dictionaries and found similar patterns to the Israeli and Hong Kong studies: the bilingualized format outperformed both pure monolingual and pure bilingual dictionaries for comprehension tasks, and learners valued the ability to cross-check between the L1 translation and L2 definition.

## Cognitive and psycholinguistic perspectives

### The Revised Hierarchical Model

**Kroll & Stewart (1994)** proposed the Revised Hierarchical Model (RHM) of bilingual lexical access. The model posits:

- L2 words are initially linked to L1 translation equivalents (strong L2→L1 connections) rather than directly to conceptual representations.
- Over time, L2 words develop direct conceptual links, but L1 mediation never fully disappears.
- Translation from L2 to L1 is faster and more fluent than translation from L1 to L2, because the L2→L1 lexical links are stronger.

The RHM has a clear implication for dictionary design: since L2 words are stored in relation to L1 equivalents (especially at lower proficiency levels), providing L1 translations aligns with how the lexicon is actually organized. Forcing learners to access meaning exclusively through L2 definitions works against the grain of their mental lexicon.

### De Groot's distributed model

**De Groot (1992, 1993)** proposed a distributed feature model where the degree of L1–L2 conceptual overlap varies by word type:
- **Concrete nouns** (table, dog, water) share more conceptual features across languages and are easier to translate.
- **Abstract words** (freedom, justice, mood) share fewer conceptual features and have more language-specific semantic boundaries.
- **Cognates** share both form and meaning features, creating stronger interlingual links.

This model predicts that bilingual dictionaries work well for concrete vocabulary (where L1 translations map closely onto L2 meanings) but may be insufficient for abstract vocabulary (where the L1 translation creates a false impression of equivalence). For abstract words, additional L2 definition and example context is needed — exactly what bilingualized dictionaries and rich bilingual dictionaries like je-dict-1 provide.

### L1 activation during L2 processing

A large body of psycholinguistic research demonstrates that the L1 is always activated during L2 processing, even in highly proficient bilinguals (Dijkstra & Van Heuven 2002; Thierry & Wu 2007). Learners cannot "turn off" their L1 when using a monolingual dictionary — they inevitably translate L2 definitions into L1 internally. The question is not whether L1 is involved but whether it is involved efficiently.

When a learner reads an L2 monolingual definition, they must:
1. Parse the L2 syntax of the definition
2. Look up any unknown words in the definition (recursive lookup)
3. Construct a meaning representation from the definition
4. Internally map that representation to L1 concepts

When a learner reads an L1 translation, they skip to step 4 directly. The cognitive savings are substantial, especially for lower-proficiency learners. The risk is that step 4 alone may be imprecise (the L1 word may not capture the full L2 meaning). This is why the combination — L1 translation for rapid anchoring plus L2 context for precision — is most effective.

## Lookup direction: L2→L1 vs. L1→L2

Dictionary type intersects with lookup direction in important ways:

### Decoding (L2→L1)

When a learner encounters an unknown Japanese word and wants to know what it means, the natural lookup is L2→L1 (Japanese→English). This is the **receptive** use case. Bilingual dictionaries excel here because they provide the immediate L1 equivalent the learner needs. Monolingual L2 dictionaries require the learner to understand an L2 definition of the unknown L2 word — a bootstrapping problem when the word is genuinely unknown.

### Encoding (L1→L2)

When a learner wants to express a concept in Japanese and needs the right word, the lookup is L1→L2 (English→Japanese). This is the **productive** use case. Here, bilingual dictionaries can be misleading if they offer multiple L2 equivalents without guidance on which to use in context. Monolingual L2 dictionaries can be more helpful for production because they describe how words are used (collocations, register, grammatical patterns) rather than just what they mean.

### je-dict-1's position

je-dict-1 is primarily a J→E dictionary (decoding direction), but its rich notes, collocations, and example sentences also support E→J production. A learner who finds an entry through English search gets the translation equivalent plus extensive L2 context showing how the word is actually used — combining the decoding efficiency of a bilingual dictionary with the productive support of a monolingual one.

## Japanese-specific considerations

### Linguistic distance

Japanese and English are among the most distant major language pairs (different language families, different scripts, different typological properties). This distance amplifies the problems with monolingual dictionaries for English-speaking learners of Japanese:

- A monolingual Japanese dictionary (国語辞典) requires native-level reading ability in Japanese, including command of thousands of kanji. It is essentially inaccessible to intermediate learners.
- Even a monolingual English learner dictionary of Japanese (where definitions are in simplified Japanese) would require reading fluency that intermediate learners lack.
- The script barrier (kanji, hiragana, katakana) adds a whole layer of difficulty to monolingual Japanese dictionary use that does not exist for European language pairs.

### Tono's findings

**Tono (2001)**, in extensive research on Japanese learners, documented several behaviors that are directly relevant:

- Japanese learners at all levels strongly preferred bilingual (English-Japanese) dictionaries over English-English monolingual dictionaries.
- The preference was even stronger than for European learners, likely because the linguistic distance makes L2-only definitions harder to process.
- Japanese learners frequently had difficulty identifying the correct part of speech of English words — a problem exacerbated by monolingual dictionaries where grammatical codes are less salient than in bilingual entries with explicit Japanese translations marking POS.

### The role of kanji

For Japanese, bilingual dictionaries serve a function that goes beyond meaning access: they provide **reading access**. A learner who encounters an unknown kanji compound needs to know not just what it means but how to read it. Monolingual Japanese dictionaries assume the reader already knows the reading (or can look up the kanji by radical/stroke count). Bilingual J→E dictionaries with furigana — like je-dict-1 — solve both problems simultaneously.

## The modern consensus

The field has largely moved beyond the binary debate. Key consensus positions include:

1. **No inherent superiority**: Neither monolingual nor bilingual dictionaries are inherently superior. Effectiveness depends on the interaction of dictionary type, learner proficiency, task type, and language pair (Lew 2016).

2. **Bilingualized dictionaries often outperform both**: When available, bilingualized dictionaries tend to produce the best outcomes across proficiency levels, because they combine the efficiency of L1 translation with the depth of L2 definition (Laufer & Hadar 1997; Laufer & Melamed 1994).

3. **The teacher orthodoxy was wrong**: The widespread prohibition of bilingual dictionaries in language classrooms was not supported by evidence and may have actively hindered learning (Adamska-Sałaciak 2010).

4. **Dictionary quality matters more than dictionary type**: A high-quality bilingual dictionary outperforms a mediocre monolingual one, and vice versa. The type distinction is less important than information richness, example quality, and usability (Bogaards & Van der Kloot 2001).

5. **Multiple dictionaries are ideal**: Skilled dictionary users employ multiple types strategically — bilingual for rapid meaning access, monolingual for depth and nuance (Atkins & Varantola 1997; Nesi 1999).

6. **Digital dictionaries blur the boundaries**: Modern electronic dictionaries often combine bilingual and monolingual features in ways that make the traditional categories less meaningful. Pop-up translations, linked definitions, and parallel L1/L2 display create hybrid experiences (Lew 2016).

## Implications for je-dict-1

### A well-positioned hybrid

je-dict-1's design aligns naturally with the research consensus. It is a bilingual J→E dictionary that provides:

- **L1 translations** (English glosses) for rapid meaning identification — the bilingual strength.
- **L2 context** (Japanese examples, collocations, patterns) for depth — the monolingual strength.
- **Rich notes** (in English) explaining nuance, register, and usage — the bilingualized dictionary's distinctive contribution.

This makes je-dict-1 effectively a bilingualized dictionary viewed from the learner's perspective: the learner gets both an English anchor (what the word means) and extensive Japanese context (how the word is used), without needing to parse a Japanese-only definition.

### Design features informed by the research

| Research finding | je-dict-1 feature |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| L1 translations are the most efficient meaning anchor | Top-level English glosses for each sense |
| L2 examples build productive knowledge | 3+ Japanese example sentences per sense |
| Collocations show how words are used | Collocations section in notes |
| Register information prevents misuse | Register notes and tags |
| Cross-references support vocabulary networking | `cross_references` and `prominent_see_also` fields |
| Furigana solves the reading-access problem | All kanji annotated with furigana readings |
| Similar-word notes prevent false equivalence | SIMILAR WORDS sections distinguishing near-synonyms |

### Remaining opportunities

1. **Explicit contrastive glossing**: When an English gloss could mislead (because the English word has broader or narrower scope than the Japanese word), the notes should explicitly flag the asymmetry. This is the bilingual dictionary's greatest risk, and systematic attention to it would set je-dict-1 apart.

2. **Productive-use support**: Research shows that bilingual dictionaries are weakest for encoding (L1→L2). je-dict-1 can compensate by ensuring that collocation patterns, particle requirements, and register notes are thorough enough to guide production, not just comprehension.

3. **Search from English**: Since many lookups originate from the English side (the learner wants to express something in Japanese), the English search index should surface all entries whose glosses or notes mention the search term, not just exact gloss matches.
