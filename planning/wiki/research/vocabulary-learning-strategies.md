# Vocabulary Learning Strategies

**Last updated**: 2026-05-05

## Overview

Vocabulary learning strategies (VLS) are the specific actions learners take to discover and consolidate the meaning of new words. Unlike the broader psycholinguistic processes of vocabulary acquisition (how lexical knowledge is mentally represented and incrementally deepened), VLS research focuses on the **deliberate choices** learners make: which strategies they select, how effective those strategies are, and how strategy instruction can improve outcomes.

This matters for dictionary design because a dictionary is itself a tool within a learner's strategy repertoire. Understanding how learners deploy strategies — and where those strategies succeed or fail — reveals which dictionary features are actually used, which are ignored, and which are missing.

## Major taxonomies

### Oxford (1990)

Rebecca Oxford's *Strategy Inventory for Language Learning* (SILL) provided the foundational classification of language learning strategies into two broad groups:

| Category | Strategy types | Examples |
|----------|---------------|----------|
| **Direct** | Memory | Creating mental linkages, imagery, reviewing |
| | Cognitive | Practicing, analysing, creating structure |
| | Compensation | Guessing intelligently, circumlocution |
| **Indirect** | Metacognitive | Planning, monitoring, evaluating learning |
| | Affective | Lowering anxiety, self-encouragement |
| | Social | Asking questions, cooperating, empathising |

Oxford's taxonomy was designed for general language learning, not vocabulary specifically, but her categories — especially the memory/cognitive/metacognitive distinction — became the scaffold for later VLS-specific work.

### Schmitt (1997)

Norbert Schmitt produced the most influential VLS-specific taxonomy, identifying 58 strategies organised into two phases of vocabulary learning:

**Discovery strategies** (determining the meaning of an unknown word):

| Subcategory | Strategies | Key examples |
|-------------|-----------|--------------|
| Determination | Using available cues without help from others | Guessing from context, analysing word parts, consulting a dictionary, using cognates |
| Social | Learning meaning through interaction | Asking a teacher, asking classmates, group discussion |

**Consolidation strategies** (retaining a word once discovered):

| Subcategory | Strategies | Key examples |
|-------------|-----------|--------------|
| Social | Using interaction to deepen knowledge | Group study, teaching others |
| Memory | Relating words to existing knowledge | Keyword method, semantic mapping, word grouping, imagery, physical association |
| Cognitive | Mechanical manipulation of language | Word lists, flashcards, note-taking, repetition, using the word in a sentence |
| Metacognitive | Controlling and evaluating one's learning | Spaced review, self-testing, selective attention, tracking known/unknown words |

Schmitt's taxonomy draws on Oxford's categories but reorganises them around the discovery/consolidation distinction, which maps well onto dictionary use: the dictionary primarily serves **discovery** (what does this word mean?) but its design can also support **consolidation** (how do I remember and use it?).

### Gu & Johnson (1996)

Gu and Johnson surveyed 850 Chinese university EFL learners using a 108-item questionnaire covering 21 strategy types. Their key finding was that **metacognitive strategies** — particularly self-initiation (seeking out new words) and selective attention (deciding which words are worth learning) — were the strongest positive predictors of both vocabulary size and general proficiency. Contextual guessing, dictionary skills, note-taking, word formation analysis, and contextual encoding also correlated positively.

Critically, **visual repetition** (mechanically re-reading a word and its definition) was the strongest *negative* predictor of both vocabulary size and general proficiency. This finding has been replicated in subsequent studies and underscores that not all "studying" is equal — shallow repetition without engagement is counterproductive.

## Key strategies and evidence

### Guessing from context

Learners encountering an unknown word in context can use surrounding linguistic and extralinguistic cues to infer its meaning. Nation (2001) identified this as one of the four most valuable VLS. However, its effectiveness depends heavily on:

- **Text coverage**: Learners need to know 95–98% of surrounding words to guess successfully (Laufer 1989, Hu & Nation 2000)
- **Context richness**: Many natural contexts are uninformative or misleading
- **Partial knowledge**: Learners often arrive at approximate rather than precise meanings
- **Verification need**: Guesses should be checked against a dictionary or word part analysis

Research consistently shows that guessing from context is effective for *receptive* vocabulary growth during extensive reading, but insufficient alone for precise or productive knowledge. It works best when combined with dictionary consultation as a verification step.

### Dictionary consultation

Dictionary use is the classic determination strategy. Research shows that learners with dictionary access during reading learn more vocabulary than those without (Luppescu & Day 1993), though they read more slowly. The critical question is not whether to use a dictionary, but *how*.

Effective dictionary consultation involves:
1. **Formulating the lookup**: Correctly lemmatising the encountered form (a significant barrier for Japanese, where inflected forms may be far from the dictionary form)
2. **Finding the entry**: Navigating the dictionary's access structure
3. **Selecting the right sense**: Avoiding "first-fit" errors (accepting the first definition without checking further)
4. **Integrating the information**: Connecting the new knowledge to the encounter context
5. **Recording for later review**: Taking notes or marking for spaced repetition

Nesi (1999) and Tono (2001) documented common failures at each stage. For Japanese specifically, the kanji barrier and homophone density create additional lookup obstacles (see [Dictionary Lookup Behavior](dictionary-lookup-behavior.md)).

### Word part analysis (morphological strategy)

Analysing word components — prefixes, suffixes, roots, or in Japanese, kanji components — helps learners both guess meanings and remember words. This is particularly powerful for Japanese because:

- **Kanji compounds**: Knowing the constituent kanji meanings enables inferencing of compound word meanings. Mori & Nagy (1999) showed that learners can successfully infer meanings of novel compounds when they know the component kanji.
- **Productive affixes**: The prefixes 不/非/無 (negation), 再 (re-), お/ご (honorific), and suffixes like 的/性/化 are highly productive and learnable as patterns.
- **Sino-Japanese compounding**: Kango follow relatively predictable compounding rules (modifier+head, coordinate, verb+object) that reward morphological awareness.

This strategy connects directly to dictionary design: entries that highlight morphological structure (component kanji meanings, productive affixes, compound formation patterns) support learners who use word part analysis.

### The keyword method

The keyword method (Atkinson 1975) is a mnemonic technique involving two steps: (1) find an L1 word that sounds like part of the L2 word (the "keyword"), and (2) form a vivid mental image linking the keyword to the L2 word's meaning.

Experimental evidence consistently shows the keyword method produces superior short-term recall compared to rote methods. However, it has significant limitations:

- **Long-term decay**: Several studies suggest the advantage fades over weeks unless reinforced
- **Production deficit**: The method primarily supports receptive recall (L2 → meaning) rather than productive use (meaning → L2)
- **Japanese difficulty**: The phonological distance between Japanese and English makes finding suitable keywords harder than for related language pairs
- **Scalability**: The method works well for individual words but becomes unwieldy at scale

Hulstijn (1997) argued that while the keyword method has strong experimental support, its real-world adoption by learners is lower than laboratory results would suggest, partly because it requires substantial creative effort per word.

### Word cards and spaced repetition

Deliberate study using word cards (physical or digital) combined with spaced repetition scheduling is among the best-evidenced vocabulary learning strategies. Nation (2001) identified word cards as one of the four most valuable VLS.

A research synthesis by Nakata & Webb (2022) confirmed that word card learning is effective for both receptive and productive vocabulary knowledge. Key design principles include:

- **Retrieval practice**: Cards should require active recall, not mere recognition
- **L2-to-L1 direction first**: Start with receptive (L2 on front, L1 on back), then add productive cards
- **Progressive information**: Cards can start with just form-meaning links, then add example sentences, collocations, and notes
- **Small sets with spacing**: 5–10 new cards per session, reviewed at expanding intervals

The spaced repetition principle (Ebbinghaus 1885, Pimsleur 1967) is now implemented in apps like Anki, and dictionaries that export word data in SRS-compatible formats directly support this strategy.

### Note-taking and word notebooks

Learners who keep structured vocabulary notebooks — recording not just meanings but also collocations, example sentences, word families, and personal associations — show better retention than those who don't (Schmitt & Schmitt 1995). The act of selecting and organising information constitutes the deep processing that Craik & Lockhart (1972) identified as critical for long-term memory.

The **involvement load hypothesis** (Laufer & Hulstijn 2001) formalises this insight: tasks with higher levels of *need* (motivation to learn the word), *search* (effort to find the meaning), and *evaluation* (effort to compare and contextualise the word) produce better retention. Of the three components, evaluation contributes the most to learning. Note-taking scores high on evaluation when learners must decide what information to record and how to organise it.

### Semantic mapping and grouping

Organising words into semantic networks — mind maps, word webs, category clusters — leverages the mental lexicon's associative structure. However, research on the effectiveness of learning words in semantic sets is surprisingly mixed:

- **Tinkham (1993, 1997)** and **Waring (1997)** found that presenting co-hyponyms (words from the same category, e.g., types of fruit) together caused *interference*, making them harder to learn than unrelated sets
- **Hoshino (2010)** and **Ishii (2015)** found facilitation or null effects under different conditions
- **Nation (2000)** resolved the apparent contradiction: semantic clustering helps during *consolidation* (deepening knowledge of already partially-known words) but hinders during *introduction* (first encounters with new words)

This has a clear implication for dictionary browsing: thematic browse pages should be designed for consolidation, not discovery, and should make dimensions of difference between related words explicit (see [Sense Relations and Semantic Networks](sense-relations-semantic-networks.md)).

## Self-regulation and strategic competence

### Beyond individual strategies

Tseng, Dörnyei, and Schmitt (2006) argued that the field's focus on cataloguing individual strategies missed the deeper question: what makes a learner strategically competent? They proposed the **Self-Regulating Capacity in Vocabulary Learning** (SRCvoc) framework, which measures five dimensions:

1. **Commitment control**: Maintaining motivation to continue vocabulary study
2. **Metacognitive control**: Planning, monitoring, and adjusting learning activities
3. **Satiation control**: Managing boredom and maintaining engagement with repetitive practice
4. **Emotion control**: Handling frustration from slow progress or difficult words
5. **Environmental control**: Arranging study conditions (time, place, materials) for effectiveness

Tseng and Schmitt (2008) found that self-regulatory capacity predicted both strategic involvement and vocabulary knowledge, suggesting that the *ability to manage one's learning process* matters more than which specific strategies one uses.

### Strategy instruction effectiveness

Mizumoto and Takeuchi (2009) conducted a controlled study with 146 Japanese EFL university students, providing explicit VLS instruction over 10 weeks. The experimental group significantly outperformed the control group on vocabulary tests, demonstrating that strategy instruction works — learners can be taught to learn more effectively.

This finding has been replicated across contexts. The consensus is that explicit strategy instruction is most beneficial for lower-proficiency learners, who may not have discovered effective strategies on their own.

## Japanese-specific considerations

Japanese L2 vocabulary learning involves several unique challenges that shape strategy use:

### Script complexity

The three-script writing system (hiragana, katakana, kanji) means learners need script-specific strategies:
- **Kanji compounds**: Morphological analysis (component kanji → compound meaning) is a high-value strategy
- **Katakana loanwords**: Cognate recognition from English is effective but complicated by phonological adaptation and semantic shift (see [Gairaigo](gairaigo-loanwords.md))
- **Reading-meaning dissociation**: The same kanji may have multiple readings, requiring separate strategies for form-meaning and form-pronunciation links

### L1-specific strategy patterns

Research shows Japanese EFL learners tend to:
- Favour cognitive strategies (rote repetition, word lists) over social strategies
- Under-use metacognitive strategies relative to their effectiveness
- Rely heavily on visual repetition, despite its negative correlation with outcomes (Gu & Johnson 1996)
- Benefit substantially from explicit strategy instruction (Mizumoto & Takeuchi 2009)

For learners of Japanese as L2, L1 background shapes strategy availability:
- **Chinese-background learners**: Can leverage shared kanji for morphological strategies but face false-friend risks
- **Korean-background learners**: Benefit from shared Sino-Korean vocabulary and similar grammar
- **Alphabetic-background learners**: Must develop entirely new strategies for kanji processing, making morphological strategies harder to acquire initially

### The dictionary as strategy enabler

In the Japanese learning context, dictionary consultation is particularly important because:
- Context-only guessing is harder due to script barriers
- Morphological analysis requires kanji component knowledge that dictionaries can provide
- Polysemy in basic vocabulary (かける has 10+ senses) makes dictionary verification essential
- Particle-verb dependencies aren't predictable from vocabulary knowledge alone

## Implications for je-dict-1

The VLS research literature suggests several concrete ways a learner dictionary can support strategic vocabulary learning:

### Supporting discovery strategies

| Strategy | Dictionary feature that supports it |
|----------|--------------------------------------|
| Guessing from context | Rich example sentences that model natural contexts; definitions that clarify semantic boundaries |
| Word part analysis | Kanji component information in notes; cross-references to words sharing components; productive affix documentation |
| Dictionary consultation | Clear entry structure; search that handles inflected forms; sense ordering that reduces first-fit errors |
| Cognate recognition | Source language notes on katakana entries; false-friend warnings |

### Supporting consolidation strategies

| Strategy | Dictionary feature that supports it |
|----------|--------------------------------------|
| Word cards / SRS | Machine-readable entry data (JSON) that can be exported to Anki; structured fields for form, meaning, examples |
| Note-taking | Collocation fields, usage notes, and particle patterns provide the information learners should record |
| Semantic mapping | Cross-references, synonym/antonym links, and thematic browse pages support network building |
| Contextual encoding | Progressive example sentences (simple → complex) that model the word in varied contexts |
| Self-testing | Conjugation tables that let learners verify their knowledge of word forms |

### Supporting self-regulation

| Self-regulation dimension | Dictionary feature |
|--------------------------|-------------------|
| Selective attention | Vocabulary tier system (basic/core/general) helps learners prioritise which words to study |
| Planning | Tier-based browsing lets learners work through a frequency-informed sequence |
| Monitoring | Clear quality and completeness standards make it possible to assess one's knowledge against what the dictionary documents |
| Satiation control | Cross-reference networks and browse paths provide variety within study sessions |

### Specific recommendations

1. **Prioritise productive information**: Collocations, particle patterns, register labels, and contrastive notes serve productive vocabulary needs, which are harder to develop and where dictionary support is most valuable.

2. **Design examples for depth of processing**: Examples that require learners to notice grammatical patterns, register choices, and collocational preferences create higher involvement load than examples that merely illustrate meaning.

3. **Support morphological strategies**: Notes that explain kanji compound formation (e.g., "修理 = 修 repair + 理 manage") support one of the most effective strategies for Japanese vocabulary learning.

4. **Make semantic networks explicit**: Cross-references and similar-words sections should highlight the *dimensions of difference* between related words (register, collocation, connotation), not just list them — this supports the evaluation component of the involvement load hypothesis.

5. **Maintain SRS compatibility**: The JSON entry format already enables data export; maintaining clean, structured fields ensures compatibility with external learning tools.
