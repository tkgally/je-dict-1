# L1 Transfer in Japanese L2 Vocabulary Acquisition

**Last updated**: 2026-06-06

## Overview

When learners acquire Japanese vocabulary, their first language (L1) acts as both a scaffold and a source of interference. The nature and extent of transfer depends heavily on the L1's writing system, its genetic and borrowing relationship to Japanese, and the specific vocabulary domain. Japanese is unusual among major target languages because it uses multiple scripts (hiragana, katakana, kanji) and draws vocabulary from multiple source strata (wago, kango, gairaigo), each of which creates different transfer dynamics for different L1 groups.

This page surveys how L1 background shapes Japanese vocabulary learning, with attention to the three learner populations most relevant to je-dict-1: English speakers (the primary audience), Chinese speakers (the largest group of Japanese learners worldwide), and Korean speakers (who share both kanji-origin vocabulary and grammatical structure with Japanese).

## Theoretical background: how L1 mediates L2 vocabulary

### Jiang's three-stage model

Jiang (2000) proposed an influential model of L2 lexical representation with three developmental stages:

1. **Formal stage**: The learner establishes an L2 lexical entry containing only formal information (spelling, pronunciation). Meaning is accessed indirectly through L1 translation equivalents.
2. **L1 lemma mediation stage**: Semantic, syntactic, and morphological information from the L1 translation is "copied" into the L2 lexical entry. The learner can use the L2 word but processes it through L1 conceptual structures.
3. **L2 integration stage**: The learner develops independent L2 semantic representations, including L2-specific syntactic patterns, collocational preferences, and pragmatic associations.

Jiang argued that most L2 words fossilize at stage 2 — learners process L2 words through L1 semantic frames indefinitely. This has direct implications for dictionary design: entries that explicitly address L1 interference patterns can help learners move beyond L1-mediated processing.

### Cross-linguistic influence at the lexical level

Odlin (2003) established that cross-linguistic influence (CLI) operates at all linguistic levels. At the vocabulary level, transfer manifests through:

- **Cognate facilitation**: Words with similar form and meaning across L1 and L2 are learned faster (Ringbom 2007)
- **False friend interference**: Words with similar form but different meaning cause persistent errors
- **Semantic transfer**: L1 semantic boundaries are imposed on L2 words (e.g., an English speaker treating 知る and 分かる as interchangeable because both map to "know")
- **Collocational transfer**: L1 collocational patterns are applied to L2 words, producing unnatural pairings
- **Conceptual transfer**: L1-specific conceptual categories (e.g., English "wear" vs. Japanese 着る/履く/被る/かける) create persistent confusion

## The three-way L1 split for Japanese learners

Japanese vocabulary learning difficulty varies dramatically based on the learner's L1 writing system and lexical stock. Three broad groups emerge:

### Group 1: Logographic L1 — Chinese speakers

Chinese-speaking learners of Japanese have the most obvious transfer resource: shared kanji/hanzi characters. An estimated 60-70% of kanji used in Japanese newspapers have recognizable Chinese counterparts (Koda 2005). This creates a massive receptive vocabulary head start — a Chinese learner encountering 経済 (economy) or 図書館 (library) for the first time can often guess the meaning from the characters alone.

**Positive transfer**:
- **Form-meaning mapping**: Shared characters provide immediate (if approximate) semantic access. Orthographic similarity of 0-2 character differences produces significantly faster recognition times than higher-difference pairs (研究/研究 = identical; 時間/时间 = simplified-traditional difference only)
- **Compound inferencing**: Chinese learners can decompose unfamiliar kanji compounds using morphemic analysis — a strategy largely unavailable to alphabetic-L1 learners
- **Reading speed**: Chinese learners achieve faster reading speeds in Japanese text with kanji than alphabetic-L1 learners at equivalent proficiency levels

**Negative transfer — false friends (同形異義語)**:
Japanese and Chinese share many kanji compounds with shifted or entirely different meanings. These "false friends" are particularly treacherous because the learner's confidence in the (wrong) meaning is high:

| Japanese | Chinese | Japanese meaning | Chinese meaning |
|----------|---------|------------------|-----------------|
| 勉強 | 勉强 | study | reluctantly; barely |
| 手紙 | 手纸 | letter | toilet paper |
| 大丈夫 | 大丈夫 | all right; safe | a real man |
| 新聞 | 新闻 | newspaper | news |
| 丈夫 | 丈夫 | sturdy; healthy | husband |
| 経理 | 经理 | accounting | manager |

Research on Chinese learners processing Japanese collocations (Li et al. 2023) shows that translational congruency — whether a Japanese collocation has a structurally parallel Chinese equivalent — significantly affects processing speed. Collocations that are congruent with Chinese are processed faster; incongruent ones produce interference.

**Phonological interference**: Chinese and Japanese assign different readings to the same characters. 人 is *rén* in Chinese but *ひと/じん/にん* in Japanese. Chinese learners must suppress L1 phonological associations to learn Japanese readings — an effortful process that research shows produces measurably longer reaction times for cognate words vs. non-cognate controls.

### Group 2: Syllabographic/alphabetic L1 with kanji heritage — Korean speakers

Korean occupies a unique position. Modern Korean uses Hangul (an alphabetic/featural script), but Korean vocabulary is heavily Sino-Korean (漢語/한자어), with an estimated 57-70% of Korean dictionary headwords having Chinese-character origins (Sohn 1999). Many educated Koreans can read basic hanja (漢字), and Sino-Korean readings are phonologically close to Sino-Japanese (on'yomi) readings due to shared medieval Chinese borrowing paths:

| Kanji | Japanese on'yomi | Korean reading | Meaning |
|-------|------------------|----------------|---------|
| 学生 | がくせい | 학생 (haksaeng) | student |
| 図書館 | としょかん | 도서관 (doseogwan) | library |
| 約束 | やくそく | 약속 (yaksok) | promise |

This phonological proximity gives Korean learners an advantage in learning on'yomi readings — once the systematic correspondences are learned (Korean initial ㅎ often corresponds to Japanese か-row, etc.), new Sino-Japanese vocabulary becomes partially predictable from Korean knowledge.

Korean learners also benefit from close grammatical alignment: SOV word order, postpositional particles, and similar agglutinative morphology make syntactic transfer largely positive. However, Korean learners may under-attend to Japanese-specific semantic and pragmatic distinctions because the superficial similarity encourages over-reliance on L1 mapping.

### Group 3: Alphabetic L1 — English speakers (je-dict-1's primary audience)

English speakers lack the kanji/hanzi bridge that Chinese and Korean learners enjoy. Their primary transfer resource is gairaigo — the thousands of English-origin loanwords written in katakana:

**Gairaigo as cognate advantage**:
- An estimated 10-15% of words in modern Japanese texts are gairaigo, overwhelmingly from English (Loveday 1996)
- At JLPT N4-N5 (beginner) level, gairaigo constitute up to 15.8% of nouns in standard textbooks, decreasing to ~10% at higher levels (Champ 2014)
- Spoken gairaigo is more easily recognized than written gairaigo, because katakana transliteration obscures the English source word's orthography while preserving (adapted) phonology
- Experienced teachers report using gairaigo strategically to "reduce the processing load when introducing new sentences" (Champ 2014)

**Gairaigo false friends (wasei-eigo and semantic shifts)**:
English-origin words in Japanese frequently undergo semantic narrowing, broadening, or shift:

| Japanese gairaigo | English source | Japanese meaning | Semantic relationship |
|-------------------|----------------|------------------|----------------------|
| マンション | mansion | apartment/condo | narrowing + shift |
| サービス | service | free/complimentary | extension |
| スマート | smart | slim/stylish | shift (not "intelligent") |
| ナイーブ | naive | sensitive/delicate | shift (not pejorative) |
| バイキング | viking | buffet | complete reanalysis |
| クレーム | claim | complaint | narrowing |

Wasei-eigo (和製英語) — English-appearing words coined in Japanese — compound the problem: ノートパソコン, コンセント (electrical outlet), サラリーマン have no direct English equivalents despite their English-sounding components.

**Alphabetic-L1 disadvantages**:
- **No kanji decomposition strategy**: English speakers cannot infer meaning from kanji components without explicit instruction in radicals and semantic cues
- **Multiple reading systems**: The same kanji having kun'yomi and on'yomi readings has no parallel in alphabetic writing systems, making the form-meaning-pronunciation mapping uniquely difficult
- **Script switching cost**: Processing text that mixes hiragana, katakana, and kanji imposes cognitive load that monoscript-L1 readers don't experience in their L1

Research on beginning-level learners shows that those with alphabetic L1s perform significantly worse on lexical judgment tasks than logographic-L1 learners, specifically due to poor L2 orthographic awareness (Koda 2005). This gap narrows with proficiency but never fully closes.

## Transfer effects by Japanese vocabulary stratum

The three-stratum structure of Japanese vocabulary (wago/kango/gairaigo) creates a matrix of transfer effects:

| Vocabulary stratum | Chinese L1 | Korean L1 | English L1 |
|--------------------|------------|-----------|------------|
| **Wago** (native Japanese) | Difficult — no cognates, unfamiliar readings | Moderate — some grammatical parallels | Difficult — no cognates |
| **Kango** (Sino-Japanese) | Strong positive transfer (shared characters) with false-friend risk | Moderate positive transfer (shared Sino-Korean roots) | Minimal transfer — must learn as new vocabulary |
| **Gairaigo** (loanwords) | Minimal transfer | Minimal transfer | Strong positive transfer with false-friend risk |

This means that **no single L1 group has a uniform advantage** across all Japanese vocabulary. Chinese speakers excel at kango but struggle with wago and gairaigo. English speakers have an entry point through gairaigo but face a steeper climb with kango and wago. Korean speakers have moderate advantages across all three strata but no dominant advantage in any one.

## Implications for je-dict-1

je-dict-1 targets English-speaking learners as its primary audience, but the dictionary's design can accommodate L1 transfer research in several ways:

### 1. Gairaigo entries should flag semantic shifts

English speakers will naturally assume gairaigo means what the English source word means. Entries for gairaigo with semantic shift should lead with the divergence: the SIMILAR WORDS or notes section should explicitly contrast the Japanese meaning with the English source meaning. This is already done for some entries (e.g., the existing gairaigo research page recommends this), but could be more systematic.

### 2. Kango compounds benefit from morphemic gloss

For kango entries, providing the per-character meaning (e.g., 図書館 = "diagram" + "book" + "building" → library) helps English speakers develop the compound-inferencing strategy that Chinese speakers use naturally. The notes field's existing practice of explaining kanji components serves this purpose.

### 3. Wago near-synonyms need explicit L1-interference notes

English speakers frequently confuse Japanese near-synonyms that map to a single English word. High-value examples include:
- 知る vs. 分かる (both "know" — but acquisition vs. state)
- 着る/履く/被る/かける (all "wear" — but by body region)
- 聞く/聴く (both "hear/listen" — but attention level)
- 見る/観る/診る (all "see/look" — but purpose)
- 使う/用いる (both "use" — but register)

The SIMILAR WORDS section in entries is the natural place for this, and many entries already provide such contrasts. The research suggests this is one of the highest-value features for English-speaking users.

### 4. Furigana supports the alphabetic-L1 learner's biggest weakness

English speakers' primary disadvantage is the kanji reading system. je-dict-1's policy of full furigana on all kanji in all fields directly addresses this — it provides the phonological access that Chinese speakers get from character recognition and Korean speakers get from Sino-Korean correspondence. The research confirms that this scaffolding is most critical for exactly the learner population je-dict-1 serves.

### 5. Register stratum markers help all L1 groups

The wago/kango/gairaigo distinction often tracks register (wago = native/casual, kango = formal/written, gairaigo = modern/technical). Marking this explicitly helps all learners, but especially English speakers who cannot infer stratum from script alone (Chinese speakers can identify kango by kanji density; Korean speakers can sometimes identify Sino-Japanese words by phonological shape).

### 6. False friend coverage should be bidirectional

The dictionary already has a research page on gairaigo that covers English→Japanese false friends. The same treatment would be valuable for Chinese→Japanese and Korean→Japanese false friends if the dictionary expands its audience. Even for the current English-speaker audience, noting when a kango compound has a different meaning in Chinese can be useful cultural context.

This is no longer hypothetical: the [Multilingual Dictionary](../ideas/multilingual-dictionary.md) plan makes Chinese the first additional target language, and the [Japanese→Chinese Adaptation Brief](japanese-chinese-adaptation-brief.md) develops the Chinese-side false friends (the 文化庁 S/O/D/N triage, an expanded 同形異義語 table, the L1-specific `common_mistakes`) into an operational reference for the translation pipeline and the human advisor. The Chinese/Korean/English transfer dynamics analyzed on this page are the research backbone those per-language briefs draw on; the briefs are where the analysis becomes per-entry note content.
