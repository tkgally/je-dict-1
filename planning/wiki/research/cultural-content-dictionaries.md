# Cultural Content in Bilingual Dictionaries

**Last updated**: 2026-05-25

## Overview

Every bilingual dictionary must decide how much cultural information to include and how to present it. The question is not whether dictionaries should contain cultural content — they inevitably do, since language and culture are inseparable — but how explicitly and systematically to treat culturally-loaded vocabulary. This page surveys the theoretical frameworks, the approaches taken by major dictionaries, the research on learner needs for cultural knowledge, and the implications for a Japanese-English learner dictionary where the cultural gap between source and target languages is wide.

## The encyclopedic–linguistic boundary

Zgusta (1971) drew the canonical distinction: a *dictionary* provides information about linguistic expressions, while an *encyclopedia* provides information about things in the world. A dictionary entry for 山 should explain its meaning, pronunciation, and grammar; an encyclopedia entry for Mount Fuji should describe its height, eruption history, and cultural significance.

In practice the boundary is porous. Atkins & Rundell (2008) argue that learner dictionaries need to include "just enough" encyclopedic information for the user to understand and use the word correctly. For a word like お盆 (Obon), a bare translation equivalent ("the Bon Festival") is almost useless — the learner needs to know *when* it falls, *what* people do, and *why* it matters, because these are the conditions under which the word is used. The relevant test is pragmatic: include cultural information when omitting it would cause a communication failure or a social misunderstanding.

Kiefer (1988) proposed a useful three-way distinction: *linguistic* knowledge (what a word means and how it behaves grammatically), *conceptual* knowledge (the cognitive category it activates), and *encyclopedic* knowledge (what we know about the real-world referent). These three interact constantly in language use, and Kiefer argued that dictionary-makers need to attend to all three rather than pretending the linguistic component can be isolated.

Svensén (2009) frames this as a continuum rather than a binary: some entries need zero encyclopedic content (e.g., grammatical particles), others need moderate cultural framing (e.g., food terms with different preparation assumptions), and a small number need substantial encyclopedic treatment (e.g., annual events, social institutions, historical periods). The compiler's job is to calibrate the depth per entry.

## Culture-bound terms and realia

### Defining the category

Culture-bound terms (also called *realia*, *culturemes*, or *culture-specific items*) are lexical items that refer to concepts, objects, or practices specific to one culture with no direct equivalent in another. They pose the sharpest challenge for bilingual dictionaries because the standard lexicographic operation — providing a translation equivalent — breaks down.

Newmark (1988) classifies culture-bound terms into five categories:

| Category | Examples (Japanese) |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Ecology** | 梅雨 (rainy season), 桜前線 (cherry blossom front), 里山 (satoyama) |
| **Material culture** | 畳 (tatami), 襖 (fusuma), 弁当 (bento) |
| **Social culture** | 花見 (hanami), 忘年会 (end-of-year party), 名刺交換 (business card exchange) |
| **Organizations, customs, ideas** | 七五三 (Shichi-Go-San), 成人式 (Coming of Age Day), 天皇 (Emperor) |
| **Gestures and habits** | お辞儀 (bowing), 土下座 (prostrate bow), 正座 (seiza sitting) |

### Translation approaches

Nida (1964) distinguishes *formal equivalence* (preserving the form and content of the source) from *dynamic equivalence* (producing the same effect on the target-language reader). For culture-bound terms, formal equivalence often yields opaque translations (七五三 → "Seven-Five-Three"?) while dynamic equivalence risks distorting the concept (七五三 → "a children's rite of passage"?). Most learner dictionaries use a hybrid: a brief transliteration or calque plus an explanatory gloss.

Nida (2001) emphasizes that "for truly successful translating, biculturalism is even more important than bilingualism, since words only have meanings in terms of the cultures in which they function." This principle applies directly to bilingual dictionary-making: the compiler must possess deep knowledge of both cultures to write glosses that are neither over-translated (imposing target-culture frames) nor under-translated (leaving the learner without the cultural context needed for comprehension).

Nied Curcio (2023) demonstrated that the problem persists in practice: "culture-bound items are often omitted in lexicographic resources, and when they are present, they are rarely described in an appropriate way, especially in bilingual dictionaries: the listed equivalents often do not reflect the meaning of the cultural word precisely, usage examples may be missing and above all important information on the cultural level is omitted." She argues that lexicographers must function as *cultural mediators*, drawing on the CEFR Companion Volume (2018) framework of mediation activities and strategies.

## The culture–language inseparability thesis

### Kramsch and the pedagogical tradition

Kramsch (1993) argues that culture is not a "fifth skill" appended to listening, speaking, reading, and writing, but a dimension that permeates all language use. Every vocabulary item carries cultural associations — not just obviously cultural terms like 正月 (New Year) but everyday words like ご飯 (gohan), which encodes assumptions about meal structure, hospitality, and the ritual significance of rice that 'cooked rice' or 'meal' does not convey. A dictionary that strips this context teaches the language without teaching the ability to use it.

### Byram's intercultural communicative competence

Byram (1997) systematizes intercultural competence into five *savoirs*:

1. **Attitudes** (savoir-être) — curiosity and openness toward cultural difference
2. **Knowledge** (savoirs) — understanding cultural practices, products, and perspectives
3. **Interpreting and relating** (savoir-comprendre) — mediating meaning across cultural viewpoints
4. **Discovery and interaction** (savoir-apprendre/faire) — applying cultural learning in real encounters
5. **Critical cultural awareness** (savoir-s'engager) — evaluating cultural practices reflectively

A dictionary contributes primarily to the *knowledge* and *interpreting* savoirs. Cultural notes that explain *why* Japanese speakers use a word in a particular context (not just *what* it means) support the learner's developing ability to interpret cultural practices and relate them to their own experience.

### Wierzbicka's cultural scripts

Wierzbicka (1991, 2003) proposes that each speech community operates according to tacit "cultural scripts" — shared norms about when and how to speak, expressed in a natural semantic metalanguage of universal human concepts. Japanese cultural scripts include indirectness norms (saying いいです to decline rather than directly refusing), gift-giving reciprocity cycles (お中元/お歳暮), and age/status-calibrated register selection. A bilingual dictionary can surface these scripts through usage notes and pragmatic guidance without requiring the learner to discover them through immersion alone.

## How major dictionaries handle cultural content

### Monolingual English learner dictionaries

| Dictionary | Approach | Mechanism |
|------------|----------|-----------|
| **OALD** | Cultural notes boxes, "Oxford Culture" section in encyclopedic edition | Inline boxes flagged with a culture icon; separate reference section for proper nouns and institutions. The OALED (encyclopedic edition) was designed explicitly for "culture and civilisation courses." |
| **LDOCE/LDELC** | "Language and Culture" boxes, "Cultural Notes" | The LDELC (Longman Dictionary of English Language and Culture) combines 80,000 words with 15,000 cultural/encyclopedic entries and over 400 cultural notes — the most explicitly encyclopedic learner dictionary. Covers social life, customs, and British/American differences. |
| **COBUILD** | Full-sentence definitions that embed cultural context naturally | The definition style itself conveys more pragmatic context than traditional definitions |
| **MED** | Cultural awareness notes within "Word Partnerships" and usage sections | Less systematic cultural coverage than OALD or LDOCE |

The Big Four English learner dictionaries converge on a model of *flagged cultural supplements*: the linguistic entry remains primary, and cultural information appears in visually distinct boxes or notes that the user can consult optionally. The OALD "encyclopedic edition" goes furthest, adding proper-noun entries (places, people, institutions) that a strict linguistic dictionary would exclude.

### Japanese-English dictionaries

| Dictionary | Cultural content approach |
|------------|-------------------------|
| **Kenkyusha (5th ed.)** | Occasional cultural notes for terms like 正月, 花見, 七夕; limited and unsystematic. The "Green Goddess" prioritizes translation accuracy over cultural framing. Kenkyusha separately published a *Bilingual Dictionary of Japanese Cultural Terms* (和英日本文化表現辞典, 2007) with 3,500 headwords covering culture, customs, seasonal events, food, and modern terms — a companion-volume strategy that keeps cultural depth out of the main dictionary. |
| **Wisdom (Sanseidō)** | Communication boxes (コミュニケーション) that explain pragmatic norms and cultural contexts. More systematic than Kenkyusha for social ritual vocabulary. |
| **JMdict/EDICT** | No cultural notes. Glosses only, sometimes with domain tags. Culture-bound terms receive a brief English paraphrase at best. |
| **Jisho.org** | Surfaces JMdict data; adds some Wikipedia-sourced context for proper nouns. No original cultural content. |

The gap in JE lexicography is clear: no freely accessible JE dictionary offers systematic cultural annotation. Kenkyusha has some but is proprietary; Wisdom's communication boxes are a model worth studying but are limited to a commercial print/app product; JMdict has none.

## Japanese-specific cultural dimensions

Japanese presents an unusually rich set of cultural vocabulary challenges for a bilingual dictionary:

### Seasonal vocabulary (季語 kigo and beyond)

Japanese culture is deeply organized around seasonal awareness. The traditional *saijiki* (歳時記, seasonal almanac) catalogs thousands of season words for haiku, but seasonal vocabulary extends far beyond poetry. Greetings change by season (暑中見舞い in summer, 寒中見舞い in winter), foods have expected seasons (秋刀魚 is autumn, 鱧 is summer in Kyoto), and many nouns carry seasonal connotations that affect their pragmatic appropriateness. A dictionary entry for 七夕 that omits "July 7th" and "star festival where people write wishes on strips of paper" leaves the learner unable to use the word in conversation.

### Food culture vocabulary

Washoku (和食) vocabulary is extensive and layered. Many food terms cannot be translated to a single English word because the concept doesn't exist in the target culture: 出汁 (dashi — stock made from kelp and bonito, foundational to Japanese cuisine), 懐石 (kaiseki — multi-course meal following tea ceremony principles), 三枚おろし (sanmai-oroshi — filleting a fish into three pieces). Even terms with apparent equivalents need cultural notes: 味噌汁 is not just "miso soup" but a daily staple with regional variation and seasonal ingredient selection.

### Social ritual vocabulary

Gift-giving (お中元, お歳暮, 内祝い, 香典), greetings and formulaic expressions (よろしくお願いします, お疲れ様です, いただきます, ごちそうさまでした), life events (入学式, 成人式, 結婚式, 葬儀), and workplace customs (飲み会, 忘年会, 歓迎会, 送別会) form a dense web of culturally-embedded vocabulary that learners encounter early and often. These words are used in highly constrained social contexts; knowing the translation is insufficient without knowing the social script.

### The keigo–culture interface

Honorific language is not merely a grammatical feature but a cultural system encoding social relationships (uchi/soto, 目上/目下). Dictionary entries for keigo forms need cultural framing to explain not just *what* the form means but *when* and *to whom* it is appropriate. See [Keigo: Honorific Language](keigo-honorifics.md) for the full research summary.

## What research says about learner needs

Studies consistently show that cultural knowledge gaps cause communication failures that are more consequential than grammatical errors:

- **Pragmatic failure** (Thomas 1983): Errors in cultural appropriateness (e.g., using casual language in a business context, or failing to perform expected rituals) produce harsher judgments from native speakers than grammatical mistakes, because they are interpreted as rudeness rather than incompetence.
- **The consultation gap**: Nesi & Haill (2002) and Tono (2001) found that learners often skip cultural notes in dictionaries, even when the cultural information is directly relevant to their lookup. This suggests that cultural content should be integrated into the main entry text rather than segregated into optional boxes that users can bypass.
- **Production vs. reception**: Cultural knowledge needs are asymmetric. For *receptive* tasks (reading about 七夕), a brief cultural gloss suffices. For *productive* tasks (writing a 暑中見舞い or participating in a 飲み会), the learner needs to understand the social expectations and behavioral norms — information that dictionaries rarely provide.

## Implications for je-dict-1

### What the dictionary already does

je-dict-1's entry format is well-suited to cultural content. The structured `notes` field with its section-based organization (CULTURAL NOTE, USAGE, SIMILAR WORDS) provides a natural home for cultural information. Many entries already include cultural context:

- Seasonal event entries explain timing and customs
- Food entries describe preparation methods and regional variation
- Keigo entries explain the social context for usage
- Expression entries include pragmatic guidance on appropriateness

### Opportunities for systematic improvement

1. **Identify the cultural-note coverage gap**. Not all culturally-loaded entries have cultural notes. A systematic audit (similar to the existing note-quality scorer) could flag entries where the headword is culturally loaded but the notes lack cultural framing. Newmark's five-category taxonomy provides a starting framework: ecology, material culture, social culture, organizations/customs, gestures/habits.

2. **Integrate cultural content into the main entry**. Following the research on the consultation gap, cultural information in je-dict-1 should appear *within* the notes — not in a separate, skippable section. The current CULTURAL NOTE section header is useful for organized scanning but should be a regular notes section, not an isolated appendix.

3. **Support productive use for social ritual vocabulary**. Entries for culturally-embedded expressions (よろしくお願いします, お疲れ様です, etc.) should explain not just the meaning but the social script: when to say it, to whom, what response is expected, and what happens if you omit it. The existing USAGE sections partially do this; a dedicated audit could ensure coverage.

4. **Seasonal awareness in examples**. Example sentences for season-associated vocabulary should reflect the season. An example for 秋刀魚 set in spring would be culturally jarring. This is a quality dimension that the example-sentence polishing task could check.

5. **Cross-reference cultural clusters**. Vocabulary items that participate in the same cultural practice (お中元 ↔ お歳暮 ↔ 内祝い ↔ 香典 for gift-giving; 入学式 ↔ 卒業式 ↔ 成人式 for life events) should be systematically cross-referenced so learners can explore cultural domains as connected vocabularies. The semantic field audit infrastructure already supports this.

6. **Flag the encyclopedic calibration per entry**. Some entries need a single sentence of cultural context; others need a paragraph. The comprehensive-polish task could include a "cultural calibration" check: is this entry culturally loaded? If so, does the cultural note match the expected depth (brief for familiar concepts, substantial for culture-specific ones)?

### Priority vocabulary for cultural enrichment

Based on Newmark's categories and the Japanese-specific dimensions above, the highest-priority vocabulary clusters for cultural note addition or improvement are:

| Cluster | Example entries | Cultural note content needed |
|---------|----------------|----------------------------|
| Annual events | 正月, お盆, 七夕, 節分, ひな祭り | Timing, customs, regional variation |
| Gift-giving | お中元, お歳暮, 内祝い, 香典 | When, to whom, amounts, reciprocity |
| Formulaic expressions | お疲れ様, よろしく, いただきます | Social script, appropriate contexts |
| Food/dining | 懐石, 出汁, 和食, お箸のマナー | Preparation, cultural significance |
| Life events | 成人式, 七五三, 還暦 | Age/timing, customs, modern changes |
| Seasonal greetings | 年賀状, 暑中見舞い, 寒中見舞い | Timing conventions, modern alternatives |
