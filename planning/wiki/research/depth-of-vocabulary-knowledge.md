# Depth of Vocabulary Knowledge

**Last updated**: 2026-05-30

## Overview

"Depth of vocabulary knowledge" refers to how well a learner knows the words they know — the richness of associations, collocational patterns, grammatical behavior, register constraints, and polysemous distinctions attached to each lexical item. It contrasts with "breadth" (how many words a learner can recognize). The distinction is central to understanding what a dictionary should provide beyond simple glosses.

## Nation's framework: what it means to "know a word"

Paul Nation (2001, *Learning Vocabulary in Another Language*, Cambridge University Press) proposed the most widely cited framework. He identifies three major categories — **form**, **meaning**, and **use** — each with three sub-aspects, and each sub-aspect having receptive and productive dimensions (18 knowledge types total):

| Category | Aspect | Receptive | Productive |
|----------|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Form** | Spoken | Recognize pronunciation | Pronounce correctly |
| | Written | Recognize spelling | Spell correctly |
| | Word parts | Recognize affixes/stems | Use word parts to create |
| **Meaning** | Form-meaning | Recall meaning when encountering | Recall form when expressing |
| | Concepts & referents | Know range of referents | Use appropriately to referents |
| | Associations | Know paradigmatic neighbors | Produce appropriate associates |
| **Use** | Grammatical functions | Recognize patterns in input | Use in correct patterns |
| | Collocations | Recognize natural combinations | Produce natural combinations |
| | Constraints | Recognize register/frequency | Use with appropriate register |

This framework implies that a learner who can translate 食べる as "to eat" may still not *know* the word — they may be unaware of its register constraints (neutral, not formal), its collocations (ご飯を食べる but not *食事を食べる), its grammatical peculiarities (食べている = progressive, not resultative), or its keigo alternatives (召し上がる, いただく).

## Measurement instruments

### Vocabulary Knowledge Scale (Paribakht & Wesche 1997)

A five-level self-report scale tracking the transition from no knowledge to productive use:

1. Never seen this word before
2. Have seen it, don't know the meaning
3. Have seen it, think it means ___
4. Know it — it means ___
5. Can use it in a sentence: ___

The VKS captures the incremental nature of acquisition but conflates multiple knowledge dimensions into a single scale. It is most useful for tracking initial recognition and early use of new words encountered in reading.

### Word Associates Test (Read 2000; Read & Chapelle 2001)

John Read's Word Associates Test (WAT) measures depth through the quality of network connections. The test presents a stimulus word and asks test-takers to select associates from three relationship types:

- **Paradigmatic**: synonyms, coordinates, superordinates
- **Syntagmatic**: collocates (words that co-occur)
- **Analytic**: words representing component meanings

Read and Chapelle (2001, "A framework for second language vocabulary assessment," *Language Testing*) argue that vocabulary assessment must capture network knowledge — how words relate to one another in the mental lexicon — not just isolated form-meaning pairs.

## Breadth vs. depth: the Qian studies

Qian (1999, "Assessing the Roles of Depth and Breadth of Vocabulary Knowledge in Reading Comprehension," *Canadian Modern Language Review* 56.2, pp. 282–308) demonstrated that vocabulary depth makes a **unique contribution** to reading comprehension beyond what size alone predicts. His 2002 study (*Language Learning*) found depth scores alone explained approximately 59% of variance in academic reading performance, compared to 54% for breadth. The two measures were correlated but not redundant.

This finding is important for dictionary design: a dictionary that helps learners deepen their knowledge of words they already partially know may contribute more to reading comprehension than one that only helps them learn new words.

## Henriksen's three dimensions (1999)

Henriksen (1999, "Three Dimensions of Vocabulary Development," *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 21.2, pp. 303–317) proposed:

1. **Precision of knowledge** (partial → precise) — from vague recognition to exact understanding of meaning boundaries
2. **Depth of knowledge** — richness of network connections (synonyms, antonyms, hyponyms, collocates)
3. **Receptive → productive** — the ability to move from recognition to active use

Henriksen emphasizes that lexical development involves both "item-learning" (individual words) and "system-changing" (reorganizing the mental lexicon as new items integrate). Adding a word doesn't just extend the list — it reshapes the network around it.

## Incremental acquisition

Webb (2007, "The Effects of Repetition on Vocabulary Knowledge," *Applied Linguistics* 28, pp. 46–65) tested Japanese university EFL learners across ten measures of word knowledge (orthography, syntagmatic and paradigmatic association, grammatical functions, meaning-form link). Key finding: **different aspects of knowledge develop at different rates.** A single exposure may yield form recognition, but productive collocational knowledge requires many encounters.

Pellicer-Sanchez and Schmitt (2010) found sizeable gains in form recognition from even one exposure, but very little gain in word class knowledge or meaning recall. Noticeable gains began at 5–8 exposures and accelerated at 10–17. The implication is that dictionaries serve learners at every stage of the acquisition curve — from first encounter (needing a basic gloss) to deep mastery (needing collocation patterns and register constraints).

## Collocation knowledge as the hardest dimension

Nesselhauf (2005, *Collocations in a Learner Corpus*, John Benjamins) found that approximately 50% of collocations produced by advanced L2 learners were incorrect, mostly due to L1 transfer. Length of residence in the target-language country did not improve collocational accuracy.

Laufer and Waldman (2011, "Verb-Noun Collocations in Second Language Writing," *Language Learning* 61, pp. 647–672) confirmed that even advanced learners use fewer collocations, a more restricted range of collocates, and produce more collocational errors than native speakers. About 50% of atypical collocations showed L1 influence.

These findings show that collocational knowledge is the dimension where even proficient learners most need support — and where dictionaries can add the most value beyond what exposure alone provides.

## Japanese-specific dimensions of depth

Japanese adds several dimensions to "knowing a word" that have no counterpart in alphabetic languages:

| Dimension | What it adds | Example |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| Multiple readings | Knowing which reading applies in which context | 生: なま/い/う/しょう/せい |
| Script choice | Kanji vs. kana vs. katakana carries register/emphasis connotations | 分かる (neutral) vs. わかる (soft) |
| Kanji morphological transparency | Compound inferencing from known components | 図書＋館 → 図書館 |
| Uchi/soto register | In-group vs. out-group forms of the same concept | 母 (my mother) vs. お母さん (someone else's) |
| Aspect behavior | ている reading varies by verb class (progressive, resultative, habitual, experiential) | 知っている (resultative) vs. 食べている (progressive) |
| Transitivity pairs | Related but distinct verb pairs | 開ける (transitive) / 開く (intransitive) |

Uno et al. (2021, *Scientific Reports*) identify three independent dimensions of kanji knowledge: reading accuracy, writing accuracy, and semantic comprehension, each underpinned by different cognitive processes.

## Implications for je-dict-1

The depth-of-knowledge framework provides theoretical justification for every major content dimension of je-dict-1 entries:

| Entry feature | Knowledge dimension served | Framework source |
|---------------|--------------------------|------------------|
| Multiple senses | Polysemy awareness (precision) | Henriksen dim. 1; Bogaards 2001 |
| Example sentences | Grammatical functions, collocations, constraints | Nation "use" category |
| Collocations section | Syntagmatic knowledge | Nesselhauf 2005; Laufer & Waldman 2011 |
| Similar Words / contrasts | Paradigmatic network depth | Read's WAT; Henriksen dim. 2 |
| Register/politeness labels | Constraints on use | Nation "constraints"; Qian 2002 |
| Cross-references | Mental lexicon network structure | Read & Chapelle 2001 |
| Conjugation tables | Form knowledge (productive) | Nation "form" |
| Transitivity/aspect notes | Japanese-specific depth dimensions | Webb 2007 (differential rates) |
| Progressive example length | Serving learners at different depth stages | VKS stages; incremental model |

The research consensus is that depth of knowledge — not just breadth — predicts language proficiency, and that collocational knowledge is the dimension where learners need the most dictionary support. je-dict-1's structured notes, collocations, and similar-word sections directly address the dimensions where depth research shows the greatest learner deficit.

### Practical recommendations

1. **Collocations are not optional.** Nesselhauf's 50% error rate demonstrates that learners cannot acquire collocations through exposure alone. Every entry should include common collocations, especially verb-noun and adjective-noun pairs.
2. **Similar-word sections serve precision.** Henriksen's "precision" dimension means learners need to know where a word's meaning *stops* — what its near-synonyms cover that it doesn't. The SIMILAR WORDS field directly serves this.
3. **Progressive examples serve incremental acquisition.** Short examples serve first-encounter learners (VKS level 3–4); longer examples with complex grammar serve learners building productive depth (VKS level 5).
4. **Cross-references build network knowledge.** Read's WAT shows that depth is measured by network connections. Each cross-reference in the dictionary mirrors a connection in the mental lexicon.
5. **Japanese-specific depth dimensions need explicit treatment.** Aspect behavior, transitivity pairs, and register constraints are depth dimensions that learners cannot infer from basic glosses and require explicit dictionary support.
