# Japanese Lexicography Challenges

**Last updated**: 2026-04-05

## Overview

Japanese presents several challenges that don't arise (or arise less acutely) in alphabetic-language lexicography. Understanding these challenges is essential for making consistent design decisions.

## Multiple writing systems

Japanese uses kanji, hiragana, katakana, and romaji. A dictionary must decide:
- How to represent headwords (kanji with furigana? kana only? both?)
- Whether readings should be in hiragana or katakana
- How to handle words with both kanji and kana spellings (できる vs. 出来る)

**je-dict-1 approach**: Headwords use kanji with furigana (`{漢字|かんじ}`), readings are always hiragana. This maximizes readability for intermediate learners.

## Word boundary ambiguity

Japanese is written without spaces. Defining what constitutes a "word" (vs. a phrase or morpheme) is a fundamental challenge:

- **Compound verbs**: Is 飛び出す one word or two?
- **Suru verbs**: Is 勉強する one word or two?
- **Auxiliary constructions**: Where does ている fit? てしまう?
- **Particles**: Are particles separate words or suffixes?

**je-dict-1 approach**: Suru verbs with する in the headword are single entries. Common compound verbs get their own entries. Auxiliary constructions are generally treated as grammar patterns rather than vocabulary items, though some (like てしまう) have entries.

## Homophone density

Japanese has an extremely high number of homophones, especially among Sino-Japanese (on'yomi) compounds:
- きかん → 期間, 機関, 器官, 気管, 帰還, 季刊...
- こうしょう → 交渉, 高尚, 鉱床, 口承, 公称...

Effective disambiguation requires kanji display, context labels, and example sentences.

**je-dict-1 approach**: Separate entries for different words, linked via the kanji index. Kanji display in headwords is the primary disambiguation tool. See [Handling Homographs](../topics/homographs.md).

## Verb conjugation complexity

Japanese verbs conjugate through agglutinative suffixation across multiple axes:
- Tense/aspect (る/た)
- Polarity (ない)
- Formality (ます)
- Voice (れる/られる)
- Causative (させる)
- Potential, volitional, conditional, te-form, and combinations

Learner dictionaries must decide how much conjugation information to present and in what format.

**je-dict-1 approach**: Full conjugation tables stored in JSON, generated by `add_conjugations.py`. This is comprehensive but creates large entries.

## Honorific and register layers

Japanese grammaticalizes social relationships through keigo (敬語):
- **尊敬語** (sonkeigo) — elevates the subject's actions
- **謙譲語** (kenjougo) — humbles the speaker's actions
- **丁寧語** (teineigo) — general politeness (です/ます)

Many common verbs have distinct honorific/humble equivalents (食べる → 召し上がる / いただく). See [Register and Formality](../topics/register.md).

## Pitch accent

Unlike stress-timed English, Japanese uses pitch accent, which varies by dialect. Most Japanese-Japanese dictionaries mark pitch accent; je-dict-1 currently does not. This is a potential future enhancement.

## Orthographic variation

Many words have multiple accepted spellings:
- Kanji vs. kana: できる vs. 出来る, ください vs. 下さい
- Multiple kanji: 分かる / 判る / 解る
- Historical vs. modern forms

**je-dict-1 approach**: Use the most common/standard form as the headword; note variants in the notes field.
