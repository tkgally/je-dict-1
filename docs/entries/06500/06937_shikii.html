敷居(しきい)

しきい
noun
threshold, doorsill
See also: 鴨居(かもい) (lintel, upper rail)
1. threshold, doorsill
The horizontal piece at the bottom of a doorway or sliding door frame in Japanese architecture.
敷居(しきい)を踏(ふ)まないように注意(ちゅうい)してください。
Please be careful not to step on the threshold.
古(ふる)い家(いえ)の敷居(しきい)はすり減(へ)っている。
The thresholds in old houses are worn down.
和室(わしつ)の敷居(しきい)に足(あし)をぶつけた。
I hit my foot on the Japanese-style room's threshold.
引(ひ)き戸(ど)の敷居(しきい)の溝(みぞ)にゴミが溜(た)まっている。
Dust has accumulated in the groove of the sliding door's threshold.
子供(こども)は敷居(しきい)の上(うえ)に座(すわ)ってはいけないと教(おそ)わった。
I was taught that children shouldn't sit on the threshold.
2. barrier, hurdle (figurative)
Used figuratively to describe psychological barriers or difficulty in approaching something.
このレストランは敷居(しきい)が高(たか)い。
This restaurant feels intimidating (hard to enter).
敷居(しきい)が高(たか)くて入(はい)りにくい店(みせ)だ。
It's a shop that's hard to go into (feels exclusive).
初心者(しょしんしゃ)には敷居(しきい)が高(たか)いかもしれないが、慣(な)れれば楽(たの)しい。
It might feel intimidating for beginners, but once you get used to it, it's fun.
敷居(しきい)を低(ひく)くして、誰(だれ)でも参加(さんか)できるようにした。
We lowered the barrier so that anyone could participate.
あの高級(こうきゅう)ホテルは敷居(しきい)が高(たか)くて、なかなか行(い)けない。
That luxury hotel feels so exclusive that I can never bring myself to go.

JAPANESE ARCHITECTURE: In traditional rooms with sliding doors (襖(ふすま) or 障子(しょうじ)), the 敷居(しきい) is the grooved bottom track the doors slide along. The matching upper track is the 鴨居(かもい).

CULTURAL NOTE: 敷居(しきい)を踏(ふ)む (stepping on the threshold) is traditionally bad manners - the sill was seen as standing for the household itself, so guests step over it rather than on it. Stepping on it also wears down the groove and makes the doors stick.

KEY IDIOM: 敷居(しきい)が高(たか)い

  • Literally: "the threshold is high"
  • Meaning: a place or activity feels intimidating or hard to approach
  • Used of establishments that feel exclusive or expensive, and of hobbies and fields that seem to demand expertise
  • Opposite: 敷居(しきい)を低(ひく)くする, to make something easier for newcomers to approach

NOTE ON USAGE: Traditionally the idiom meant feeling too awkward to revisit somewhere after some failing or breach on one's own part. The modern sense - "too upmarket or too advanced for me" - is now the dominant one and is what learners will hear, though prescriptive style guides still object to it.
