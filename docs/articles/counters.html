Japanese Counters and Classifiers

助(じょ)数(すう)詞(し)

What Are Counters?

In Japanese, you cannot simply put a number next to a noun the way English does with "three books" or "five cats." Instead, Japanese uses special words called counters (or classifiers) that go between the number and the noun — or sometimes after the noun with the particle の. Each counter is chosen based on a property of the thing being counted: its shape, size, or category.

For example, 本(ほん) is used for long, thin objects, 枚(まい) for flat things, and 匹(ひき) for small animals. Choosing the right counter is an important part of sounding natural in Japanese.

The Universal Counter: つ

The counter つ (using native Japanese numbers: ひとつ, ふたつ, みっつ...) works for almost anything when you do not know or cannot remember the specific counter. It covers general objects, abstract concepts, and many everyday items. However, it only works with numbers 1 through 9 (and とお for 10). For larger numbers or when precision matters, you need the appropriate specific counter.

Common Counters by Category

People

人(にん) counts people. It has irregular readings for 1 and 2:

  • 1 person: ひとり
  • 2 people: ふたり
  • 3 people: さんにん
  • 4 people: よにん

Flat Objects

枚(まい) counts flat, thin objects: paper, plates, shirts, photos, slices of bread, tickets, and similar items.

  • 1: いちまい
  • 2: にまい
  • 3: さんまい

Long Objects

本(ほん) counts long, cylindrical objects: pens, bottles, trees, roads, rivers, and even phone calls and movies. This is one of the most important counters to learn because it applies to a surprisingly wide range of items.

  • 1: いっぽん
  • 2: にほん
  • 3: さんぼん

Small Objects

個(こ) counts small, compact objects that do not fit a more specific counter: apples, eggs, stones, boxes, and similar items.

  • 1: いっこ
  • 2: にこ
  • 3: さんこ

Machines and Vehicles

台(だい) counts machines, vehicles, and large equipment: cars, computers, bicycles, televisions.

  • 1: いちだい
  • 2: にだい
  • 3: さんだい

Books and Volumes

冊(さつ) counts bound volumes: books, notebooks, magazines.

  • 1: いっさつ
  • 2: にさつ
  • 3: さんさつ

Cups and Glasses

杯(はい) counts cups, glasses, and bowls of liquid or food.

  • 1: いっぱい
  • 2: にはい
  • 3: さんばい

Animals

Small and medium animals use 匹(ひき): dogs, cats, fish, insects.

  • 1: いっぴき
  • 2: にひき
  • 3: さんびき

Large animals use 頭(とう): horses, cows, elephants.

  • 1: いっとう
  • 2: にとう
  • 3: さんとう

Birds (and rabbits) use 羽(わ):

  • 1: いちわ
  • 2: にわ
  • 3: さんわ

Buildings

軒(けん) counts houses and buildings.

  • 1: いっけん
  • 2: にけん
  • 3: さんげん

Frequency

回(かい) counts the number of times something happens.

  • 1: いっかい
  • 2: にかい
  • 3: さんかい

Sound Change Rules

Many counters undergo sound changes (音(おん)便(びん)) when combined with certain numbers. The most important pattern to learn:

Numbers 1, 6, 8, 10 often trigger sound changes:

  • Counters starting with h: h → pp (1, 6, 8, 10), h → b (3)
  • Examples: いっぽん, ろっぽん, はっぽん, じゅっぽん; さんぼん
  • Counters starting with k: k → kk (1, 6, 8, 10)
  • Examples: いっかい, ろっかい, はっかい, じゅっかい

These are not random — they follow consistent phonological rules. Once you learn the pattern for one counter, the same rules apply to others starting with the same consonant.

Tips for Learners

  1. Start with つ. It works in most casual situations and buys you time to learn specific counters.
  2. Learn the "big five" first: 人(にん), 枚(まい), 本(ほん), 個(こ), 台(だい). These cover most daily situations.
  3. Pay attention to sound changes. The irregular readings for 1, 3, 6, 8, and 10 follow patterns — they are not random.
  4. Listen for counters in context. Native speakers sometimes simplify counter use in casual speech, but correct usage is expected in formal contexts.
