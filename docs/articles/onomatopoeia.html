Common Onomatopoeia and Mimetic Words

擬(ぎ)音(おん)語(ご)と擬(ぎ)態(たい)語(ご)

What Are Onomatopoeia in Japanese?

Japanese has an extraordinarily rich system of sound-symbolic words — far larger and more integrated into everyday speech than English onomatopoeia like "buzz" or "splash." These words are divided into two broad categories:

  • 擬(ぎ)音(おん)語(ご) (giongo): words that imitate actual sounds — rain falling, doors slamming, animals calling
  • 擬(ぎ)態(たい)語(ご) (gitaigo): words that describe states, sensations, or manners that have no actual sound — softness, nervousness, sparkling

Together these are sometimes called オノマトペ (onomatope, from the French). They appear constantly in conversation, manga, literature, and advertising. Learning them makes your Japanese more vivid and natural.

Major Groups

Rain and Water Sounds

  • ぽたぽた — dripping (slow, individual drops)
  • じゃぶじゃぶ — splashing; wading through water
  • ぱらぱら — light, scattered rain; flipping pages

Emotional States

  • ハラハラする — to feel anxious while watching something unfold
  • くらくら — dizzy, woozy (from heat, shock, or illness)
  • むずむず — itching to do something; feeling restless
  • うずうず — unable to sit still; eager and impatient
  • じわじわ — gradual emotional impact; something that moves you slowly
  • ほのぼの — heartwarming; a gentle, peaceful feeling

Textures and Physical Sensations

  • もふもふ — fluffy and soft (fur, plush toys, animals)
  • ふかふか — soft and cushiony (pillows, bread, cushions)
  • ふんわり — softly, gently, with lightness (cotton candy, clouds)
  • ガチガチ — stiff, rigid, frozen (from cold or nervousness)
  • ゴツゴツ — rough, rugged (rocky surfaces, angular features)
  • ギザギザ — jagged, serrated (saw teeth, torn paper)

Movement and Manner

  • くねくね — winding, wiggling, curving (roads, snakes, dance moves)
  • てきぱき — briskly, efficiently, with no wasted motion
  • きりっと — with sharpness and precision; crisply
  • しゃんと — upright and alert; pulled together
  • ぴしっと — neatly and precisely; with snap and crispness
  • すらっと — slender and graceful; smoothly

Eating and Drinking

  • ぱくぱく — munching; opening and closing the mouth while eating
  • ぐつぐつ — simmering, bubbling (pots of food)
  • しゃぶしゃぶ — the swishing sound of meat in hot broth (also a dish name)
  • ざくざく — crunching (cutting vegetables; walking on gravel)

Sounds and Noises

  • ぶんぶん — buzzing (insects, spinning objects)
  • ガタゴト — rumbling, rattling (trains, carts on rough ground)
  • ごうごう — roaring, rushing (wind, rivers, fire)
  • カチカチ — clicking, ticking (clocks, frozen solid)
  • かたかた — light rattling or clattering
  • とんとん — knocking, tapping lightly
  • けらけら — cackling, laughing loudly and openly

Usage Patterns

Onomatopoeia in Japanese are not just thrown into sentences randomly — they follow specific grammatical patterns:

With と (quotation particle)

Many onomatopoeia attach to と to function as adverbs:

  • ぽたぽたと落(お)ちる — to drip down
  • じわじわと広(ひろ)がる — to spread gradually

With する (to do)

Some onomatopoeia combine with する to form verbs:

  • ハラハラする — to feel anxious
  • ガタガタする — to rattle; to be shaky

As direct adverbs (no particle)

Many can modify verbs directly without と:

  • てきぱき働(はたら)く — to work briskly
  • ふんわり包(つつ)む — to wrap softly

As な-adjectives

Some can modify nouns with な or の:

  • ふかふかなベッド — a fluffy bed
  • ギザギザのはさみ — serrated scissors

Patterns in Form

Notice that many onomatopoeia share structural patterns:

  • ABAB repetition (もふもふ, ぱくぱく, じわじわ): suggests continuous or repeated action
  • っと ending (ぴしっと, きりっと, すらっと): suggests a single crisp action or state
  • り ending (ふんわり, ぐっすり, しっくり): suggests a soft or settled quality
  • Voiced consonants (ガタゴト vs. カタカタ): voiced sounds (g, b, d, z) often suggest heavier, rougher, or more forceful versions of their voiceless counterparts

Tips for Learners

  1. Learn them in context. Onomatopoeia are best learned through example sentences and real usage, not as isolated vocabulary.
  2. Start with common ones. Focus on the ones you hear most often: ぴったり (exactly right), すっきり (refreshed), ぎりぎり (barely making it).
  3. Read manga. Manga is full of onomatopoeia in both speech bubbles and sound effects — it is one of the best ways to build familiarity.
  4. Notice the voiced/voiceless pairs. Once you see that ガタガタ is the heavy version of カタカタ, the whole system starts to make more sense.
